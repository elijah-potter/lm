A quiet weekend in the mountains after the city’s chaos.
First, the air—thick with pine and the faint scent of rain.
Then, the silence. No honking, no sirens. Just the crackle of firewood in the hearth and the hum of cicadas.
She sat on the porch, coffee steaming in her hands, watching the stars stretch like a black canvas over jagged peaks. Her phone buzzed: *"Just realized I’ve been late to every meeting this week. Too much caffeine."*

A single sentence. Unloaded.
She’d been running—on schedules, on guilt, on the illusion that time was something to be *managed* rather than experienced. But here, time was a river, slow and endless. The child who built a dam from sticks and stones to "catch the water" for the village’s next day played beside her. No lessons, no rules—just a child learning that water flows downhill.

Back at the cabin, she lay in the dark, the cold mountain air seeping into her bones. The night before, she’d cried. Not for the city, but for the way she’d spent years chasing ghosts—things that weren’t hers to own. This trip wasn’t about escape; it was about realizing she didn’t need to leave to start again.

When she checked in on Monday, her boss’s email was still unread: *"You’re off to a great start this week."* She smiled, but the weight in her chest was different. Not relief, but something deeper: the quiet understanding that she’d been carrying a bag of things she didn’t need to carry anymore. The child’s dam had taught her that even the smallest things could hold the weight of truth.
