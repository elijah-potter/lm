After a quiet evening spent reviewing data, Dr. Chen hesitated over the last row of the spreadsheet. The numbers seemed inconsistent with previous trends, but the gut feeling told her to double-check.

She recalled the initial hypothesis: *"This pattern suggests a seasonal shift."* Yet, as she cross-referenced with external sources, discrepancies emerged—supply chain delays, unexpected demand surges. The data didn’t align.

A cold sweat prickled her fingers as she printed the report. The morning meeting was scheduled at 9 AM. Her colleague, Mark, would be there. She hadn’t presented this version yet.

Her phone buzzed. A text from her boss: *"Quick update on the new client project—can you review the timeline?"* The urgency stung. She’d spent weeks on this analysis, but the numbers didn’t justify her original conclusion.

That’s when the realization hit her. The data wasn’t wrong—it was *contextually wrong*. The seasonal shift wasn’t about the numbers but the assumptions behind them. She’d overfitted the model to a flawed premise.

She erased the old draft, opened a new one. The revised hypothesis: *"The anomaly may stem from a one-time disruption."* She adjusted projections, accounting for the external factors.

As she sent the updated report, her stomach dropped. The team would see the change. But this time, it wasn’t about fear—it was about honesty. The data led her there, and she’d follow it, no matter the cost.

By the end of the meeting, her colleagues nodded, surprised but understanding. The change wasn’t about weakness; it was about clarity. And for the first time, she felt like she’d found the truth.
