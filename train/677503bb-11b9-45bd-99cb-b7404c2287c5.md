A weekend in a coastal village revealed the quiet depth of nature’s resilience. The first morning, I woke to the sound of waves crashing against the cliffs, their rhythm steady and unchanging. The air smelled of salt and wild thyme. A hike along the shore revealed shells stacked like forgotten ruins and a river that carried the same mystery as the ocean itself.

On the second day, I sat by the harbor, watching fishermen unload nets. One old man, his hands rough with age, showed me how to pick clams from the sand with a simple fork. When I asked why he did it, he said, *"The earth gives, and it takes back what it gives."* I left with a handful of clams in my pocket, a memory of how small acts carry weight.

The return flight was quiet. I stared out the window at the clouds, thinking about how the village had taught me to see stillness in motion—how the ocean doesn’t rush, how seasons repeat, and how some truths aren’t meant to be rushed. I kept the clams in my pocket, not to eat, but to remember that the smallest changes are the most meaningful.
