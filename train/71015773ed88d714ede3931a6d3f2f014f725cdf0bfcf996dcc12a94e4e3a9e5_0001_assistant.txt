The tale of the Plate of Peas still sends shivers down my spine, a haunting memory that lingers in the depths of my being. It began innocently enough, with the passing of my grandfather and my grandmother's arrival to stay with us for half of every year. Her presence brought an overpowering scent that permeated the air—a scent so potent it could knock out a charging moose. The aroma, emanating from her enigmatic perfume, was stored in a colossal atomizer and liberally sprayed at every opportunity. Venturing into her room meant subjecting oneself to an assault on the senses, gasping for breath in the face of the overwhelming fragrance. Whenever she left to stay with Aunt Lillian, a frantic ritual ensued, involving open windows, stripped bedding, and days dedicated to washing and airing out the rooms in a desperate attempt to banish the lingering odor.

But it was during an ill-fated lunch at the Biltmore Hotel, a place I deemed the epitome of luxury in my eight-year-old mind, that the nightmare unfolded. Accompanied by my grandmother and mother, I indulged in what I thought was a sophisticated choice—a salisbury steak, a fancy name disguising a humble hamburger smothered in gravy. Alas, the innocent-looking dish arrived with an unwelcome companion—a plate of detestable peas.

Peas, those repulsive green orbs, were my nemesis. I detested them with every fiber of my being. Their slimy texture and bland taste baffled me, leaving me bewildered as to why anyone willingly subjected themselves to such culinary torment. I refused to consume peas at home or in restaurants, and I certainly had no intention of succumbing to them now.

"Eat your peas," my grandmother commanded, her voice carrying an ominous undertone.

"Mother," my mother interjected, her voice a warning. "He doesn't like peas. Leave him be."

My grandmother remained silent, her eyes glinting with a determined resolve, her jaw set in unwavering determination. Inclining herself toward me, she fixed her gaze upon my own and uttered words that would forever alter the course of my life.

"I'll pay you five dollars if you eat those peas."

At the time, the impending catastrophe eluded me entirely. All I comprehended was the sheer magnitude of the offer—five dollars, a sum unimaginable to my young mind. The allure of possessing that vast fortune outweighed the wretchedness of the peas. With a mixture of apprehension and greed, I began to force the loathsome legumes down my throat, one after another.

My mother, livid with anger, directed her glare at both her mother and me. No one could glare like my mother—an Olympic champion in the art of withering stares. The glares unsettled me, each pea causing me to retch internally, but the promise of that coveted five dollars sustained me. And so, with each agonizing bite, I persevered until the last pea disappeared from my plate. My grandmother, triumphant, bestowed upon me the coveted prize, a five-dollar bill presented with a flourish. Meanwhile, my mother's silent glare continued, a testament to her disapproval.

Weeks later, my grandmother departed for Aunt Lillian's. That evening, at the dinner table, a sinister plot unfolded. My mother served two of my all-time favorite dishes—meatloaf and mashed potatoes. But accompanying them, like a steaming bowl of malevolence, were peas—endless, vile peas. She offered me a serving, and in the waning moments of my youthful innocence, I declined. A cold, piercing gaze from my mother accompanied her actions as she piled a mountain of peas onto my plate. And then, the words were spoken, words that would haunt my existence for years to come.

"You ate them for money," she hissed. "You can eat them for love."

Despair consumed me. Devastation washed over my soul. In that moment, the cruel reality sank in—I had unwittingly damned myself to an eternal torment from which there was no escape.

"You ate them for money. You can eat them for love."

What defense could I possibly muster against such a damning proclamation? There was none. I ate the peas that day, and every time they were served thereafter. The five dollars evaporated as quickly as it came, and my grandmother, herself, passed away in the following years. Yet, the legacy of the peas endured, a relentless reminder of my capitulation. Even now, if I dare to display the slightest distaste upon their appearance, my mother resurrects the dreadful words, her voice dripping with a sinister tone.

"You ate them for money," she hisses once more. "You can eat them for love."

And so, the peas continue to haunt me, an eternal punishment for my youthful folly.
