The first time I saw the glow in my hands, I thought it was a mistake. My fingers tingled, the tips of my toes buzzed, and suddenly, I could see colors no human eye had ever captured before—deep blues like the night sky, greens that shimmered like fresh-cut grass, and pinks that pulsed like a heartbeat. My breath caught. I was not supposed to be seeing this.

I was a normal kid—well, as normal as I could be with a family who believed in the old ways. My parents, Mr. and Mrs. Carter, ran the local hardware store, and my brother, Jake, was already six years older than me, a year ahead of me in school. He was the one who had always been "special," the one who could solve equations in his head, who never forgot a face. I was the quiet one, the one who doodled in the margins of my notebooks and wondered if the world was really real.

But now, I was not just me anymore.

That night, I stayed up until the clock struck midnight, my fingers tracing the strange light in my palms. I tried to explain it to Jake, but he just laughed and said, "It’s just your imagination, brother." I wanted to believe him. But then I saw it again—the next morning, in the kitchen mirror. My reflection was the same, but the world around me seemed different. Shadows stretched longer, colors shifted under my eyes. I told my parents, but they dismissed it as stress from my new job at the hardware store.

"Hardly anything happens here," my father had said, rubbing my shoulder. "You’re overreacting."

That was when I realized I wasn’t the only one who had noticed the change. Across the street, old Mrs. Thompson had stopped her gardening mid-sip of tea, her hands hovering over the pot. She blinked once, twice, and then stared at the sidewalk. "The ground’s… *wrong*," she whispered before she disappeared into her house.

I followed her home, but her yard looked the same—until I stepped inside. The floorboards beneath me were warm, pulsing faintly, and the walls seemed to breathe. Mrs. Thompson’s cat, Mr. Whiskers, stood frozen, staring at the ceiling. His eyes glowed a sickly green.

I ran.

I didn’t know where to go. The city’s streets were too familiar, too safe. I remembered something Jake had once said—about a place outside town, a forest where the trees were older than the hills. It was dangerous, he’d warned, but if anyone knew what was happening, it was him.

Jake found me at the edge of the woods, his face pale. "You shouldn’t have followed her," he said. "The forest doesn’t like outsiders."

I didn’t listen. The trees were alive, their bark shifting like living flesh. The air smelled of damp earth and something older, something like old books and forgotten dreams. Then I saw it—the first of many—small, glowing marks on the ground, like stars pressed into the dirt. They pulsed when I walked over them.

"Jake," I whispered, "what are you?"

He didn’t answer. Instead, he reached into his backpack and pulled out a small, rusted key. "This isn’t just a forest," he said. "It’s a prison. And we’re the keys."

That night, we found more of them. The marks led deeper into the woods, where the trees grew taller, their branches twisting into shapes that made my stomach twist. Some of the marks glowed brighter, like they were calling. Others pulsed slowly, like a heartbeat.

I didn’t know what was happening. But I knew one thing for sure: we weren’t the only ones who had seen the change. And if we didn’t figure this out, the world might not be safe anymore.

The next morning, the city woke up to something different. The sun rose, but the sky wasn’t blue—it was gray, like the color of old paper. The streets were empty, save for a few cars that didn’t start. The hardware store was quiet, the shelves empty. My parents were gone.

Jake and I stood outside, the air thick with the scent of ozone. "We have to find out what’s happening," Jake said. "Before it’s too late."

I looked at the woods, at the glowing marks, at the way the world seemed to shift around us. I didn’t know what was real anymore.

But I knew one thing for sure: we had to figure this out. Because if the world was changing, then maybe—just maybe—we could stop it.

And that was when we decided to go back. Not to the forest, but to the source. To the place where the marks were strongest. To the place where the light was brightest.

The journey was harder than we thought. The woods were alive, and they tried to keep us away. But we pressed on, because we had to know.

And when we finally reached the center of it all, we found what we were looking for—a great, circular mark, like a wound in the earth. At its heart, something glowed. Something that wasn’t quite light, but something else entirely.

It pulsed.

And then, slowly, it spoke.

The voice was not human. It was like the sound of water dripping, but deeper, older. It whispered in a language that made my teeth ache, that made my skin crawl.

"We have been waiting for them," it said. "For the ones who could see. For the ones who would remember."

Jake and I exchanged a look. We had been chosen.

The mark stretched, pulling us deeper into the earth. The world above us faded, replaced by darkness, by something that felt like a dream. And then, we were there.

Beneath the world.

The place was vast, like the inside of a great, sleeping beast. Walls of stone pulsed with light, like veins carrying something dark and beautiful. And in the center, standing on a pedestal of black metal, was something that made my breath catch.

It was a person. Not a man, not a woman, but something in between. Their skin was smooth, like polished marble, their eyes black and endless. They held a staff in their hand, and when they spoke, the air around them shimmered.

"We have been waiting for the ones who could see," they said. "For the ones who would remember. The world above is not as it should be. It is broken, and it needs to be mended."

Jake and I had never met anyone like them. They didn’t have faces, but eyes—endless, watching eyes. Their voice was not a sound, but a vibration, a feeling in the bones.

"We are the keepers," they said. "We have kept the balance for centuries. But the world above has forgotten. It has grown careless, and now it must be reminded."

I looked at Jake. He was staring at me, his eyes wide. "What does that mean?"

"It means we’re not just kids anymore," Jake said. "We’re not just human."

The being reached out, and the air between us shimmered. Jake and I felt the pull, the weight of something ancient and powerful. We were connected. We were *changed*.

The being’s voice was like a whisper in the wind. "The world above has forgotten the old ways. It has forgotten the balance. But you remember. You remember what it is to be truly alive."

Jake and I looked at each other. The world above was changing. The marks were spreading. The lights were getting brighter.

And we were the only ones who could stop it.

The being extended their staff. It was like a bridge, a way to the world above. "You must choose," they said. "Do you want to be part of this? Do you want to be the ones who remember?"

Jake and I exchanged another look. We had been chosen. We had been chosen for a reason.

And then, we stepped forward.

The world around us shifted. The stone walls blurred, the light flickered. And then—

We were back.

The world was different. The marks were everywhere. The air hummed, like a song that had been forgotten. The city was alive, but it wasn’t human. It was *something else*. The trees bent, the rivers flowed faster, the sky was always twilight.

And then, we saw it—the first of the new people. They were like us, but different. Their eyes glowed faintly, like embers. They moved differently, like they were always one step ahead.

Jake and I had been chosen. We had been chosen to be the ones who could see. To be the ones who could remember.

And we had to decide what to do.
