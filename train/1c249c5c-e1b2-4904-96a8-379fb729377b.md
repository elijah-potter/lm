A crumbling stone archway stands at the edge of a village square, its once-vibrant red tiles now faded and splintered. The bricks beneath are loose, revealing damp patches where water seeps through cracks in the mortar. The archway’s iron hinges rust, and the metal is warped, unable to hold the weight of the overhanging roof tiles.

Beyond the archway, a row of uneven wooden doors marks the last remaining houses, their shutters painted peeling yellow and black, the paint flaking in places. Some doors are slightly ajar, revealing dimly lit rooms with cobwebbed corners and half-burned candles still clinging to their holders. The walls of one house have a fresh layer of plaster, but it’s cracked in a jagged diagonal, as if someone tried to repair it but stopped too soon.

The square itself is a patchwork of uneven cobblestones, some broken, others worn smooth by centuries of use. A small, overgrown garden fences the perimeter, its plants tangled with wild vines—roses that have outgrown their trellises, and weeds that choke out any remnants of cultivated soil. A single, rusted iron gate stands half-buried in the dirt, its lock missing, its hinges groaning when pushed.

In the distance, a church tower looms, its stained-glass windows still intact but stained with time, casting shifting patterns of color through the cracks. The spires are bare, and the bell tower’s wooden scaffolding, once used for repairs, now lies abandoned. The bell itself is silent, its chains rusted and still.

A faint smell of damp earth and old wood lingers, mixed with the scent of something else—something metallic and sour, like iron rusting in the damp. It’s the kind of place where the past lingers, where every shadow feels like a memory waiting to be remembered. The air hums with the sound of distant traffic, but it’s muffled, as if the world outside this square has already moved on, leaving only echoes behind.
