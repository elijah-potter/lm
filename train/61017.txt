With their multi-petaled flowers and sweet scents, roses (Rosa) deserve their reputation as a classic garden plant. But roses also have a reputation as temperamental plants prone to disease. However, if you give them what they need, roses of different varieties thrive in all U.S. Department of Agriculture plant hardiness zones, albeit as annuals in USDA zone 1. If disease does strike, treat it quickly, remove diseased leaves and sterilize your tools with rubbing alcohol.
Keeping Roses Healthy
Roses do best and resist disease when they get at least six hours of full sun each day, though filtered sun is best in extremely hot temperatures. They also need good air circulation between plants, regular water and feeding, organic and well-draining soil, and organic mulch to retain moisture. Your individual micro-climate influences the health of your roses as well. Choose a variety suited for your USDA zone and choose a spot in your garden with the best conditions.
Black spot fungal disease appears with small black spots on the leaves of roses, eventually causing them to turn yellow and drop off the plant. One of the most common rose problems, black spot spreads when high humidity, rainfall or overhead watering gives fungal spores the right conditions to germinate and grow. Methods to control black spot include watering early in the day so the plant can dry out or spraying with horticultural oil, a natural fungicide or a baking soda mixture.
Downy Mildew, Powdery Mildew and Rust
Powdery mildew, with its white powdery coating, and rust, with tiny, dark powdery spots on the underside of leaves, are types of fungi that may affect your roses at some point. Downy mildew, with angular reddish-black spots, occurs periodically, with flareups in certain regions now and then that cause rapid and severe damage. All three fungal diseases occur less frequently if you grow resistant varieties of roses and use good growing practices to keep your roses as healthy as possible.
Rose Rosette Virus
A microscopic mite carries the rose rosette virus that causes rose stems to sprout thick clusters of red leaflets and that ultimately kills the entire plant. The disease primarily affects multiflora and shrub roses rather than roses such as hybrid teas that are pruned heavily. If you see symptoms of rose rosette virus, destroy the plant because no effective treatment exists. Fortunately, rose rosette occurs infrequently.
