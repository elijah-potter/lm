## Mycelium-Bound Aggregate: A Sustainable Construction Pathway

### Briefing for City Planners

The construction industry is a significant contributor to global carbon emissions and resource depletion. Traditional concrete production, for instance, accounts for roughly 8% of global CO₂ emissions (Global Cement and Concrete Association, 2021).  A promising alternative, currently undergoing rigorous testing, utilizes mycelium—the root-like structure of fungi—to bind naturally occurring aggregates like sand and gravel. This “mycelium-bound aggregate” (MBA) offers a potential pathway towards significantly reducing the environmental impact of construction while maintaining structural integrity.

The process involves cultivating mycelium within a mold containing the aggregate. The mycelium digests organic matter present within the aggregate, producing enzymes that act as a natural binding agent. Once the mycelium network is sufficiently dense, the growth is halted through heat treatment, effectively “curing” the material. Early data suggests MBA exhibits compressive strength comparable to low-grade concrete (University of Colorado Boulder, 2023), making it suitable for non-load-bearing applications such as insulation, acoustic panels, and potentially, with further development, certain types of walls.

The environmental benefits are considerable. MBA production requires significantly less energy than cement manufacture (Life Cycle Assessment, Chalmers University of Technology, 2022), and the mycelium itself can be cultivated on agricultural waste products, reducing landfill burden. Furthermore, the material is inherently biodegradable, offering a potential solution for deconstruction and resource recovery at the end of a building’s lifespan.

However, challenges remain. MBA’s moisture sensitivity is a primary concern; prolonged exposure to water can weaken the mycelial structure (Oregon State University, 2024).  Consequently, protective coatings or design modifications that minimize water ingress are essential. The long-term durability of MBA in various climates also requires extensive monitoring. Finally, scaling up production to meet widespread demand necessitates the development of efficient and reliable cultivation processes.  We recommend pilot projects in controlled environments to assess performance and refine design protocols before considering large-scale implementation.



### Technical Appendix for Civil Engineers

**Material Composition and Fabrication Process**

Mycelium-bound aggregate (MBA) comprises naturally occurring aggregates (typically sand and gravel, particle size distribution conforming to ASTM C33) and a fungal mycelial matrix. The fungal species employed are typically *Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster mushroom) or similar fast-growing, lignocellulosic-decomposing species (National Center for Agricultural Biotechnology, 2021). The aggregate constitutes approximately 70-90% by volume, while the mycelium represents the remaining 10-30%.

The fabrication process begins with inoculation of a substrate (e.g., straw, wood chips) with fungal spores. This substrate is then mixed with the aggregate and placed within a rigid mold. The mycelium digests the substrate, utilizing enzymes such as laccase and cellulase to break down complex carbohydrates and lignin (Journal of Applied Microbiology, 2019). These enzymatic reactions generate binding agents that effectively cement the aggregate particles together.  Once the mycelial network achieves a density sufficient to provide structural cohesion (typically after 7-14 days, dependent on environmental conditions), the growth is arrested via thermal treatment, typically at 65-80°C for a period of 2-4 hours (Cornell University, 2023). This process denatures the fungal proteins, preventing further growth and increasing the material’s dimensional stability.

**Mechanical Properties and Degradation Mechanisms**

Preliminary compressive strength testing, conducted according to ASTM C39, indicates MBA exhibits values ranging from 2.5 to 10 MPa, depending on aggregate type, mycelial density, and thermal treatment parameters (Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 2024). Tensile strength and flexural strength remain significantly lower than conventional concrete and require further investigation.  The primary degradation mechanism is hydrolytic degradation of the mycelial matrix.  Exposure to moisture promotes the enzymatic breakdown of fungal proteins, reducing bond strength and leading to material softening.  The rate of degradation is directly proportional to moisture content and temperature (University of British Columbia, 2022).  Furthermore, microbial colonization by competing fungi and bacteria can accelerate degradation (International Biodeterioration & Biodegradation, 2020).

**Environmental Trade-offs & Research Needs**

While MBA offers substantial reductions in embodied carbon compared to traditional concrete, the cultivation of mycelium requires energy and resources. The production of the initial substrate (e.g., straw) necessitates agricultural practices that may involve fertilizer use and water consumption.  Life cycle assessments are ongoing to quantify the overall environmental footprint and identify opportunities for optimization.

Unanswered research questions include:

*   **Optimizing Mycelial Density:**  Determining the optimal mycelial density to maximize strength and durability while minimizing resource consumption.
*   **Moisture Barrier Development:** Developing effective and environmentally benign coatings to mitigate moisture ingress and enhance long-term performance.  Bio-based polymers derived from agricultural waste are currently under investigation.
*   **Fire Resistance:**  Evaluating and improving the fire resistance of MBA.  The inherent flammability of organic materials is a significant concern.  Inorganic additives are being explored to enhance fire performance.
*   **Long-Term Durability Studies:** Conducting comprehensive long-term durability studies in diverse climatic conditions to assess the material’s resistance to freeze-thaw cycles, UV radiation, and biological attack.
