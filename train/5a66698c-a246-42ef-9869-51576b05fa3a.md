The late afternoon sunlight slanted through the window of room 312, casting long shadows across the room. I adjusted the blinds slightly, ensuring Mr. Alistair Finch wouldn’t be bothered by the glare. He was asleep, a thin blanket tucked around his shoulders. His breathing was shallow but steady, a significant improvement from his arrival three days prior.

Mr. Finch, a retired clockmaker, had been admitted with a severe exacerbation of his chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. He’s a man of precise routines and quiet dignity, qualities that shone through even in his distress. Initially, he’s been quite agitated, resistant to the oxygen therapy and frustrated by his inability to communicate clearly. He’s a man accustomed to meticulous work, to the delicate manipulation of tiny gears, and the feeling of helplessness must have been profoundly unsettling.

I checked his vital signs: blood pressure 138/82, heart rate 78, respiratory rate 18, oxygen saturation 94% on two liters of oxygen via nasal cannula. All within acceptable parameters. I’m pleased with the progress he’s made. The respiratory therapist, David, had been instrumental in adjusting his medication and providing education on breathing techniques. He’s a patient advocate, and his efforts have clearly resonated with Mr. Finch.

I paused at his bedside, noticing the small, antique pocket watch resting on his nightstand. It was intricately engraved with floral patterns, a testament to his craft. I remembered him mentioning, during a brief moment of lucidity yesterday, that it had belonged to his grandfather, a railway engineer. He’s been careful to keep it within arm’s reach, a tangible link to his past.

I made a note to remind the housekeeper to ensure the pocket watch remained undisturbed during room cleaning. Small details like these, the preservation of personal belongings, are often overlooked, but they can significantly impact a patient’s sense of comfort and security.

Later, I would administer his evening medications – a bronchodilator and a corticosteroid – and reassess his respiratory status. For now, I would simply allow him to rest, hoping for a peaceful night and anticipating further improvement tomorrow. The quiet rhythm of his breathing filled the room, a subtle reassurance that, despite the challenges, he was on the path to recovery. It’s moments like these, witnessing the resilience of the human spirit, that reaffirm the purpose and privilege of being a nurse.
