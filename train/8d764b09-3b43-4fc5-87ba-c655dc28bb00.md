In the dim glow of a flickering oil lamp, the old woman sat cross-legged on the worn rug, fingers tracing the rim of a cracked porcelain cup. The scent of tea, sweetened with honey from the garden, lingered in the air, a remnant of the morning’s quiet. Her eyes, lined with age and memory, shifted to the framed photograph on the mantel—one she had saved since the day she made the promise.

The photograph showed three children: her son, Elias, kneeling beside his younger brother, Daniel, who clutched a small wooden train set. Their father, a man with a weathered face and a handshake that had earned him the nickname "Strongarm," stood behind them, his own hands rough but warm. Behind him, a crumbling stone wall stretched into the distance, where the garden once flourished before the war took it all.

"You promise," Elias had whispered, his voice small but sure, as he pressed the train set into her hands. "One day, we’ll fix this place. No matter what happens." The war had come like a thief in the night, stealing sons, husbands, and dreams. But in that moment, in the quiet before the first bombs, she had promised herself she would keep that promise.

Now, the tea was cold. The photograph was her anchor. And though the world had changed, the promise—simple, stubborn, unbroken—had found its way into the cracks of her memory.
