**Key Debates:**

1. **The "Right to Roof" vs. "Right to Roofspace"**
   - Opponents argue that the act is an unjustified land grab, displacing small businesses and forcing property owners to sell land at artificially depressed prices for "green buffers." They compare it to the Soviet-era collectivization of private property, citing historical cases where forced redistribution leads to economic stagnation.
   - Supporters counter that the act is a "market correction," forcing developers to internalize the cost of urban sprawl. They point to studies showing that cities with mandatory open space see 20% lower crime rates and 15% higher property values in adjacent zones.

2. **The "Human Scale" vs. "Ecosystem Scale" Dilemma**
   - Traditionalists (e.g., architect Michael Graves’ followers) argue that the act is a betrayal of "formal urbanism"—the idea that cities should be designed for human interaction, not algorithmic sustainability. They compare it to the dystopian "Tragedy of the Commons" scenarios where individual actions (here, developer profits) destroy collective value.
   - Environmentalists push back by citing the "Great Filter" theory: if humanity is to survive long-term, we must abandon the "short-termism" of capitalism and enforce hard limits on urban expansion. The act, they say, is the first step toward a post-growth economy.

3. **The "Neoliberal Urbanism" Backlash**
   - Critics from the "anti-planner" movement (e.g., figures like David Harvey’s critics) argue that the act is a tool for "urban planning authoritarianism"—a way to centralize control over land use, eliminating local autonomy. They compare it to the 1970s "New Urbanism" movement, which they claim was a cover for gentrification and exclusionary zoning.
   - The Council responds by invoking "social justice," noting that 40% of low-income families live in neighborhoods where green spaces are non-existent. They argue that the act is a necessary step toward "equitable urbanism," not just environmentalism.

4. **The "Post-Capitalist" Gambit**
   - A fringe group (e.g., anarchist urbanists) argues that the act is a precursor to "communal land trusts," where cities would take ownership of all undeveloped land and redistribute it based on need. They cite the 2019 "Green New Deal" as a precursor, arguing that the Council is already moving toward a "post-capitalist urbanism."
   - The Council dismisses this as "utopian nonsense," arguing that such measures would require a complete overhaul of the tax system, which is politically impossible.

5. **The "Cultural Clash" of Values**
   - The dispute also reflects broader philosophical divides: **teleology** (ends justify means) vs. **deontology** (rules must be followed regardless of outcomes). Supporters argue that the act is a necessary evil for long-term survival; opponents argue that it violates the "rights of property" and the "principle of least restrictive means."
