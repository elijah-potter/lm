**ANNOUNCING THE RETURN OF THE OLD WAYS**
---
In the hollow of an ancient oak, where the wind hummed through gnarled roots like forgotten lullabies, a whisper slithered into the damp earth. It was not the voice of men, but something older—something that had learned to speak in tongues long since drowned in the tide of progress.

*"The past is not dead. It’s just sleeping,"* the thing murmured, its presence thick as the scent of damp moss and decay. And then, the world remembered.

The first to wake was the clockmaker in the village square, his hands still stained with the last drops of molten silver. His daughter, Clara, knelt beside him as the gears of his watch began to spin backward, one by one. The brass turned cold, the clock hands traced paths they had never followed before, and when they stopped, they did not point to noon—only to a time before the first fire burned.

Clara reached out, and the air between her fingers *rippled*. The village elders had told stories of the "Old Man in the Clock," but no one had believed them. Now, Clara knew the truth: the clock was not just a mechanism. It was a door.

Outside, the fields bloomed overnight—not with the stubborn stubbornness of weeds, but with the slow, patient bloom of things forgotten. The river, once choked with plastic, now ran clear, its banks lined with roots that curled like serpents toward the sky. And then, the animals returned.

The crow returned, its feathers black as the void between stars, its cry a warning in the language of the wind. The wolf returned, its breath steaming in the crisp autumn air, and the first time it howled, it did not sound like a beast. It sounded like a song.

The villagers did not welcome the change. Some burned the old books, others hung themselves from the branches of the oak to keep the thing from finding them. But the thing did not care for their fear. It only wanted what had been lost: the stories, the songs, the *meaning* of things.

Clara stood at the edge of the village square, her hands trembling as she pressed the brass key into the clock’s face. The gears screamed like a dying man, then stilled. The hands spun forward, and when they stopped again, they pointed to the present—not as it had been, but as it *should have been*.

The Old Man in the Clock smiled. *"You have begun the work,"* he said, his voice like the creak of a bridge over water. *"But the world is not ready. The chains are still too tight."*

And then, the village burned.
