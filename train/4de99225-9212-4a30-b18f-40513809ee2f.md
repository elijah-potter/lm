## A Counseling Session: Facing Uncertainty

**Setting:** A warmly lit, comfortably furnished therapy office.

**Characters:**

*   **Dr. Eleanor Vance:** A seasoned therapist, known for her calm demeanor and insightful approach.
*   **Liam Walker:** A young professional, experiencing significant anxiety related to a career transition.

---

**Liam:** (Sighs, fidgeting slightly in his chair) It just feels… overwhelming, Dr. Vance. I accepted the new position, which I *should* be excited about, but the more I think about it, the more I feel like I’m standing on the edge of something I can’t quite grasp.

**Dr. Vance:** (Nods gently, maintaining eye contact) It’s understandable to feel that way, Liam. Accepting a new role, especially one that represents a significant change, naturally brings a degree of uncertainty. You’re acknowledging that feeling of being on the edge, and that’s a very important first step. Can you tell me a little more about what aspects of this transition feel particularly daunting?

**Liam:** I’m moving into a management position, which is great, on paper. I've been in individual contributor roles my entire career. Suddenly, I’m responsible for a team, for their performance, for… well, everything. I worry about letting them down, about making the wrong decisions, about not being *good* at it. It’s not just the workload, it’s the responsibility.

**Dr. Vance:** (Leans forward slightly) So, the shift in responsibility is a core component of your anxiety. It sounds like you’re carrying a significant weight of expectation, both from external sources and, importantly, from yourself. It’s admirable that you’re so concerned with the well-being and success of your team. It suggests a strong sense of empathy and a genuine desire to lead effectively.

**Liam:** (Looks down at his hands) I just… I don’t want to fail. I'm terrified of proving myself to be inadequate. I keep replaying scenarios in my head, imagining all the things that could go wrong.

**Dr. Vance:** (Pauses, allowing him space) Those "what if" scenarios can be incredibly powerful, and they often amplify our fears. It's natural to anticipate potential challenges, but when those thoughts become a constant loop, they can be debilitating. We can explore some techniques to help you manage those intrusive thoughts. Before we do that, I’m curious: what are some of the qualities you believe a *good* manager possesses?

**Liam:** (Thinks for a moment) Someone who listens, definitely. Someone who’s approachable, who creates a supportive environment. Someone who’s… fair. I guess I'm trying to be those things, but I'm also terrified I’m not.

**Dr. Vance:** (Smiles warmly) You’ve articulated some truly valuable qualities, Liam. And it’s interesting that you’re already identifying those as hallmarks of effective leadership. It suggests you have a clear vision of what you want to embody in this new role. Sometimes, the very qualities we strive for are already present within us, even if we’re not fully recognizing them. It sounds like you’re setting a very high bar for yourself.

**Liam:** I suppose I am. I just… I want to do well.

**Dr. Vance:** (Nods) That desire to excel is a positive driving force. We can work on shifting your perspective, perhaps focusing less on the potential for failure and more on the opportunity to learn and grow. We can also explore strategies for self-compassion – treating yourself with the same kindness and understanding you would offer a friend facing a similar challenge. Would you be open to discussing some practical techniques for managing those anxious thoughts and cultivating a more balanced perspective?

**Liam:** (Takes a deep breath) Yes, please. I think I need that.
