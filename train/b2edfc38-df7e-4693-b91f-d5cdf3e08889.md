The dim glow of a flickering streetlamp cast long shadows as **Marcus** stepped outside, his breath misting in the cold air. The air smelled of damp earth and something metallic—rust and old oil. A distant siren wailed, swallowed by the wind. Around him, neighbors huddled under blankets, their faces half-lit by the eerie blue of emergency flashlights.

A crack of static filled the silence as **Lena** emerged from her apartment, her coat damp, her eyes scanning the street. "They’re still working on the line," she muttered, tugging at her gloves. "But the power’s gone for good in this block." Her phone buzzed—a text from her sister: *They’re cutting the generator. Meet at the park.*

Marcus exhaled sharply, his fingers digging into his pockets. The last time they’d been out here, the fire had spread faster than they’d expected. They moved quickly, weaving between cars, their footsteps muffled by the gravel underfoot. The park was a maze of flickering lanterns and half-burned campfires, the scent of charred wood thick in the air.

Ahead, the crowd thinned—only **Elias**, a man with a scar running from his temple to his jaw, stood near the old clock tower. His arms were crossed, his gaze fixed on the horizon where the first stars began to pierce the dark. "We don’t have time for games," Elias said, voice low. "We’re either with them or against them. And right now, we’re against the world that left us in the dark."

Lena’s phone beeped again. *They’re moving to the subway tunnels. Meet at the old train station in 15.*

Marcus didn’t hesitate. He grabbed Lena’s wrist, pulling her toward the abandoned subway entrance—a rusted metal archway, its doors half-buried in snow. The air inside was stale, thick with the scent of damp concrete and fear. A single flickering bulb cast long shadows across the platform, where the last of the stranded passengers milled about, their faces gaunt in the dim light.

Elias was already there, his hand pressed against the door handle. "Move," he growled. The crowd surged forward, some pushing, others shoving, but they all moved—toward the stairs that spiraled down into the earth.

At the bottom, the tunnel stretched endlessly, its walls slick with condensation. The distant hum of machinery—some kind of generator, perhaps—cut through the silence. But it was weak, fading, and they knew it wouldn’t last.

Lena grabbed Marcus’s arm. "We’re not going down there," she whispered. But Elias was already ahead, his boots pounding against the concrete.

"Lena."

She turned. "What?"

"Stay back," he said, but his voice was hollow. The group was moving. The machine’s hum grew louder, brighter—then died.

Silence.

Then, a single voice, muffled by the tunnel walls: *"They’re coming."*
