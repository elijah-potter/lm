The alleyway between the old stone warehouse and the new glass-fronted café hummed with the quiet energy of time erasing itself. Rusted bolts and peeling paint clung to the walls like the ghosts of forgotten hands, while the pavement beneath cracked in patterns that mirrored the fractures in the city’s soul. A single, weathered bench sat half-lost in shadows, its wooden slats worn smooth by decades of abandoned visitors. The café’s neon sign flickered—*Café HOME*—as if a hand had just scrawled it there, but the "H" was faded, the "O" a smudge of smoke from a long-dead cigarette.

This was not a place that welcomed you. It wasn’t supposed to. The warehouse’s iron gates had stood guard for half a century, their rusted hinges groaning like a dying man’s last breath. But here, in the crevice between old and new, something stubbornly refused to fade. A streetlamp’s bulb cast long, trembling shadows that didn’t quite reach the pavement—just enough to dance with the flicker of the café’s neon. The air smelled of damp earth and something metallic, like old blood drying. You could hear the distant murmur of the river, the distant laugh of a child playing somewhere beyond the next bridge, but it was distant because it wasn’t here.

This was a place where the past had a mouth and spoke in whispers. The stone floor bore the weight of a thousand footsteps, but no one walked in the same path twice. A graffiti tag, half-smothered by moss, read *"Here."* It wasn’t there when you arrived, but it was there when you left, as if the city itself had decided to mark its presence. The alley wasn’t temporary. It was a wound that never closed. It was the place where the city forgot to forget itself.
