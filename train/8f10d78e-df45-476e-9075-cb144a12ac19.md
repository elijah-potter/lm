## A Counseling Session

**Setting:** A comfortably furnished therapist's office. Soft lighting, a few plants, and a box of tissues are visible.

**Characters:**

* **Dr. Eleanor Vance:** A therapist in her late 40s, known for her calm demeanor and insightful approach.
* **Liam Davies:** A client in his early 30s, visibly stressed and somewhat withdrawn.

**(The session begins with Liam seated on a comfortable armchair, facing Dr. Vance.)**

**Liam:** It feels… overwhelming, to be honest. Just constantly. There’s this low hum of worry that I can’t seem to shake.

**Dr. Vance:** (Nods gently, maintaining eye contact) It sounds incredibly draining, Liam. That persistent feeling of worry can certainly take a toll. Can you tell me a little more about what that "hum" feels like, and when you first noticed it?

**Liam:** Well, it’s been building for a few months, I think. It started with work, just the usual pressure. Deadlines, meetings, trying to please everyone. But it’s… it’s spread. Now I worry about my family, about my friendships, even about things that are completely outside of my control. Like the weather. Ridiculous, I know.

**Dr. Vance:** It's not ridiculous at all. Worry often expands to encompass a wider range of concerns when it becomes a more pervasive feeling. It’s a natural response to feeling out of control. You mentioned work initially. Are you feeling supported in your professional environment?

**Liam:** I am, mostly. My colleagues are good people. My boss is… reasonable. But there’s this constant feeling that I’m falling short. That I need to be doing more, achieving more. I compare myself to others, which I know is unproductive, but I can't seem to stop.

**Dr. Vance:** The comparison trap. It’s a very common experience, particularly in today's society. It’s easy to believe that everyone else has it figured out. But often, what we see on the surface is just a carefully curated image. Tell me, when you find yourself comparing yourself to others, what thoughts typically accompany that?

**Liam:** (Hesitates, looking down at his hands) That I’m not good enough. That I’m failing. That I’ll disappoint people. It’s a cascade, really. One thought leads to another, and suddenly I’m spiraling.

**Dr. Vance:** That sounds incredibly difficult to navigate. It's understandable that you feel overwhelmed. Let’s explore those thoughts a little further. When you think, “I’m not good enough,” what evidence do you have to support that belief? And, perhaps more importantly, what evidence contradicts it?

**Liam:** (Pauses, considering) That’s… that’s a good question. I haven’t really looked at it that way before. I suppose I’ve been so focused on what I *haven’t* done, I haven’t acknowledged what I *have*. I did get that promotion last year, didn't I? And my family is always saying how proud they are.

**Dr. Vance:** Those are significant accomplishments, Liam. And it’s important to acknowledge them. It sounds like you’re minimizing your successes in favor of focusing on perceived shortcomings. That's a pattern we can certainly examine. It’s also important to recognize that your family's pride in you is a genuine reflection of your value.

**Liam:** It just feels… fragile, you know? Like it could all disappear in an instant.

**Dr. Vance:** That sense of fragility is often rooted in a fear of vulnerability. Allowing ourselves to be seen, to be valued, also opens us up to the possibility of disappointment. But avoiding vulnerability entirely can be equally debilitating. We can work on developing a more balanced perspective, one that acknowledges both the potential for disappointment and the inherent worth of your efforts. For next week, I'd like you to try a simple exercise. When you notice yourself engaging in negative self-comparison, write down the thought, then immediately write down one positive thing you’ve accomplished or a strength you possess. Just a few sentences.

**Liam:** Okay. I can try that. It sounds… manageable.

**Dr. Vance:** That’s all we ask. Small steps, Liam. And remember, it takes time and practice to shift deeply ingrained patterns of thought. I'm here to support you through that process.



**(The session concludes with Liam appearing slightly more relaxed, and Dr. Vance offering a reassuring smile.)**
