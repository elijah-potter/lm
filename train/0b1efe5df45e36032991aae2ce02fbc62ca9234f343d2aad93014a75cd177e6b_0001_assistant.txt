Leaves crunched under Bailey's feet as she hiked along the tree-filled path. A bee softly landed on a red-osier dogwood flower and searched for nectar. Red-osier dogwood flowers are a source of nectar and pollen for many insects, such as native bees, wasps, flies, and butterflies.

Bailey gazed at the glittering lake where waves were gently rising and falling. A dark shape with a long neck popped out of the water for a few seconds, then disappeared. Bailey wasn't sure what the strange outline was, but she was curious. 

Further down the trail, Bailey saw a black squirrel rustling in the leaves, searching for food. Black squirrels are a melanistic subgroup of squirrels with black coloration on their fur. They are a result of an abnormal pigment gene carried over from the fox squirrel. 

Hearing the splash of water, Bailey glanced towards the lake again and saw the same shape dipping below the surface. Walking over to a large, flat rock, Bailey sat down and patiently waited to see it again. The water started rippling, and a dark head appeared above the water, moving in her direction. It had something in its mouth. The creature wasn't too large, so Bailey wasn't frightened. 

The fuzzy, brown animal scampered out of the water with a fish in its mouth. It was an otter! For most otters, fish is the staple of their diet.  This is often supplemented by frogs, crayfish, and crabs. Some otters are experts at opening shellfish, and others will feed on available small mammals or birds. 

Hiking had always been a favorite pastime for Bailey. She never knew what she would see during each adventure, and after a hike, Bailey always felt uplifted. According to the American Hiking Society, “Countless studies and research have consistently shown that regular exercise not only improves our overall health and fitness but lengthens and improves the quality of our lives.”
