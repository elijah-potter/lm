## TIL the "Smell of Rain" Has a Name: Petrichor

Today I learned that the distinctive, earthy scent released when rain falls on dry ground has a scientifically recognized name: petrichor. Derived from the Greek words "petra" (stone) and "ichor" (the ethereal fluid that flows in the veins of the gods in Greek mythology), it’s a surprisingly complex phenomenon.

For centuries, people have appreciated this aroma, but its chemical origins remained largely unknown until 1964. Two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and R. G. Thomas, were the first to identify it while studying the scent of eucalyptus oil after rain. They discovered that petrichor isn’t solely the smell of rain itself, but rather a combination of oils released from plants and geosmin, a metabolic byproduct produced by bacteria (specifically *Streptomyces*) residing in the soil.

During dry periods, these bacteria produce geosmin, which accumulates in the soil. When rain falls, the impact dislodges these compounds, which are then aerosolized and carried into the air, creating the characteristic scent we associate with rainfall. The intensity of the smell varies depending on the type of soil, the plant life present, and the duration of the dry spell. It’s a beautiful example of how seemingly simple sensory experiences are rooted in intricate biological and chemical processes.
