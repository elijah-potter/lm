The alarm blared at 04:17, its shrill tone vibrating through the room like an uninvited guest. The coffee machine hissed, filling the air with a bitter, familiar scent as steam curled from the cracked lid. The kitchen was a chaotic tableau of half-packed boxes, half-emptied drawers, and a mug still holding yesterday’s remnants—black coffee, half a granola bar, and a note from the last customer. The day had started like this for the past three years.

The first task was the mail. The stack was thicker than usual—utility bills, a credit card statement, and that mysterious package from *No Reply* with a return address that wasn’t a real address. The postmark was from last week, but the envelope had been opened. The contents were a single sheet of paper, folded in half, the edges singed as if hastily tossed into a fire. The ink had faded, the words barely legible:

*"You’re not the first. Neither are you the last."*

The mailbox was locked, but the key was in the drawer where it belonged. The lock was rusted, the key too. The mailbox creaked open with a sound like a door left ajar too long. The rest of the mail spilled out—paychecks, subscription renewals, and a letter from the landlord about the new tenant upstairs. The one who’d moved in last month. The one who’d stopped coming home.

The weather app flickered on the counter. A storm front was moving in from the west, a cold front that would turn the day into a slow, grinding slog. The radio crackled to life, the static hissing through the speakers as a news anchor intoned over the static:

*"…reports of unusual activity in the eastern states. Officials advise caution."*

The coffee was cold. The granola bar was stale. The day stretched ahead like a line of tiles laid in the wrong pattern, one wrong step and it would all fall apart. The phone buzzed. A text from the landlord: *"Your new neighbor’s name is in the papers. Looks like a real estate agent. Or maybe a developer. Either way, they’re not local."*
