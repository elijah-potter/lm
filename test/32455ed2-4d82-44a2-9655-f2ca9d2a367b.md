## TIL The "Smell of Rain" Has a Name: Petrichor

Today I learned that the distinctive, earthy scent many of us associate with rain actually has a scientific name: petrichor. Derived from the Greek words *petra* (stone) and *ichor* (the fluid that flows in the veins of the gods in Greek mythology), it describes the aroma released when rain falls on dry earth.

For decades, scientists were perplexed by this scent. It wasn’t simply the smell of wet dirt. Research in the 1960s by Australian scientists Isabel Joy Bear and R.G. Thomas revealed the primary source to be oils secreted by plants, particularly during dry periods. These oils accumulate on soil and rocks. When rain falls, they are released into the air as aerosols, creating the characteristic fragrance. Additionally, a compound called geosmin, produced by soil-dwelling bacteria, is also released and contributes to the overall smell. Humans are remarkably sensitive to geosmin, capable of detecting it in concentrations as low as 5 parts per trillion.

The phenomenon is most noticeable after a prolonged dry spell, as the oils have had more time to accumulate. It’s a surprisingly complex chemical process resulting in a universally recognized and appreciated sensory experience.
