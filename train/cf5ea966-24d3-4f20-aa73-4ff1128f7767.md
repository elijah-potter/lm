The first time I experienced routine, I was seventeen, sitting in a dimly lit diner on a Saturday morning, watching the clock tick past between orders. The waitress—brown hair tied in a messy bun, her fingers tracing the rim of a coffee cup—slid a stack of pancakes across the counter. The syrup dripped like molten gold. It wasn’t the first time I’d seen food disappear into a face, but this time, the act felt deliberate. The morning was routine: the same coffee, the same waitress, the same slow, rhythmic hum of the diner’s fluorescent lights. I drank it all in, then left with a stack of bills and a half-eaten burger, the routine feeling like a second skin I’d never shed.

By twenty-three, I’d learned to recognize the patterns: the same coffee shop at 5:30 AM, the same coffee, the same way the barista stirred it without looking. The routine wasn’t about perfection; it was about the quiet rebellion of doing the same thing, every time, as if it were the only thing worth doing. I stopped questioning it. The world outside was chaos, but inside this routine, I found something stable. I ate the same cereal, drove the same route, and woke up at the same time—because it was easier than figuring out what to do instead. The routine had become my anchor.
