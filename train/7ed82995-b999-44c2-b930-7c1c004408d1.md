The first mistake came when Elias left his coffee untouched on the kitchen counter after breakfast. He had meant to save it for later, but the morning’s rush had blurred his memory. By the time he realized, the coffee was cold and the cup’s residue had turned the countertop sticky.

That evening, he found a trail of crumbs leading from his office chair to the printer—he had forgotten to clear his desk before leaving for the day. The printer jammed on his last draft, and the output was a jagged, ink-smeared mess.

The next morning, he woke to find the thermostat set to 20°C (68°F) instead of his usual 22°C (72°F). The house was warm, but he didn’t notice until his dog whined for a cold drink. He had left the window open last night while fixing the printer.

By midday, the office’s Wi-Fi router had been replaced three times after Elias accidentally unplugged it during a "quick fix." The third attempt left the building’s entire network offline for an hour.

That night, he realized his mistake was in how he saw it: the system was a network of small, interconnected failures. Each one had been a misstep, but together, they had formed a feedback loop of his own creation. He started documenting every mistake—what caused it, how it unfolded, and what he learned from it. By the end of the week, the office’s chaos had turned into a structured chaos, and the mistakes were no longer mistakes but data points.

One evening, while reviewing the notes, he noticed the first mistake had been a miscommunication—he had assumed his coffee was intentional when it was accidental. The second was a delay in fixing a problem that had cascaded. The third was a misstep in understanding the system’s feedback loops. He began to see that mistakes weren’t failures but feedback.

By the end of the month, the office’s systems were more reliable, but Elias still made them—he just knew how to fix them faster. The coffee was still cold sometimes, but he had a system for it. The printer still jammed, but he had a checklist. The Wi-Fi still failed, but he had a backup plan.

One day, his assistant walked in and asked, *"Why do you keep writing about mistakes?"*
Elias smiled. *"Because every mistake is just data. The real question is what we do with it."*

The story isn’t over yet—it’s still being written.
