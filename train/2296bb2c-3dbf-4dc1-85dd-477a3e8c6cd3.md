## TIL the "Smell of Rain" is Actually a Combination of Several Chemical Reactions

Today I learned that the distinct, earthy scent many associate with rain – often referred to as "petrichor" – isn’t a single compound, but rather a complex blend of volatile organic compounds released by several different processes.

For centuries, this aroma has captivated people, inspiring poetry and provoking curiosity. While the term "petrichor" was only coined in 1964 by two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas, the phenomenon itself has been observed and appreciated for generations.

The primary contributors to this scent are two distinct sources. Firstly, geosmin, an organic compound produced by bacteria (specifically *Streptomyces*) living in the soil, is released into the air when raindrops impact the ground. Humans are incredibly sensitive to geosmin, capable of detecting it at concentrations as low as five parts per trillion. Secondly, oils secreted by plants accumulate on surfaces during dry periods. When rain falls, these oils are released into the air, creating a fragrant, citrusy note that complements the earthy geosmin. 

Therefore, the evocative "smell of rain" is not a singular olfactory experience, but a fascinating interplay of microbial activity and botanical secretions – a testament to the intricate chemistry occurring within our natural world.
