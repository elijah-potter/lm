The old farmhouse stood half-collapsed under the weight of autumn leaves, its sagging porch swing creaking under a child’s weight. The boy, **Elias**, sat cross-legged on the cracked wooden floor, his breath visible in the cold air as he traced the words *"I’ll fix your bike"* into the dirt with a stick. His father, **Mister Hargrove**, leaned against the kitchen doorframe, arms crossed, his worn boots still damp from the rain outside.

*"You’ve been saying that for three weeks, son,"* Mister Hargrove muttered, not looking up. *"And still nothing’s changed."* Elias swallowed hard, the promise hanging between them like a spider’s web. Outside, the wind howled through the broken windowpanes, rattling the glass.

*"I’ll do it,"* Elias whispered, voice trembling. *"I swear on my grandma’s old Bible—"* He dug into his pocket, pulling out the faded book she’d left behind. The pages smelled of old ink and dust. Mister Hargrove finally met his eyes, the lines of exhaustion etched deeper with every passing day.

*"You’re not ten anymore,"* he said, voice rough. *"You’re supposed to know when to keep your word."* Elias didn’t flinch. He didn’t move. The promise was carved into his heart now, heavier than his own shadow.

The bike—rusted, missing the front wheel—lay abandoned in the barn. Mister Hargrove exhaled, running a hand through his salt-and-pepper hair. *"Fine."* He didn’t sound convinced. *"But if I find out you lied to me again, I’ll make you do the chores for a year."* Elias nodded. *"I won’t let you down."* The barn door creaked open. Mister Hargrove stepped outside, leaving him alone with his choice.

The rain fell harder now, but Elias didn’t move. The promise was his now. The question was whether he’d keep it.
