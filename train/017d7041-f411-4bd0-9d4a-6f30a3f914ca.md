The city’s skyline lacks the neon glow of a thriving nightlife hub, instead dominated by the muted, flickering LED strips of a **fossil-fuel-free** transit hub, their cold blue hues casting long shadows over the abandoned arcades beneath. The rivers that once pulsed with riverboat traffic now flow sluggishly through canyon walls lined with rusted billboards—once advertising **gambling dens, live music halls, and late-night diners**—now displaying only static or corrupted ads for **souvenirs from the old world**. The streets, where the last of the skyscrapers once stood, are choked with overgrown vines and the skeletal remains of billboards that once promoted **theatrical shows, carnival rides, and the latest "must-see" attractions**. The air smells of ozone and old concrete, where the only entertainment left is the occasional **ghostly echo of a jukebox playing a loop of a 1970s disco track** from a building that burned to the ground last century. The people who wander these streets—those who still move at all—wear masks that obscure not faces but **the places they refuse to visit**: the carnivals, the casinos, the neon-lit dive bars that were the city’s heartbeat before the great **unplugging**. The last remaining theaters, now repurposed as **community centers or storage units**, hang their marquees upside down, as if the audience never left. The only thing that moves freely is the **cobwebbed, abandoned traffic**—a ghostly parade of cars that once roared down these streets, now reduced to a slow, creaking procession of rusted metal and silent engines. The city’s bones whisper of what was lost before the **last great fire**, the one that burned the last of the old-world magic away, leaving only the silence where the music used to be.
