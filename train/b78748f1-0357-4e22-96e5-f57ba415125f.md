Decisions made under uncertainty often hinge on heuristics and trade-offs between risk and opportunity. In a clinical trial, a physician must weigh patient symptoms against limited diagnostic data, choosing between empirical treatment and delayed confirmation. The **Bayesian approach** may integrate prior probabilities (e.g., 30% chance of infection) with new evidence (e.g., fever) to update likelihoods, but miscalibration of probabilities can lead to overreliance on anecdotes or underestimation of systemic biases. For example, a surgeon may prioritize a 15% chance of a positive MRI result over a 90% confidence in symptoms, opting for an invasive procedure despite a 20% false-positive rate. The tension between immediate action and data-driven caution reflects the paradox of incomplete information: decisions are not purely rational but a blend of intuition, cultural conditioning, and the constraints of real-time constraints. Outcomes often reveal hindsight bias—what was deemed uncertain then may seem obvious now.
