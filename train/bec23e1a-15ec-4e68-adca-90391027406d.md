The weight of silence pressed down like a second skin. I had always known the city would eat itself whole, but tonight, the hunger was visible—flickering streetlights, the slow drip of rain on pavement, the way the neon signs blurred into ghosts. My fingers traced the cracks in the floor, where the old tiles had long since given up the ghost of their color. A café door swung open, and for a heartbeat, the world was warm—steam curling from a cup, the scent of espresso mingling with the faint, metallic tang of the subway’s last train. I sat in the booth, the kind that smelled like old paper and forgotten coffee, and let the city breathe through me.

Outside, the windows were mirrors, reflecting the dying light of a thousand forgotten lives. A woman in a raincoat moved past, her breath fogging the glass like a ghost’s whisper. The traffic hummed a lullaby of engines and tires, but I heard nothing. Not the distant wail of a siren, not the laughter of children playing in some other corner of the world, but only the echo of my own name, whispered by the wind.

The sky was a bruise—pale and swollen, the last remnants of day bleeding into something deeper, something heavier. It wasn’t just dark; it was *hungry*, swallowing the last light like a mouth full of teeth. The stars were still there, but they weren’t stars anymore. They were just more of the same: distant, indifferent. The Milky Way stretched like a scar across the heavens, its jagged edges cutting through the haze. Below, the city pulsed—lifeblood and rust, the heart of a machine that had long since forgotten how to beat. The rain came in sheets now, a slow, relentless curtain, and for a moment, I thought I saw my own reflection in the puddles, but it wasn’t me. It was something else. Something older.

The city slept, and I was still. The sky had already begun its work—turning the world into something new.
