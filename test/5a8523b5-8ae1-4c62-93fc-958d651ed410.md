The lighthouse keeper, Silas Blackwood, adjusted the wick of the lamp, the familiar scent of kerosene a comforting presence in the swirling November fog. He had maintained the beacon on Cormorant’s Point for thirty-seven years, a solitary existence punctuated only by the rhythmic crash of waves and the occasional supply ship. Tonight, the fog was particularly dense, a milky, impenetrable curtain that swallowed the world beyond the tower’s immediate vicinity.

He checked the chronometer – 11:17 PM. Routine demanded a thorough inspection every hour. Silas was a man of routine; it was the bedrock of his sanity in this isolated realm. He’s seen storms that threatened to tear the lighthouse from its foundations, witnessed shipwrecks that left him haunted by the cries of the lost, and endured months of unrelenting solitude. Routine, he’s always believed, was his shield against despair.

A faint, persistent sound, different from the usual symphony of the sea, reached his ears. It was a high-pitched whine, almost imperceptible beneath the wind’s moan. Silas paused, his hand resting on the cool stone of the wall. He strained to listen, dismissing it initially as a trick of the wind. But the sound persisted, growing slightly louder.

He descended the winding staircase, his boots echoing in the hollow space. He reached the base of the tower and stepped out onto the small, circular platform that surrounded the structure. The fog pressed against him, damp and chilling. He scanned the gray expanse, his eyes struggling to penetrate the gloom.

Then he saw it. A small vessel, barely visible through the swirling mist, its lights flickering weakly. It was far too close to the jagged rocks that guarded the shoreline. Silas recognized the design – a fishing trawler, likely caught in the unexpected fog bank. 

He immediately began the emergency protocol. He activated the foghorn, its mournful bellow cutting through the silence. He reached for the radio, his fingers nimble despite the urgency. 

“Cormorant’s Point to unidentified vessel,” he spoke, his voice calm and steady. “You are dangerously close to the rocks. Adjust your course immediately. Cormorant’s Point, over.”

A crackling response came back, faint but audible. “This is the *Sea Serpent*. We’re having difficulty navigating. Visibility is near zero. Requesting assistance.”

Silas identified himself and began to issue instructions, guiding the *Sea Serpent* away from the treacherous coastline. The process was slow and painstaking, requiring precise communication and unwavering focus. He could sense the anxiety in the voice of the captain, a young man named Thomas Ashton.

After what felt like an eternity, Silas received confirmation that the *Sea Serpent* had altered its course and was safely navigating towards open water. 

“You’re clear, Captain Ashton,” Silas said, relief flooding through him. “Maintain a steady course and exercise caution. Cormorant’s Point, over.”

“Thank you, Mr. Blackwood,” Thomas replied. “You saved us. We owe you a great debt.”

Silas simply acknowledged the gratitude and returned to the lamp room, the warmth of the light a welcome contrast to the chill of the fog. He resumed his vigil, the rhythmic sweep of the beam a silent promise of safety for those who sailed the waters around Cormorant’s Point. He thought of Thomas Ashton, and for the first time in many years, Silas felt a flicker of something other than routine – a quiet sense of connection, a subtle warmth that eased the burden of his solitary existence. The fog, though still present, seemed a little less oppressive.
