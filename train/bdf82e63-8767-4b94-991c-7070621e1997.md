## A Counseling Session

**Setting:** A comfortable, warmly lit therapist’s office.

**Characters:**

* **Eleanor Vance:** A client in her early thirties, visibly anxious.
* **Dr. Alistair Finch:** A therapist in his late forties, calm and composed.

**(The session begins with Eleanor seated on a comfortable armchair, facing Dr. Finch.)**

**Eleanor:** It feels… overwhelming, Dr. Finch. Just constantly. A low hum of worry that never quite goes away.

**Dr. Finch:** (Nodding gently) That sounds incredibly draining, Eleanor. To carry that persistent feeling, that “hum of worry,” as you describe it, must take a significant toll. Can you tell me a little more about what that feels like, specifically?

**Eleanor:** It’s hard to pinpoint. It’s not always about a specific event. Sometimes it’s just… the feeling that something bad is about to happen. I find myself checking things repeatedly – locking the doors, turning off the stove – things I know I’ve already done. And then I worry that I *haven’t* done them, even after I’ve checked.

**Dr. Finch:** So, a cycle of checking, then doubting, then rechecking. That makes perfect sense. It’s a very common response to anxiety; a way of trying to regain a sense of control. You’re attempting to neutralize the potential for something negative to occur.  How long have you been experiencing this, would you say?

**Eleanor:** It’s gotten worse over the past six months, I think. It used to be occasional, something I could brush off. Now… now it’s impacting my sleep, my work. I’m isolating myself.

**Dr. Finch:** Isolation is a natural consequence when anxiety becomes that pervasive. It’s understandable that you’re withdrawing. You're protecting yourself, in a way.  Tell me, what are some of the situations where you notice this worry is most intense?

**Eleanor:**  Big decisions, definitely. Anything that feels irreversible. I’m agonizing over whether to accept a promotion at work. It’s a great opportunity, but it also comes with a lot more responsibility, and I’m terrified of failing.

**Dr. Finch:** It’s admirable that you’re acknowledging both the potential benefits and the risks.  It sounds like the fear of failure is a significant driver of your anxiety in this situation.  What thoughts are going through your head when you consider accepting the promotion?

**Eleanor:**  "I'm not good enough." "I’ll mess it up." "Everyone will see how incompetent I am." It's a constant barrage.

**Dr. Finch:** Those are very powerful and critical voices. It's important to recognize them for what they are: thoughts, not necessarily truths.  They are narratives you’re telling yourself. We can explore those narratives further and examine where they might originate.  Have you always been a person who tends to be self-critical?

**Eleanor:** I think so. My parents were… demanding. High expectations. There was a lot of pressure to succeed.

**Dr. Finch:** It's common for childhood experiences to shape our internal dialogue.  The pressure you describe could certainly contribute to the self-critical voice you’re hearing now.  We can work on challenging those ingrained patterns of thought.  For now, let’s focus on this promotion. What would it look like to approach this decision with a little more self-compassion?

**Eleanor:** (Hesitates) I don’t know… I guess… acknowledging that it’s okay to be scared? And that even if I *do* make a mistake, it doesn’t mean I’m a failure.

**Dr. Finch:** That’s an excellent start, Eleanor. Recognizing that vulnerability and imperfection are part of the human experience is a powerful step.  We can practice some techniques to help you cultivate that self-compassion and challenge those negative thought patterns.  How does that sound?

**Eleanor:** It sounds… hopeful. Thank you, Dr. Finch.



**(The session continues.)**
