A dimly lit chamber hums with the low murmur of flickering candles, their wax pooling unevenly. The air smells of old incense and damp stone. Three figures—once bound by tradition—now sit in stiff, silent rows, their faces half-hidden beneath veils. The elder, once a priest, now speaks in a voice that lacks conviction:

*"Tonight, we honor the forgotten. The words are the same, but the hearts that once beat for them now stutter like a broken clock. The stones we touch are cold; the waters we drink are bitter. The offering is still a knife, still a libation, still a prayer—but the hunger that fed them is gone. The gods have stopped answering."*

The elder gestures to a cracked altar where a single, unreadable inscription gleams beneath the candlelight. A younger figure reaches out, their fingers brushing the stone. They close their eyes, but their breath comes fast, as if caught between disbelief and something older. The last act: the knife’s edge grazes their palm—not in sacrifice, but as a test. Blood wells, but no offering is made.

The chamber falls silent. The candles die. The ritual is over. The meaning has been erased.
