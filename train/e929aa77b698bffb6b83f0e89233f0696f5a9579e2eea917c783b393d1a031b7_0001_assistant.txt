That afternoon, Mr. Stern, the geometry teacher, started drawing cubes and cylinders on the board. Gerry sat at their desk, adding up a column of figures in their notebook—the rent, the light and gas bills, a new gym suit, some socks. Maybe they would move somewhere else, and they could have their own room. Gerry turned the squares and triangles into little houses in the country.

"For your homework," Mr. Stern was saying with their back to the class, "set your problem up this way." They wrote GIVEN: in large letters and then gave a formula for the first problem. Then they wrote TO FIND: and listed three items the students were to include in their answers.

Gerry started to raise their hand to ask what all the squares and angles had to do with solving real problems, like the ones they had. But they decided it was better not to and sat on their hands. Your big mouth got you in trouble last term.

In hygiene class, Mrs. Potter kept saying that the body was a wonderful machine. Every time Gerry looked up from their notebook, they would hear the same thing. "Right now, your body is manufacturing all the proteins and tissues and energy you will need to get through tomorrow."

And Gerry kept wondering, How? How does my body know what it will need when I don't even know what I'll need to get through tomorrow?

As they headed down the hall to their next class, Gerry remembered that they hadn't done the homework for English. Mrs. Scott had said to write a poem, and Gerry had meant to do it during lunchtime. After all, there was nothing to it—a flower here, a raindrop there, moon, June, rose, nose. But the men carrying off the furniture had made them forget.

"And now put away your books," Mrs. Scott was saying as Gerry tried to scribble a poem quickly. "Today, we can give King Arthur's knights a rest. Let's talk about poetry."

Mrs. Scott moved up and down the aisles, talking about her favorite poems and reciting a line now and then. She got very excited whenever she passed a desk and could pick up the homework from a student who had remembered to do the assignment.

"A poem is your own special way of saying what you feel and what you see," Mrs. Scott went on, her lips moist. It was her favorite subject.

"Some poets write about the light that... that... makes the world sunny," she said, passing Gerry's desk. "Sometimes an idea takes the form of a picture—an image."

For almost half an hour, Mrs. Scott stood at the front of the room, reading poems and talking about the lives of the great poets. Gerry drew more houses and designs for curtains.

"So, for those who haven't done their homework, try it now," Mrs. Scott said. "Try expressing what it is like to be... to be alive in this... this glorious world."

"Oh, brother," Gerry muttered to themselves as Mrs. Scott moved up and down the aisles again, waving her hands and leaning over the students' shoulders, saying, "That's nice," or "Keep trying." Finally, she came to Gerry's desk and stopped, looking down at them.

"I can't write a poem," Gerry said flatly, before they even realized they were going to speak at all. They said it very loudly, and the whole class looked up.

"And why not?" Mrs. Scott asked, looking hurt.

"I can't write a poem, Mrs. Scott, because nothing lovely's been happening in my life. I haven't seen a flower since Mother's Day, and the sun doesn't shine on my side of the street. No robins come sing on my windowsill."

Gerry swallowed hard. They thought about saying that their father doesn't even come to visit anymore but changed their mind. "Just the rain comes," they went on, "and the bills come, and the men to move out our furniture. I'm sorry, but I can't write no pretty poem."

Teddy Johnson leaned over and was about to giggle and crack the whole class up, but Mrs. Scott looked so serious that he changed his mind.

"You have just said the most... the most poetic thing, Gerry Moore," said Mrs. Scott. Her hands flew up to touch the silk scarf around her neck. "'Nothing lovely's been happening in my life.' She repeated it so quietly that everyone had to lean forward to hear.

"Class," Mrs. Scott said very sadly, clearing her throat, "you have just heard the best poem you will ever hear." She went to the board and stood there for a long time, staring at the chalk in her hand.

"I'd like you to copy it down," she said. She wrote it just as Gerry had said it, bad grammar and all.

Nothing lovely's been happening in my life.
I haven't seen a flower since Mother's Day,
And the sun doesn't even shine on my side of the street.
No robins come sing on my windowsill.
Just the rain comes, and the bills come,
And the men to move out our furniture.
I'm sorry, but I can't write no pretty poem.

Mrs. Scott stopped writing, but she kept her back to the class for a long time—long after Gerry had closed their notebook.

And even when the bell rang, and everyone came over to smile at Gerry or to tap them on the shoulder or to kid them about being the school poet, Gerry waited for Mrs. Scott to put the chalk down and turn around. Finally, Gerry stacked up their books and started to leave. Then they thought they heard a whimper—the way Mrs. Watson's dog whimpered sometimes—and they saw Mrs. Scott's shoulders shake a little.
