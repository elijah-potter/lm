A weathered, half-timbered cottage nestles at the edge of a mist-shrouded valley, its stone walls worn smooth by decades of rain. The roof, once steeply pitched, now leans slightly inward, like a sleeping giant. The floorboards creak underfoot, each one telling a story of generations—some cracked from age, others warped from the slow erosion of damp. Windows, once frosted with stained glass, now have their panes slightly misaligned, letting in the light in uneven patches. A narrow stone path, overgrown with ivy, winds up the hill to the cottage, its path barely visible in the undergrowth.

The garden is a tangled mess of wildflowers, roses, and thistles—some thriving, others stubbornly clinging to life. A rusted iron gate, half-sunken in the earth, stands as the only barrier between the cottage and the woods. Inside, the fireplace is cold, its hearth lined with old, uneven bricks. A single, frayed blanket lies over a wooden chair, its scent of mothballs and damp wood lingering. A worn leather armchair faces the fireplace, its cushion patched with fabric from an unknown repair. On a low table, a half-empty teacup and a cracked porcelain teapot sit beside a cracked mirror that reflects a world that hasn’t changed much in a hundred years.

The air hums with the sound of distant birds, the occasional rustle of leaves, and the faint whisper of wind through the trees. It feels like a place that has been here forever—yet every object, every crack, every faded stain whispers of time slipping through its fingers. This isn’t a place that was built to last forever; it’s a place that was meant to endure.
