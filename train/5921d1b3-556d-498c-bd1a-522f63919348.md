The discovery of **highly efficient quantum error correction codes** with **threshold theorems** revealing that arbitrary computation can be performed with fault tolerance below a critical error rate (~1% per gate) raises profound questions about the fundamental limits of quantum computation. This challenges classical notions of error suppression, where redundancy often requires exponential overhead.

Key unresolved queries:
- **What are the physical constraints on gate errors?** (e.g., decoherence, noise spectra, or material imperfections)
- **How does error propagation scale with system size?** (e.g., does error growth follow a non-linear path?)
- **What are the minimal resources needed to construct a robust quantum computer?** (e.g., topological vs. gate-based architectures)
- **Are there quantum phenomena (e.g., non-Markovian noise, emergent symmetries) that could bypass current error-correction assumptions?**

The paradox of **"fewer errors enabling more computation"** forces a reevaluation of both theoretical frameworks (e.g., the **Church-Turing boundary**) and practical implementations (e.g., **quantum supremacy benchmarks**). Could this indicate a **hidden efficiency in quantum systems** beyond classical intuition?
