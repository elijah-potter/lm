Wintersvain was a village that you wouldn't find if you weren't looking for it, and it was supposed to be that way. The people were cold, just like the weather. With the weight of the village's secrets, it was easier to be quiet and bear your burdens alone.

Visitors were few and seldom; they rarely returned. Inevitably, some vacationers who had decided to forgo the traditional luxuries of hotels and vacation planners in favor of saving a few pennies would drive through the narrow pass into Wintersvain. 

The car would lurch and sputter, inevitably giving out just in front of the only inn available. It was the home of Mr. Crink, who had opened it up to visitors to supplement his income as a butcher. If the idea of raw meat and clean linens sits uncomfortably with you, then you might well understand why some visitors would risk the 3-hour drive to get to the nearest vestige of civilization rather than staying there. 

Still, the village had charm, if only in the quaint, ghost town kind of way. It might have been more comforting if the village were completely abandoned; that way, visitors wouldn't have to reckon with the unsettling fact that people chose to live there. Houses were sprawled unevenly along the snow banks, an apparent indicator that there had been no housing planner.

The people weren't happy about living in Wintersvain, but they underwent what they must. The world could not handle its secrets—at least, not if the status quo was to be maintained; however, all of that would change when Ms. Gracy Corwell, a small-town journalist, pulled into the inn on a cold January night.
