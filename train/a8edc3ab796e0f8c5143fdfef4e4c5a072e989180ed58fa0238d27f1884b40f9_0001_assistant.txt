Between its rocky mountain faces and thorny forests, few people had much interest in Chenton Island. Few people, that is, except for the island’s inhabitants. The locals knew that hidden between stands of treacherous forest were fertile plains perfect for rice and amaranth. They knew that secreted around the steep rock faces were jumping penguin nests full of delicious eggs. And they knew it was worth keeping quiet about their home’s natural wonders.

And so, though sizable and centrally located in the Weeping Sea, none of the surrounding kingdoms had ever bothered trying to conquer Chenton. It also didn’t hurt that the island’s only natural harbor was nestled deep in Lamar Bay. Its narrow entrance was hard to navigate without previous experience but easy for even the nation’s irregular militia to defend. 

The island produced no gold or gemstones. Instead, Chenton society was known for its shipwrights. Chentonian thornwood was no stronger than mainland hardwoods, but the artisans themselves were a step above the rest. Kings and nobles had fawned over Chentonian schooners for centuries, and they paid handsomely to fly their flags on thornwood masts. Indeed, it wasn’t unusual for the flagships of both sides to have the same Chentonian shipwright’s name on their keels.
