In the quiet hum of the morning, the city slept like a beast half-asleep. I sat on the rooftop, coffee cup half-empty, watching the sunrise paint the sky in streaks of gold and crimson. The air smelled of damp earth and distant rain.

Then I saw it—the pattern.

It started with the pigeons. Every morning, at nearly the same hour, they would gather in a loose formation, their feathers ruffled in the cold wind. They didn’t fly south; they stayed. They didn’t fight; they waited. And then—disciplined as soldiers—they would fan out, each one taking its own path, returning at dusk with the same unspoken rhythm.

Next came the streetlamps. Their flicker was erratic, but not random. They blinked in a slow, predictable cycle—on for a set time, off for a set time—like a heartbeat. The city’s pulse, I thought, though I knew better than to attribute meaning to such things.

Then the cars. The same model, same route, same speed limit—though no one enforced it. They moved in perfect, invisible choreography, never cutting each other off, never swerving, just obeying an unseen hand. The traffic, like a living thing, moved as one.

And then the shadows.

On the building across the street, the pattern was most obvious. The sun moved in arcs, but the shadows stretched and shrank in a rhythm so precise they could be counted. Day by day, I marked them. The length of the long shadow, the angle of the short one. They didn’t just move—they *waited*.

I traced it with a finger, watching the numbers on my phone’s clock rise and fall. The city was a machine, and I was its mechanic. The pattern was everywhere. In the way the trees leaned, in the way the birds sang, in the way the wind sighed through the wires.

I exhaled, watching the smoke curl from my coffee cup. The pattern was the only thing that felt real. The rest was noise. The rest was noise.
