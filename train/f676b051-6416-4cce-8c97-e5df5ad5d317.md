The morning light filtered through the cracks in the blinds, painting the room in golden streaks that never quite matched the same spot twice. The narrator had spent years counting them—not the ones that slipped through, but the ones that *flickered*, as if caught in a moment of hesitation between day and night. Each morning, the same sequence: a brief pause, then a shift, a new pattern forming.

The first time they noticed, it was a Tuesday. A Tuesday with a specific temperature, the kind that made the air feel like it had a memory of its own. The second time, it was a Thursday. A Thursday with a name on the calendar that rhymed with "pattern." By the third week, the narrator had memorized the days—some were slow, others rushed—but all followed a rhythm they couldn’t explain.

Then came the weekends. No pattern there, just the weight of silence between the days that had already begun to repeat. The narrator started writing them down, but the numbers blurred into shapes on the page, like stars in the dark. One evening, after the seventh observation, they realized: the days were *fading*. Not disappearing, but thinning, as if the light itself was learning to ignore them.

The pattern wasn’t in the days. It was in the *missing*. The things that had always been there—like the way the streetlamps flickered in unison with the wind, or how the clock on the wall always stopped at twelve when no one was looking—had begun to dissolve. The narrator traced their fingers over the calendar, where the days now stood like ghosts, half-formed, half-erased.

They didn’t know if they were losing time or time was losing them. But one thing was certain: the pattern wasn’t breaking. It was *growing*. And the narrator had never felt so alone, so connected.
