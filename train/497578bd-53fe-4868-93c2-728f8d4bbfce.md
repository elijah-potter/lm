The morning fog curled around the old church steps, thick as a forgotten memory. I wiped the rain from my spectacles and noticed the peeling paint on the doorframe—one of the last remnants of the war’s neglect. The priest had called it a "sacred ruin" years ago, but no one else seemed to notice the cracks in the foundation. The bells still tolled every hour, though no one came to hear them.

I sat on the bench outside, staring at the church’s stained-glass windows—one still intact, though faded, depicting a saint holding a broken cup. The others were gone. I remembered the day the priest told me the story behind it: a child’s glass shattered during a sermon, and the congregation had no way to replace it. The cup was never fixed.

I exhaled through my nose, watching the steam dissolve into the damp air. The confession I’d been holding was simple: *I was the one who broke the cup.* Not in anger, but in quiet, useless frustration. The glass had been brittle, and when I reached for it, my fingers had slipped. I’d tried to fix it, but the pieces didn’t fit. The priest never knew.

A gust of wind rattled the stained glass, and for a moment, the saints’ faces blurred—then sharpened again, as if they’d always been watching. I picked up the cup from the ground beside me, its edges jagged. It was heavier than I remembered. The priest would never understand why I’d kept it. Maybe it was just another thing to bury.
