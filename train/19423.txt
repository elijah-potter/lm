What Is ADHD? Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder Overview
What is ADHD?
Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), also known as hyperkinetic disorder and attention deficit disorder (ADD), is a disease characteristic of children and consists of a range of problem behaviors characterized by a persistent pattern of loss of attention and/or hyperactivity. Along with hyperactivity, they can also include high impulsivity, agitation, in addition to inattention, and often prevent children from learning and relating to other children or adults appropriately.
Who is affected by ADHD?
It is estimated that between 1-5% of school-age children suffer from ADHD, ADD or hyperkinetic disorder, being 3 to 5 times more frequent in boys than in girls.
What are the symptoms of ADHD?
According to the World Health Organization, the criteria for diagnosis are grouped into three large groups of symptoms. Although the two main groups of symptoms are hyperactivity and attention deficit, there is a third group of symptoms, impulsivity, which must be taken into account. Symptoms must have persisted for at least six months and should not be explainable and customary for the child’s age or intelligence level.
Sometimes problems do not show up at home, but they can be very evident when the child goes to school. This can happen when parents do not realize that their child’s behavior is out of the norm (perhaps because they have not had more children, or have other children who behave similarly). It may also be because the problems are minor or because the family has treated inattention at home in such a way that there is no evidence that it is a serious problem or because the child is very young. In these cases, it is quite reasonable that the parents do not consider that their child has a problem with attention deficit.

What other disorders should be ruled out?
First of all, keep in mind that many children can be very active or easily distracted, or have difficulty concentrating. If these behaviors are relatively mild, they should not be considered an alteration.
Before diagnosing ADHD, ADD or hyperkinetic disorder, it must be ruled out that the child has any of the following symptoms:
- Epileptic seizures or seizures that can cause a child to fall asleep, affecting their attention. Epilepsy can also cause unusual behavior and lead to abnormal perceptions.
- Hearing problems such as deafness or repeated ear infections that can make it difficult for the child to follow the instructions and appear inattentive.
- Reading problems that make it difficult for them to complete tasks or follow instructions.
- Obsessive-compulsive disorders lead people to follow strange rituals that occupy their thoughts and distract their attention.
- Gilles de la Tourette’s disease that involves repetitive involuntary jerking movements of the body, along with sudden noises or swears words.
- Autism and Asperger syndrome as this leads to difficulties in the understanding and use of language, along with alterations in their social functioning.
- Prolonged periods of insufficient sleep, causing poor concentration.
- Depressive episodes.
What other difficulties can arise along with ADHD?
ADHD often appears to accompany other difficulties and is not the only cause of behavioral and behavioral disturbances. Children may show tantrums, sleep disturbances, and clumsiness. Other behavioral problems that occur with ADHD are:
- Deliberate oppositional behaviors. The child loses his temper, argues and refuses to obey adults, and deliberately annoys others.
- Behavioral disturbances that occur in at least 25% of children. The child can be destructive or display false behavior, such as repeatedly telling lies, breaking the rules, or stealing.
- Specific learning disorders, such as dyslexia, that occur in 25-30% of children.
- Severe clinical depression.
- Anxiety disorders.
What are the causes of ADHD?
The causes of ADHD, ADD, or hyperkinetic disorder are not known. It usually arises from a combination of psychological, biological, and environmental factors.
- The child’s temperament, as this influences his attitude and personality.
- Twin studies suggest there is a genetic link to ADHD. In 80 to 90% of identical twins in which one has ADHD the other also has it. Recent research suggests that there is a high probability of inheriting it from male relatives, such as grandparents and uncles.
- Brain injuries due to birth trauma or prenatal problems. Brain structures that were believed to be linked to the development of ADHD are vulnerable to hypoxic injury during birth. The injury is caused by an insufficient amount of oxygen reaching parts of the brain while reducing blood flow.
- Family stress
- Educational difficulties
How is ADHD diagnosed?
ADHD requires a medical diagnosis made by a doctor, usually a psychiatrist or pediatrician. It will often be appropriate for other professionals such as psychologists, speech therapists, and teachers to contribute their observations to the evaluation of the child with possible ADHD.
There is no single diagnostic test for ADHD, so all kinds of information should be collected (1), such as the following:
- History of symptoms: the exact nature of the difficulties, when they were first noticed, in what situation they occurred, factors that aggravated or alleviated it.
- Medical history: Risk factors that could predispose a child to ADHD include difficulties and risks during pregnancy and childbirth, for example, if the mother had had health problems, was very young, or drank alcohol and smoked. This is important since there are serious medical conditions associated with ADHD such as fragile X chromosome syndrome, fetal alcohol syndrome, phenylketonuria, or thyroid hormone abnormalities. They must be taken into account: accidents, operations and chronic medical conditions such as epilepsy, asthma and cardiac, liver and kidney disorders. Any medication the child takes may also be relevant, as well as any adverse reactions they have had to medications in the past.
- Past psychiatric history: Asking about a child’s mental health problems can help rule out depression or anxiety after symptoms.
- Educational history: this indicates the level of their ability and specific difficulties they have, how they work in their group, how they get along with teachers and any behavioral difficulties such as suspensions or exclusions. A more detailed evaluation of the child’s learning by a psychologist may be necessary.
- Assessment of a child’s temperament and personality: It may be necessary to assess the child’s temperament and personality, those of other family members, and the nature of their relationships within the family. This will include a discussion of the methods used by parents to manage the child’s behavior and the success achieved. Although this seems indiscreet, the counselor will remain neutral and parents should not feel that the disorder is “their fault.”
- Family history: The mental and physical health of the child’s parents and other family members may be relevant, especially about the incidence of ADHD or depression.
- Social assessment: Family social conditions such as home, poverty, and social support can have an impact on the child’s behavior.
What is the treatment for these disorders?
Treatment will depend on the exact diagnosis of the child and what the salient symptoms are. You should be aware of your specific difficulties and strengths that can help you improve. Medication alone is often not enough to treat all needs with ADHD, ADD, or hyperkinetic disorder, but often needs to be combined with targeted psychological treatments to help modify a child’s behavior, support parents, and improve school performance.
Management techniques for parents and teachers:
Although it is the child who suffers the most, it is not easy to live with a child with this type of disorder. Both parents and teachers are fundamental elements in treatment and must establish strategies for behavior modification, with a structured regime of punishments and rewards, always with the support and advice of a specialist, be it a psychiatrist, a psychologist or a child therapist. Parents must understand that excessive permissiveness will not help their children and in any case will prevent them from learning to be responsible for their actions. Therefore, it is not beneficial to completely exempt these children from the tasks, rules, and responsibilities that apply to other children.
Some of the following strategies can be very useful:
- Create a daily routine for the child; for example, homework programs, bedtime and eating routines.
- Be specific and concrete in the instructions to the child and ask for clear and reasonable things; for example, instead of telling the child to behave, suggest that he play quietly with a certain toy for the next 10 minutes.
- Establish clear and easy to understand rules; for example, how much television can they watch, and that certain behaviors are unacceptable.
- Be consistent in the treatment and handling of the child.
- Remove from your environment those elements that may distract you from your daily routine. For example, removing toys from the room when doing homework or turning off the TV.
- Plan, with the help of a professional, structured program designed to gradually increase the child’s concentration and ability to focus on tasks.
- If there is more than one child in the home, communicate with each one one at a time and avoid addressing multiple children at the same time.
- Use rewards (such as goodies, vouchers, or even money) consistently and frequently to reinforce appropriate behavior such as listening to adults, doing homework, finishing food without getting up, etc.
- Completely ignore the child or use a series of sanctions (such as not playing on the video console, being sent to his room, or not watching TV) when his behavior is unacceptable or he has exceeded limits. The objective is that the child gradually learns to recognize which of his behaviors are not only not acceptable, but they will also get him into trouble. Under no circumstances should you resort to physical punishment.
- It is essential to maintain good communication and collaboration with the school or nursery.
While as mentioned above, medication is often not enough, in many cases it is necessary for the treatment of these disorders. When they are taking medication, it must be clarified that it is simply one more help so that they can function and fit better in school, with other children and with the elderly. Stigmas and misconceptions need to be addressed and quickly removed (like if I take these pills it’s because I’m crazy).
The drugs most commonly used in the treatment of ADHD are stimulants of the central nervous system, such as some derivatives of amphetamines, the most widely used drug being Methylphenidate (Rubifen). These drugs are effective in reducing hyperactivity and impulsivity and help improve the child’s attention and concentration. These drugs, which should not be used in children under 6 years of age, have some important side effects and must be administered with close medical supervision.
Other drugs that have been used successfully in the treatment of these disorders are antidepressants such as imipramine, fluoxetine, or other serotonin reuptake inhibitor drugs, or bupropion.
Behavior modification techniques, such as those mentioned above, are essential in the treatment of ADHD, ADD or hyperkinetic disorder, and for mild attention deficit problems, they are the treatment of choice. Other psychological treatment modalities that may be helpful include targeted therapies for decreased anxiety, therapies for improved social skills, and certain group therapies. Likewise, it is also necessary to support individual or group learning, aimed at improving achievement and school integration.
What is the prognosis?
The course and evolution of symptoms are highly variable. Symptoms may persist through adolescence and adulthood, disappear during puberty, or there may be a partial remission so that hyperactivity disappears but attention deficits and impulse control problems persist. Approximately half of those affected present a complete remission of symptoms upon reaching puberty, this being very infrequent before the age of 12 years. However, a significant number of patients will experience problems that will persist in their adult lives. In fact, in 15-20% of cases, the symptoms persist in adolescence and adulthood. In this case, it is often accompanied by other disorders such as the presence of depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, abuse of alcohol or other drugs, or antisocial behaviors.
