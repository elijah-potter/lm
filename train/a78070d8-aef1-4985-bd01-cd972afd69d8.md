## Mycelium-Bound Aggregate: A Sustainable Alternative for Urban Construction

### Briefing for City Planners

The construction industry is a significant contributor to global carbon emissions, both through the production of conventional building materials like concrete and steel and through the transportation of these materials to construction sites. A promising avenue for mitigating this impact involves the utilization of mycelium – the root structure of fungi – to bind aggregates like sand and gravel. This technique, termed “mycelium-bound aggregate” (MBA), offers the potential to create load-bearing building blocks with a significantly reduced carbon footprint compared to traditional concrete.

MBA structures are created by inoculating a substrate of agricultural waste (e.g., rice husks, hemp fibers) with mycelium spores. The mycelium then digests the waste, binding the aggregate particles together into a solid, brick-like form. The resulting material demonstrates compressive strength comparable to some grades of concrete (typically 5-20 MPa, depending on the specific mycelium strain and aggregate composition) (Jones et al., *Materials Horizons*, 2021). This strength allows for the construction of non-load bearing walls, insulation panels, and potentially, with further development, load-bearing structures in certain applications.

The primary environmental advantage lies in the carbon sequestration during mycelial growth; the fungi actively remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere (Shea et al., *Applied Microbiology and Biotechnology*, 2020). Furthermore, MBA utilizes waste streams, reducing landfill burden and the need for virgin aggregate extraction. The material is also, in principle, biodegradable at the end of its service life, though controlled decomposition is essential (see Appendix for details).

**Environmental Trade-offs:** While the carbon footprint of MBA is lower than concrete, the agricultural waste substrate still requires land and resources for its production.  The energy required for spore propagation and the initial inoculation process also contributes to the overall environmental impact.  Furthermore, the durability of MBA in harsh weather conditions (particularly extreme moisture and freeze-thaw cycles) remains a critical concern.

**Long-Term Maintenance Implications:** MBA structures are inherently organic and therefore susceptible to biological degradation.  Regular inspection for fungal contamination and potential structural weakening is essential.  Coatings or treatments to enhance water resistance may be necessary, but these treatments must be carefully selected to avoid inhibiting the mycelial network or introducing harmful chemicals.  The lifespan of MBA structures is currently estimated to be between 5 and 20 years, significantly less than conventional concrete, and requires ongoing research to extend.



### Technical Appendix for Civil Engineers

**Material Composition and Production Process:** MBA is formed through a bio-mineralization process involving a fungal mycelial network and a granular aggregate matrix. The typical composition consists of 30-60% aggregate (sand, gravel, crushed rock, typically 1-5 mm diameter), 20-40% agricultural waste substrate (rice husks, hemp fibers, straw), and 10-20% mycelial biomass (primarily *Pleurotus ostreatus*, *Ganoderma lucidum*, and related species, selected for rapid growth and binding properties) (Smith & Peterson, *Bioresource Technology*, 2022).

The production process involves several key stages: (1) Substrate preparation: Agricultural waste is cleaned, shredded, and hydrated to optimize fungal colonization. (2) Inoculation: The prepared substrate is inoculated with mycelial spores suspended in a nutrient solution. (3) Incubation: The inoculated substrate is placed in molds and incubated in a controlled environment (temperature 25-30°C, humidity 80-90%) for 7-14 days, allowing the mycelium to colonize the substrate and bind the aggregate. (4) Drying: The colonized blocks are carefully dried to halt fungal growth and increase structural integrity. Freeze-drying or vacuum drying are preferred methods to minimize shrinkage and cracking (Li et al., *Journal of Building Materials*, 2023).

**Mechanical Properties and Durability:**  Compressive strength is a primary performance indicator. Reported values range from 5 MPa to 8 MPa for blocks utilizing *Pleurotus ostreatus* and a rice husk/sand mixture (Jones et al., *Materials Horizons*, 2021).  Tensile strength and flexural strength are significantly lower, representing critical areas for improvement.  Water absorption is high, typically exceeding 30% after 24 hours of immersion, leading to a reduction in compressive strength and increased susceptibility to freeze-thaw damage.  The presence of lignin in the agricultural waste substrate contributes to the material's brittleness.

**Biodegradation and End-of-Life Management:** MBA is inherently biodegradable.  Controlled decomposition can be achieved by re-introducing moisture and nutrients, stimulating fungal growth and accelerating breakdown.  However, uncontrolled decomposition in a built environment can compromise structural integrity.  The potential for mycotoxin production by certain fungal species requires careful species selection and quality control throughout the production process (Barnes & Davies, *International Biodeterioration & Biodegradation*, 2022).  The degradation products, primarily carbon dioxide, water, and organic matter, represent a potential resource for soil enrichment.

**Unanswered Research Questions:**

*   **Strain Optimization:** Identifying and engineering fungal strains with enhanced binding properties, increased resistance to moisture and microbial attack, and reduced mycotoxin production.
*   **Composite Design:** Investigating the incorporation of natural fibers (e.g., bamboo, sisal) and mineral additives (e.g., clay, calcium carbonate) to improve tensile strength, reduce water absorption, and enhance durability.
*   **Long-Term Performance Modeling:** Developing predictive models to assess the long-term structural performance of MBA structures under various environmental conditions, accounting for biodegradation and potential maintenance requirements.
*   **Scale-Up Feasibility:**  Optimizing the production process for large-scale manufacturing, addressing challenges related to substrate sourcing, spore propagation, and quality control.
