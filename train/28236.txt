Nancy Folbre is an economics professor at the University of Massachusetts Amherst.
Perhaps the topic seems more personal than political. But current public policies in the United States reinforce painful inequalities in access to mothers’ milk.
Poor African-American infants breast-feed less, and for shorter periods, than other infants in the United States, with negative consequences for their health and future success.
Both the World Health Organization and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend that infants be exclusively breast-fed for the first six months of life — an experience that only about 12 percent of infants in the United States currently enjoy.
The percentage of infants ever breast-fed is higher, at 74 percent, but varies considerably by family income and race.
A 2004 survey analyzed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention found that the proportion of children ever breast-fed was 23 to 26 percentage points higher in the top income group compared to those of the same race in the lowest income group. The proportion of African-American children ever breast-fed was 10 to 17 percentage points lower than that of white children in the same income group.
Why are these numbers so important?
Considerable evidence shows that breast-feeding improves both infants’ and mothers’ health. The long-run benefits add up to major savings in total health care expenditures.
These benefits don’t come without costs to individual mothers. Breast-feeding requires considerable time and effort, and often limits ability to earn income. The injunction to breast-feed for as long as possible contributes to the ratcheting up of supermom expectations.
