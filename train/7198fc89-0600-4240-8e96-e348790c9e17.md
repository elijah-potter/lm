**The Last Light**

The city’s breath was a slow unraveling—neon veins bleeding into the sky like a dying star. I stood at the edge of the park, where the cobblestones cracked like old bones underfoot. The air smelled of damp earth and rust, the kind of smell that clings to places where time forgot to walk. A single streetlamp flickered, its bulb a dying ember, casting long shadows that twisted into shapes no one remembered.

I was looking for her name.

Not the one in the phone book, not the one scribbled on napkins at diners, but the one she carved into her own skin—*V*, a single, jagged line that had never quite healed. The tattoos on her arms were maps, each one a story of places she’d been lost and found again. I traced one with my thumb, the ink still dark against her pale skin. *"Where do you think you’re going?"* she’d asked once, her voice rough as gravel. I’d said, *"Everywhere."*

The park was empty now, save for the pigeons—familiar, relentless, as if they knew better than to let the world’s end go unnoticed. I bent to pick up a half-eaten ice cream cone, the paper wrapper still damp with condensation. The last crumbs were gone, but the sugar had stained my fingers. I pressed them to my lips, tasting something sweet and bitter, like the first sip of poison.

Then I saw it.

The photograph was tucked between the pages of an old notebook, its corners yellowed with age. It was a snapshot—blurry, but sharp enough to remember: a woman, standing in a field of wildflowers, her hair tangled in the wind, her smile wide and unguarded. She was holding a camera, her fingers steady as she snapped a shot of something beyond the frame—something unseen. The image was grainy, the colors faded, but there was a certainty in her posture, a quiet defiance.

I held the photo up to the light.

The moment I did, the photograph *moved*.

The background wasn’t a field anymore. It was a void, black and endless, stretching into infinity. But in the center of the frame, the woman was still there—she stood on a cliff, her arms outstretched as if embracing the sky. Behind her, something glowed. Not a light, not a star—*the image itself was alive*, pulsing with a soft, golden light. It wasn’t a reflection. It was her.

And then, for a single, breathless second, the photograph *blurred*.

Not from movement. From *transformation*.

The woman was no longer holding a camera. She was holding *herself*—the edges of her form dissolved into light, and for a heartbeat, she was everywhere at once. The camera was gone. The field was gone. Only she remained, suspended between worlds, a bridge between two realities.

The photo held her name in the air.
