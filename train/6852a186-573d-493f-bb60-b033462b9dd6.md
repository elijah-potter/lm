A sprawling urban park, its green spaces overgrown with wildflowers and ivy, where fences have long since rotted or been replaced by makeshift metal gates. Children play in an abandoned playground whose swings creak ominously, their ropes frayed. The air smells of damp earth and distant rain, with the occasional scent of fried food drifting from a food cart that once served tourists but now attracts only stray cats and the occasional homeless person. A makeshift bridge over a canal, its planks patched with duct tape and rust, connects two halves of the park that used to be a single, grand promenade. Streetlights flicker intermittently, their bulbs either broken or powered by unreliable generators. In the distance, a construction crane hums, its blades spinning slowly as it circles over a site that was once a shopping mall but now stands half-collapsed, its glass facades boarded up. The whole place feels like it’s holding together by sheer will—until the next storm or the next wave of neglect.
