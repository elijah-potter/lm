Until recently, scant information had been available on how exercise benefits older women. Now, two studies have shown it can decrease their risk of heart disease and strengthen their bones.
As women enter menopause, their likelihood of developing heart disease increases sharply. But a University of Pittsburgh study shows that modest exercise can help ward off some of the biological changes that put women at risk.
At the start of the study, 541 healthy women age 42 to 50 underwent intensive physical exams and filled out questionnaires that assessed their physical activity and stress levels. Three years later, they were evaluated again.
Women who had increased their physical activity reaped measurable benefits. They put on the least weight-important because excess weight increases the chance of developing heart disease-and they had the smallest declines in high-density lipoproteins, the so-called ``good`` cholesterol that appears to lower heart-disease risk.
Another bonus for the active women: They had the smallest increases in symptoms of depression and stress. The activity boost that yielded those benefits was equivalent to walking 20 minutes three times a week.
The second study, at Family Health International in Durham, N.C., involved 352 healthy white women age 40 to 54. For three days, they each wore a portable ``personal activity computer`` that assessed their activity levels by estimating how many kilocalories they burned. The most active women had significantly greater bone density in their spinal vertebrae and forearms than their less active counterparts, a benefit that helps protect women against fractures that often occur after menopause.
``Previous studies have looked at the effect of exercise on bone density among trained athletes,`` says study co-author Paul Feldblum. ``Our study shows that the same benefits of exercise apply to women who engage in more moderate levels of physical activity.``
