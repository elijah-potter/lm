Tulip poplar (Liriodendron tulipifera) belongs neither to the tulip nor the poplar family but to the magnolia family. A pyramidal deciduous shade tree reaching 60 to 80 feet tall, it survives winters in U.S. Department of Agriculture hardiness zones 5a through 9a. Tulip poplars resist most pests, leaving only four to cause worry.
Tuliptree scales attach to branches and twigs, sucking the sap from the tree. This particular scale is a soft, oval insect with a waxy protective covering, reaching about 5 to 10 millimeters in length and ranging in color from gray to yellow, orange, pink and black. If you look at the branch of an infested tulip poplar, the bark will appear knotted or wart-ridden. In large infestations, the scales can reduce the vigor of the tree, causing dieback, yellowing of the leaves and premature leaf fall. Even in small infestations, however, the scale can cause problems with the sticky honeydew they excrete while feeding that attracts ants and sooty black mold.
Keep the tulip poplar properly fertilized and watered, as the tuliptree aphids usually infest weak trees. These insects have natural predators in ladybird beetles, predatory mites and parasitic wasps that in some years control the population. You can try to preserve these predators and decrease your pest population by applying horticultural oil or insecticidal soap. If you have a large problem, apply a pesticide containing acephate, carbaryl, imidacloprid, malathion or permethrin in late summer or early fall when the pest is in its crawler stage.
Tuliptree aphid is a small, light green to pink insect that feeds on the underside of tulip poplar tree leaves. In small infestations the leaves simply turn yellow, while in larger infestations the leaves can turn brown and fall off the tree. This aphid excretes large amounts of honeydew, making a sticky mess of whatever sits below the tree. The honeydew can then attract bees and wasps to the area as well as sooty mold.
Again, keep the tree healthy with proper water and fertilizer amounts for your soil and environment to help it resist these pests. If you catch the problem early, you may be able to rinse these insects off the tree with periodic blasts of water to the leaves. If that doesn't work, apply an insecticidal soap to the leaves, which will help decrease this pest but keep its predators alive.
Yellow Poplar Weevil
The yellow poplar weevil is a ticklike insect with wings, measuring about 3/16 of an inch. The adult females inject eggs into the leaves, which then hatch and feed on the surrounding tissue, creating a mine that turns brown. The adults feed on the leaves, leaving bean-shaped holes. Although unsightly, in most cases these pests will not cause much damage to tulip poplar trees.
To eliminate this pest, apply a pesticide containing acephate or carbaryl as soon as you see the bean-shaped feeding holes on the leaves. This will target the adults, interrupting their breeding cycles.
A less prevalent tulip poplar pest is the mealybug, a small, elongated and segmented gray insect with white filaments radiating from its body. These bugs also feed on the sap of the plant and excrete honeydew. They often gather in large numbers on a branch, appearing as a cottony bunch and causing dieback, leaf yellowing and premature leaf drop on certain branches.
Because of their armorlike segmented covering, mealybugs are difficult to control with insecticides. Insecticidal soaps, horticultural oil and blasts of water can reduce populations but must be employed consistently to have any effect.
