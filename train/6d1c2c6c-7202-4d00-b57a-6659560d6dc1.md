## TIL the "Marquise Ring" Test Originated to Detect Poisoning in Noblewomen

Today I learned about a peculiar and historically significant practice known as the “Marquise Ring” test, used primarily in 18th and 19th century Europe. The test, named for its shape, wasn’t a ring in the jewelry sense, but rather a small, bell-shaped silver vessel used to detect arsenic poisoning.

During this period, arsenic was readily available and, unfortunately, sometimes used to intentionally poison rivals or enemies. Because arsenic often tasted odorless and tasteless when mixed into food, it was exceedingly difficult to detect. Suspicion often fell on household servants, and proving guilt was nearly impossible.

The Marquise Ring test arose as a solution. A small portion of the suspect food was placed inside the silver vessel, and then heated. If arsenic was present, it would react with the silver, creating a distinctive black sulfide deposit on the inner surface of the ring. This visible discoloration served as evidence of poisoning, providing a crucial tool for investigators.

What’s particularly fascinating is the ingenuity of the method and the social context it reflects – a time when suspicion and intrigue were rife amongst the aristocracy, and a simple, albeit imperfect, scientific test could offer a semblance of justice.
