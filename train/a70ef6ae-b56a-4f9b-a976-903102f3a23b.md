## TIL the "Phantom Islands" of Cartography

Today I learned about a fascinating and rather humbling chapter in the history of mapmaking: the prevalence of "phantom islands." For centuries, cartographers included islands on maps that simply did not, and do not, exist. These weren't the result of malicious fabrication, but rather stemmed from a combination of misinterpretation, wishful thinking, and the inherent limitations of early navigation.

The most famous example is Hy-Brasil, a mythical island appearing on maps from the 14th to 19th centuries, purportedly located west of Ireland. It was often depicted as a circular island, shrouded in mist, and said to appear only once every seven years. Numerous explorers set out to find it, all returning empty-handed. Other phantom islands, such as Buss Island and Frisland, also appeared and disappeared from maps, their existence fueled by sailors' tales and the desire to fill in the vast, unexplored regions of the Atlantic.

The reasons for these cartographic errors are varied. Sometimes, sailors would report land based on mirages or unusual cloud formations. Others might embellish stories to gain prestige or funding for future voyages. Cartographers, lacking accurate surveying techniques, would incorporate these reports into their maps, perpetuating the myth. The disappearance of these islands from modern maps highlights the evolution of navigational tools and the rigorous verification processes now in place, reminding us that even the most seemingly authoritative sources can be subject to error.
