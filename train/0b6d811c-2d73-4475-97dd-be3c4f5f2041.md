In the quiet hours before dawn, a man sat on a cracked wooden bench, watching the city sleep. It was not a place for grand gestures—just the steady drip of coffee into a thermos, the slow unspooling of a newspaper, the way the streetlamps flickered like dying stars.

The weather? Mild. The day? Unremarkable. The only constant was the hum of the subway tracks beneath his feet, a faint, unending rhythm like the breath of some forgotten machine.

His pocket held a single coin, its edge worn smooth by years of handling. It wasn’t much, but it was all he had left after the things that mattered—love, time, the weight of choices—had been stripped away like leaves in autumn.

He lit a cigarette with the trembling fingers of someone who had long since stopped believing in miracles. The smoke curled into the air like a confession, thin and unconvincing. Outside, the distant wail of a siren, another reminder that the world kept turning, indifferent to the weight of his existence.

No one noticed. No one would. The city was too busy being itself.
