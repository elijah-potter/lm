A sleepless night in a cozy motel room by the highway, the hum of the engine outside the window, the scent of coffee and distant rain. The morning sun found me at the edge of a small town where the main street was lined with boarded-up shops and a single diner that smelled of fried eggs and old leather. The woman at the counter, her hands calloused, poured me a cup of black coffee and said, *"You look like you need a ride."* I didn’t ask. She handed me a map, a folded newspaper, and a key to her car. *"No questions. Just drive."*

We left the town behind and drove through fields where the corn swayed like ghosts in the wind. She spoke little, but her eyes lingered on the horizon as if memorizing something. At a rest stop, she pulled over, lit a cigarette, and said, *"You ever think about where you’re really from?"* I didn’t. But that night, in the backseat of her old pickup, I didn’t sleep. I just sat and stared out the window, watching the stars blur through the windshield.

By the time we reached the next town, I had a suitcase, a train ticket, and a name scribbled on a napkin. She handed me the ticket and said, *"Take it."* The train pulled away, leaving her standing on the platform with a coat draped over her shoulders, watching until it disappeared into the distance. I didn’t look back. But for the first time, I understood what it meant to leave something behind—not just a place, but a question, a weight, a quiet certainty that I wasn’t who I thought I was anymore. The trip had changed me. Not in a way I could define, but in a way that made me feel lighter, even as my heart ached.
