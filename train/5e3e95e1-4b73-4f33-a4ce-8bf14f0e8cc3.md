Returned to the old kitchen, its wooden cabinets creaking under the weight of half-empty shelves. The scent of aged spices lingers—cardamom, cloves—mixed with the faint rust of forgotten utensils. The table, once polished to a gleam, now bears the faint scuff of years, its edges worn smooth. The walls hold the ghostly marks of old hands: a faded water stain near the sink, a cracked spot where the floor meets the baseboard. The garden fence, once a boundary of neat rows, is now overgrown with ivy and wildflowers, their blooms brushing against the rusted gate. The café outside, where I used to linger with a steaming cup, has a new sign: *"Café de l'Absence"*—though the name feels like a stranger's joke. The cobblestones underfoot are slick with morning dew, reflecting the muted light of a city that hasn’t changed much, but time has.
