A sprawling, half-abandoned marketplace in a forgotten district of a megacity, its grand arches now crumbling into moss and ivy. The air hums with the faint, rhythmic pulse of unseen machinery beneath the cracked pavement, though no one knows why it runs. Thousands arrive each evening—some by choice, others dragged in by the sound of distant music or the scent of burning incense that lingers even after the winds shift.

Stalls are scattered haphazardly, each offering the same: a single, identical object—mirror-finished bottles with no liquid inside, or wooden spoons that hum when touched. The crowd moves in tight, unspoken formations, their eyes darting toward a central, always-empty stage where a single figure stands, their silhouette shifting like smoke. They speak in a language no one understands, but the words carry the weight of a warning or a promise. Some leave after a few hours; others stay for months, their faces blurred by the same flickering, unnatural light that casts long shadows across the cobblestones.

No one knows the rules—whether to follow the stranger, avoid the whispers, or if the whole place is a trap. The only constant is the slow, inexorable change: the walls bleed color, the mirrors reflect faces that aren’t there, and the crowd’s numbers grow without end. Some say it’s a test. Others say it’s a disease. A few dare to ask what happens when the stage goes dark. The answers come in dreams—or in the form of a single, identical object left on their doorsteps.
