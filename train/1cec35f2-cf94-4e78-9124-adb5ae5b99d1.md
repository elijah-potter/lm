The absence lingers like a half-remembered dream—its edges frayed, its presence more tangible than any tangible thing. There are hollows in the walls where warmth used to be, the scent of morning coffee now just a whisper of what it once was. Silence doesn’t quite fill the void; instead, it’s the absence of sound that makes the quiet so sharp. Mirrors show faces that aren’t quite the same, eyes that flicker with a memory of someone else’s gaze. Time moves differently when a person is gone: the days stretch thin, the nights feel heavier, each moment a careful pacing through a space that’s no longer quite right.

The body remembers what the mind forgets—knuckles that ache from gripping something no longer there, the way the mattress dips where a partner once lay. Clothes hang on hangers that were once draped with someone else’s scent. The absence is the space between the last goodbye and the first time you realize you’re alone. It’s not gone; it’s here, pressing against you like a second skin you never wanted to shed. Sometimes, you think you hear their voice, not in the usual way—more like a static hum, a ghost in the background, a whisper that fades before you can quite understand it. The absence is the thing that’s always there, always watching, always just out of reach. It’s the reason the days feel like they’re moving backward, and the nights feel like they’re stretching on forever.
