The late afternoon sun cast long shadows across the fourth-floor observation unit, painting the room in hues of amber and rose. My name is Eleanor Vance, and I am a registered nurse here at St. Jude’s. Today, my primary patient was Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, a retired clockmaker admitted two days prior following a fall at his home. He’s a man of quiet dignity, with hands that still tremble slightly despite his age, and eyes that hold a lifetime of meticulous work.

Mr. Penhaligon sustained a fractured hip, a particularly challenging injury given his age and the potential for complications. He's seventy-eight years old, fiercely independent, and understandably frustrated by his current immobility. Initially, he was quite resistant to assistance, attempting to sit up in bed without support and expressing a palpable annoyance at the need for help with even the simplest tasks. 

My approach has been to acknowledge his feelings and frustrations while firmly reinforcing the necessity of adhering to his care plan. I spent the morning reviewing his medication schedule, ensuring he understood the purpose of each drug and addressing any concerns he might have. He’s on a regimen of analgesics for pain management, a prophylactic anticoagulant to prevent deep vein thrombosis, and a stool softener to combat the constipation that often accompanies immobility and pain medication. 

During rounds with Dr. Ramirez, we discussed the possibility of initiating physical therapy. Mr. Penhaligon voiced his apprehension about the process, fearing he would be pushed too hard. Dr. Ramirez, with a reassuring smile, explained the importance of gradual strengthening and the role of the physical therapist in tailoring the exercises to his specific needs and comfort level. I reinforced this explanation, emphasizing that the goal was not to rush him but to help him regain his independence safely. 

This afternoon, I assisted him with his afternoon meal – a pureed chicken and vegetable soup, mashed potatoes, and applesauce. He ate with a slow, deliberate pace, commenting on the subtle differences in flavor compared to his usual fare. He reminisced about his late wife, Beatrice, who, he said, always made the best mashed potatoes. The sadness in his voice was palpable, but I simply offered a gentle, "I’m so sorry for your loss, Mr. Penhaligon." Sometimes, a simple acknowledgement is all that is needed.

Later, I checked his vital signs – blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiratory rate – all within acceptable parameters. I repositioned him in bed, ensuring his pressure points were relieved to prevent skin breakdown. As I prepared to leave for the end of my shift, I noticed a small, intricately carved wooden bird resting on his bedside table. 

“That’s quite lovely, Mr. Penhaligon,” I remarked.

He looked at it with a faint smile. “A robin,” he said. “Carved it myself, many years ago. Beatrice loved robins.”

I paused, understanding the significance of the object. “It’s beautiful,” I replied sincerely. “I hope you have a peaceful evening.”

As I closed the door behind me, I felt a quiet satisfaction. Nursing isn’t just about administering medications and monitoring vital signs; it’s about providing comfort, offering a listening ear, and recognizing the humanity in each patient, even amidst pain and uncertainty. Mr. Penhaligon’s recovery will be a process, but I believe that with careful attention, compassionate care, and a little patience, he will regain his strength and, perhaps, find a renewed sense of purpose.
