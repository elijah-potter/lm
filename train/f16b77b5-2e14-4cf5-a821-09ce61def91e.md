A weathered wooden porch, its slats splintered yet weathered by time, frames a small garden where ivy coils around an old, gnarled oak tree. The garden blooms with wildflowers—tulips fading to brown, daisies clinging to the edge of decay—yet each bloom seems to have been here forever, growing in cycles that repeat like a slow heartbeat. The house behind it is two stories, its shutters painted a faded blue, the paint peeling in places where rain has found its way. The foundation cracks subtly, like the skin of an aging person, but the walls hold firm, resisting collapse. The air smells of damp earth and old wood, a scent that is both transient and permanent, shifting with the seasons but never truly gone.

In the backyard, a rusted metal swing hangs from a branch, its chains bent but still sturdy. Children’s laughter, echoes of a past life, drift through the trees. The swing has been here so long it feels like part of the earth itself, yet its movement is still visible in the way the branches lean. There’s a bench beneath the oak, its seat worn smooth, the back broken in the middle, but no one sits there anymore. The place is full of ghosts—some lingering, others long gone—but the ground beneath it never stops.

At night, the porch light flickers weakly, casting long shadows that stretch like fingers across the garden. The light itself is a ghost—sometimes on, sometimes off—yet it never truly disappears. The house, the garden, the swing, the porch—each is temporary in its own way, but together, they form something that endures.
