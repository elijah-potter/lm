The earthquake that struck the San Francisco Bay area focused attention on what geologists consider their worst nightmare: a devastating earthquake, 25 to 30 times more powerful than Tuesday`s, that may hit San Francisco in the next 50 years, and an equally damaging one that could destroy much of Los Angeles at any time.
Scientists had believed that a major earthquake-one measuring about 8 on the Richter scale-was most probable in the Coachella Valley, south of Palm Springs, at the southern part of the San Andreas fault.
That was where they expected an earthquake to happen, even though San Francisco had defied the odds for 83 years.
``Tuesday`s earthquake was serious, but not very large,`` said Eldridge Moores, chairman of the geology department of the University of California at Davis. He said it was only about one-thirtieth of the size of the big ones that are expected-a size similar to that of the quake that struck San Francisco in 1906.
In the central region of the San Andreas fault, stress is being constantly relieved by smaller earthquakes and slippage of the fault boundaries, Moores said. But the southern section has been locked in place and under increasing pressure since 1857, he said.
``People tend to forget that a huge earthquake struck southern California that year,`` he noted. ``It hit Tejon Pass, north of Los Angeles on what`s now Interstate 5, where the highway crosses the fault. It was sparsely populated then, and also now. But the 1857 quake was just as powerful as the estimated 8.3 earthquake that later struck San Francisco.``
Miles underneath California, parts of the Earth`s rocky crust are inexorably creeping in opposite directions along the 600-mile San Andreas fault, a giant gash in the Earth that stretches southeast of San Francisco and Los Angeles to the Gulf of California.
For eons, in the slowest of motion, the rocks have been moving an average of 1.5 to 2.5 inches a year. But in other parts of the fault region, the rocks are no longer moving. They have inexplicably halted, and the strain is building up.
Sometimes, when the pressure becomes too great, the rocks slip over each other and complain in a shuddering spasm, as in Tuesday`s devastating 15-second tremor that struck the San Francisco area.
That relieved some strain for a while.
But eventually something else must give, geologists said Wednesday. The question is whether the pressure can be relieved by series of small tremors. If not, is something large and unforgettable in the offing?
Geologists were flocking to the San Andreas fault to analyze the presumed ground shift after the tremor, said Steve Bryant, seismic analyst at California Institute of Technology and the U.S. Geologic Survey.
``They haven`t reported back yet,`` Bryant said. ``It will take months before we fully analyze the data. The major things we`ll learn about this earthquake aren`t going to happen immediately.``
The epicenter of Tuesday`s earthquake was about 60 miles south of San Francisco in a section of the San Andreas fault that did not feel the full brunt of the 1906 earthquake, said Thomas Henyey, chairman of the department of geological sciences at the University of Southern California.
The epicenter of that historic quake was directly under the site of the Golden Gate Bridge, and geologists estimate that the earth slipped about 20 feet along the fault zone going through San Francisco.
However, the shift along the fault boundary south of the city was less than 10 feet, and it is now believed that Tuesday`s shift was an attempt to relieve the pressure that had remained since 1906, Henyey said.
It has not yet been determined how far the plates shifted in this quake.
While the shift, however far, may have relieved the strains in the fault in northern California, southern California is still facing a potentially devastating earthquake, he said.
``It`s been more than 130 years since the last big earthquake relieved the fault pressures in this region,`` Henyey said, ``and if anything, Tuesday`s earthquake could have added to the fault stress in southern California.``
Many lessons may be learned from Tuesday`s disaster, scientists said.
``What happened in San Francisco will be a good lesson as to what southern California can expect in the future,`` Henyey said. ``The nearest segment of the San Andreas fault is about the same distance from downtown Los Angeles as Tuesday`s earthquake was from downtown San Francisco.
``It`ll give us a good feel at least for what a 7 (on the Richter scale)
will do to Los Angeles.``
