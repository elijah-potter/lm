The fire crackled low in the hearth, casting long shadows across the rough-hewn walls of the old inn. Rain lashed the cobblestones outside, drumming a steady rhythm against the wooden shutters.

Old Man Harkin, his fingers wrapped around a mug of blackened ale, leaned forward as the wind howled through the room. "You ever wonder what’s beyond the next turn, boy?" he asked, his voice rough as gravel. "Or maybe what’s hiding in the stories we tell?"

The boy—no older than ten, with wide eyes and a face still damp from the rain—nodded. He’d been listening for hours, the way children do when they know the tale will be worth the wait.

Harkin took a slow sip, then cleared his throat. "Once there was a man who walked the same path every dawn. Not for gold or land, but because he swore he’d find what he’d lost. A door, carved deep into the cliffs where the river bends. The villagers said it was cursed, that whoever opened it never came back."

The boy’s breath hitched. "And what happened to him?"

Harkin chuckled darkly. "He opened it. And the night after, they found him standing in the village square, his hands wrapped in thorned vines. His eyes—empty. His voice—gone."

The boy shivered. "What was *inside*?"

Harkin’s gaze darkened. "A woman. Not like any you’ve seen. Her skin was silver, her hair spun from starlight. She spoke in whispers, but her voice was the wind itself. She offered him a choice: stay, and never age; or leave, and walk away forever. He chose the door."

The boy’s voice was small. "What did she say?"

Harkin’s grin was slow, cruel. "She said, ‘You’re already inside.’" The rain outside seemed to echo his words. Then, quieter: "And the man who walked home that night never spoke again."
