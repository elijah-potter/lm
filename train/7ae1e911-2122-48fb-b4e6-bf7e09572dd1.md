In the dim glow of a flickering streetlamp, Elias sat on the curb, his fingers tracing the grain of an old newspaper. The city pulsed around him—honking horns, distant laughter, the occasional siren—but none of it mattered anymore. The last letter from his sister had arrived yesterday, her scrawl crumpled in his pocket: *"You’re not the same person who wrote this."* His stomach twisted. He had always been the one who stayed, the one who built things, who fixed the world when it cracked. But lately, he felt like a ghost in his own life.

The rain came down harder, turning the sidewalk into a mirror. Elias watched his reflection—sharp, tired, and unrecognizable. He had tried to be strong, to keep the promises he’d made, but the weight of them was suffocating. The job he hated. The friends who drifted away. The silence that now wrapped around him like a shroud.

A car passed, its headlights cutting through the drizzle. Elias didn’t move. The world kept turning, but he had stopped. The letter in his pocket burned with something worse than grief—resignation. He stood, his shoes splashing through the puddles, and walked toward the distant hum of the train tracks. The last train to the station was due in twenty minutes. He didn’t care if he made it. He just wanted to disappear.

The platform was empty when he arrived. The train’s whistle pierced the air, but he didn’t look back. He stepped onto the platform, his boots thudding against the concrete, and boarded the train without a glance. The doors closed behind him. The city blurred into a distant, unrecognizable shape. He sat in the last seat, his hands clenched around a half-empty bottle of whiskey, and let the motion of the train carry him away.
