It wasn’t supposed to happen like this.

The clock struck midnight, but the city slept—except for the hum of distant sirens, the crackle of fire alarms, and the way the neon glow of a half-empty bar pulsed through the rain-streaked windows like a slow, dying heartbeat. Outside, the streets were slick with the aftermath of a storm, the usual rush of late-night pedestrians replaced by a eerie silence. She stood at the edge of the sidewalk, her breath fogging in the cold, her fingers curled around the worn leather of her coat. The coat had been her mother’s.

She had found it in the back of an old trunk, half-buried under forgotten boxes, the scent of lavender and mothballs clinging to the fabric. The trunk had been in the attic, the attic in a house that had been hers and her father’s for as long as she could remember. But it wasn’t hers anymore. Not really. Not after the divorce, not after the way he had looked at her the last time they’d spoken, like she was a ghost he’d already outgrown. The divorce papers had been signed months ago, but the weight of it still settled in her chest like a stone.

She should have been at home, packing up the last of her things. But she hadn’t. Instead, she had walked here, to the corner where the streetlight flickered and the pavement groaned under the weight of the rain. The bar across the street was half-empty, the bartender wiping down a glass with a rag that smelled of old cigarettes and cheap whiskey. She ordered a drink, her voice rough from disuse, and watched the bartender’s hands move with practiced ease.

“First time?” the bartender asked, not looking up.

“Not exactly.”

She sipped the whiskey, the burn of it sharp against her tongue. The night was long, and the world felt like it was moving too fast. The bar was too loud, the conversations too loud, the way people laughed too loud. She wanted to disappear, but the whiskey only made her more aware of the way her reflection in the glass flickered like a dying star.

Then, there was the sound. A car backfired. Not just any car—this was a car that belonged to someone she knew. The one who called her “sweetheart” in the morning and “loved one” in the car. The one who had promised her the world, who had built her a life she never wanted to leave.

The door of the bar creaked open. She turned, her heart pounding.

He stood there, his coat damp, his jaw set in the way it always was when he was angry. She should have run. She should have screamed. But her hands stayed still, her breath caught in her throat. Because he wasn’t just angry. He was *happy*. His smile was too wide, his eyes too bright, like he’d swallowed something she couldn’t see.

“You’re here,” he said, his voice low and rough. “I thought you’d never come.”

The room spun. The bartender’s glass clinked against the counter. The world narrowed to the sound of his footsteps on the floor, the way his hand brushed against hers when he reached for her arm. And then—

Light. The flash of something bright, sharp, like a knife in the dark. She screamed, but the world dissolved into static and pain. The last thing she saw was his face, his eyes wide with surprise, his mouth open in a sound that wasn’t a word, wasn’t a scream, but something else entirely. Something that made her stomach twist. And then—

Silence.
