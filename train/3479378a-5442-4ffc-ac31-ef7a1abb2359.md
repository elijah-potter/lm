A routine where every morning at 4:17 AM, the assembly hall’s clock chimed twelve times—an unspoken signal for the third shift to begin their ritual. Each worker followed a precise sequence: first, the ritualist knelt at the central altar, palms pressed to the wooden floor, reciting a mantra whispered into the hollow of their hand. The others lined up, hands clasped behind their backs, eyes closed, awaiting the next step. The first candle was lit with a flick of the wrist, its flame catching the oil from the bowl, casting flickering shadows across the faces. The second worker hummed a tune only they knew, a melody that felt like a second heartbeat. Then came the offering: a small pouch filled with the day’s first harvest, a single blade of wheat, a drop of dew from the morning’s first rain. The leader would trace the symbol of their guild onto the table’s edge with a knife, then pass it clockwise, each member adding their own mark—scars, initials, or symbols carved into the wood over years. By the time the twelfth strike of the clock rang, the bowl of oil had turned a deep amber, the candles burned low, and the ritualist stood, holding a single grain of wheat between their fingers. They bowed their head, whispered a prayer, and let it fall into the bowl, sealing the beginning of the workday with silence. The hall fell into a trance-like stillness until the next shift began.
