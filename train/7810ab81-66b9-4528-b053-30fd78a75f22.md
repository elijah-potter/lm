The morning after the argument, I sat at my kitchen table, coffee steaming in the hollow of my hands. A half-eaten bagel sat beside it, forgotten. Outside, the city’s hum was a familiar mask—too loud to hear the quiet ache inside. I wiped my mouth, my fingers trembling. *"What did I do?"* The words were half-alive, half-frozen, like a memory I’d tried to bury but couldn’t quite erase. The phone buzzed—an old contact, a voice I’d sworn I’d never hear again. The screen lit up with a name I’d blocked, but it was too late. The guilt coiled tighter than the lie I’d told myself to forget.

The text came in: *"You know you’re thinking about me."* My hands clenched the table. The regret wasn’t in the words I’d said, but in the ones I’d left unsaid. The way I’d looked at her—*really* looked—before the panic took over. The way I’d tried to convince myself it was just exhaustion, just time, just *everything* I’d ever done wrong. But the truth was simpler: I’d been afraid. Afraid of what she’d see if I was honest. Afraid of what I’d become if I wasn’t. The silence after the lie was worse than the words themselves.

I stared at the phone, at the name, at the city outside the window. The regret wasn’t in the past; it was in the way it lingered, a ghost in the present, waiting to whisper to me again if I let it. The bagel was cold now. The coffee was gone. But the moment was still there—flickering, half-lit, half-forgotten. I exhaled, slow and deep, like I was trying to push it away. But it wouldn’t go. It always came back.
