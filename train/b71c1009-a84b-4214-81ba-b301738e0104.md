The afterthought lingers in the hollow ache of morning coffee—*that* text sent at 11:59 PM, the last one before the alarm, the one that should have stayed unread. The words, half-formed, half-truth: *"You’ll never forgive yourself if you don’t say it."* The silence after hitting send is the only confirmation. The clock ticks, and the phone sits in another room, its screen dark. The guilt is a ghost, just visible in the corner of the eye, like a half-remembered dream.

The next day, the office buzzes with laughter. A colleague’s joke lands like a punchline; the tension in the room is just the natural hum of shared exhaustion. No one notices the weight in the stomach, the way the keyboard feels too small, the need to *move*—to scrub the dishes, to rearrange the bookshelf, anything to drown the echo of the words that were never meant to be spoken aloud.

Regret, in those moments, is a whisper. It’s the way the mirror’s reflection seems to shift when you look at it just right, the way the past feels like a room you left open, half-packed. The moment it’s hidden isn’t when it’s buried, but when it’s forgotten. The lie of forgetting is the lie of time.
