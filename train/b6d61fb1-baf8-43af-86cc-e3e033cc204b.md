## TIL the "Smell of Rain" is Actually a Combination of Several Chemical Reactions

Today I learned that the distinctive, earthy scent many associate with rain – often called "petrichor" – isn't a single aroma, but rather a complex blend of chemical processes. For years, I assumed it was a singular, mysterious fragrance, but the reality is far more fascinating.

The term "petrichor" itself was coined in 1964 by Australian scientists Isabel Joy Bear and R.G. Thomas, who were studying the scent after heavy rainfall. The word comes from the Greek words "petra" (stone) and "ichor" (the fluid that flows in the veins of the gods in Greek mythology). 

The primary contributor to this scent is a compound called geosmin, produced by bacteria, particularly *Streptomyces*, which live in the soil. These bacteria release geosmin as they metabolize organic matter. When rain falls, the impact dislodges these molecules, which are then carried into the air. Geosmin is detectable by humans at incredibly low concentrations – as little as 5 parts per trillion.

Furthermore, the aroma also incorporates a volatile oil released from plants. During dry periods, plants secrete oils that accumulate on surfaces like rocks and soil. The rain then releases these oils, adding another layer to the overall scent. The combination of geosmin and these plant oils creates the uniquely recognizable and often quite pleasant fragrance we associate with rainfall.



It's remarkable how a seemingly simple sensory experience is the result of such intricate biological and chemical interactions.
