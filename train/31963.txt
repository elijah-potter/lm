More Canadian adults have a post-secondary education than any other country in the developed world, largely because of our robust system of community colleges, says a new survey of global education.
And Canada does a better job than many countries at helping low-income kids in public school overcome hurdles to learning, according to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) in its sweeping report, Education At a Glance, to be released Tuesday.
The wide educational snapshot of everything from class size to reading scores even asked people in each country whether they trust each other — something officials said is needed for a strong economy and democracy — and it seems Canadians are more trusting than their average OECD peers, although not quite as trusting as the Dutch, Danish, Finns or Norwegians. Wherever you live, the more education you have, the more trusting.
On the down side, Canada’s math scores have slipped behind what they were in 2003 by about roughly the amount of knowledge learned in one-third of a school year, although they are still higher than the OECD average. This math drop is mirrored in several other math powerhouses, such as Finland and the Netherlands.
“The math question is complicated; it’s very unlikely there is one lever you can pull to fix it and more likely to be a mix of factors, such as a changing student population, how teachers are trained, the way math is taught,” said Andrew Parkin, director general of the Council of Ministers of Education of Canada.
“That’s why these global comparisons are useful, even in areas that are encouraging they point out lots of places where we have work to do.”
Whatever the problem, Canadian students’ math scores still have less to do with their family income than students in most other countries because of the support Canada offers low-income students, the report showed. Only a handful of countries do as well as Canada at both math, and reducing the gap in math scores between rich and poor students. These countries include Hong Kong, Estonia, Lichtenstein and the Macao region of China.
Some 24 per cent of Canadian adults have graduated from a community college.
“That’s the strength of the Canadian system; it offers many ways to get a post-secondary education and many bridges from the different pathways,” said Andreas Schleicher, OECD’s director of education and skills.
While Canada spends roughly twice as much to educate each post-secondary student as the average OECD country, much of that comes from tuition, which is exceeded by fees in several other countries including the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan and Korea.
- The proportion of Canadian 15-29-year-olds who were neither employed nor in school or training (NEET) rose slightly to 13 per cent in 2012, which is still below the OECD average of 17 per cent
- Canada’s share of international students increased slightly between 2000 and 2012 to about 5 per cent, during which time the United States saw its share of international students drop from 23 per cent to 16 per cent
- In Canada, about 43 per cent of the cost of post-secondary education is picked up by privately, much of it from tuition, and this is higher than the OECD average of 31 per cent
- Canadian primary teachers teach an average of 802 hours per year, slightly more than the OECD average of 782
- Canada spends about 7 per cent of its Gross Domestic Product in Education, compared to 6 per cent for the OECD average
- In Canada, about $23,226 is spent per year to educate the average post-secondary student (some of it through tuition), compared to the OECD average of $13,958.
