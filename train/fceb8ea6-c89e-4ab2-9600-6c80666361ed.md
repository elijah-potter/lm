A nascent urban hub emerges along the eastern edge of a sprawling metropolis, where a former industrial waterfront is being repurposed. Concrete overgrowth is being cleared by heavy machinery, revealing the skeletal remains of old warehouses and factories. A network of crumbling piers juts into a murky canal, lined with rusted shipping containers now covered in graffiti. Newer structures—steel-framed and glass-fronted—are rising above, their skeletal forms still exposed as cranes work around the clock. Pedestrians, clad in high-visibility vests, move between scaffolding, organizing the space. The air smells of damp earth and fresh concrete. A few makeshift stalls dot the area, selling handmade goods and food from pop-up stalls, hinting at a local market soon to be established. The rhythmic sound of construction mixes with the distant hum of traffic, and the occasional chatter of workers and passersby. The ground is uneven, patches of grass still clinging to the edges of the cleared site, marking the transition between old and new. The area is still unmarked, its boundaries undefined—just visible, just beginning.
