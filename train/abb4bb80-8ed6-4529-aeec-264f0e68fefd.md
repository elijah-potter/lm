The lighthouse keeper, Silas Blackwood, meticulously polished the Fresnel lens, his movements economical and precise. The rhythmic swish of the polishing cloth against the glass was the only sound competing with the mournful cry of the gulls circling overhead. He had performed this ritual every evening for thirty-seven years, ever since his father, Bartholomew, had passed the responsibility to him. The beam, a vital reassurance to sailors navigating the treacherous reefs of the Serpent’s Tooth, demanded unwavering diligence.

Tonight, however, a disquiet settled upon Silas. The wind, typically a predictable westerly, shifted unexpectedly to the north, carrying with it a peculiar scent—something akin to brine and woodsmoke, a combination he had never encountered before. He paused in his polishing, peering out into the gathering dusk. The sea, usually a predictable slate grey, was churning with an unnatural restlessness. 

A small boat, barely visible against the darkening water, appeared on the horizon. It was a rowboat, an unlikely vessel for these waters, and it moved with a disconcerting speed. Silas frowned, adjusting his spectacles. He recognized the pattern of its erratic course; it was not being steered with any discernible skill.

He activated the speaking tube, his voice echoing down to the lower levels of the lighthouse. "Elara, observe a vessel approaching from the northeast. A rowboat, appearing distressed. Prepare the rescue skiff."

Elara Finch, his apprentice, responded promptly. "Acknowledged, Silas. I'm already monitoring its position. It’s moving erratically, almost as if the occupant is incapacitated."

Silas descended the spiral staircase, his boots echoing against the stone. He reached the base of the lighthouse and watched as Elara prepared the small rescue skiff. Her movements were efficient and practiced; she had proven herself a capable assistant in the short time she had been under his tutelage.

The rowboat drew closer, revealing a single occupant: a young woman, her face pale and drawn, her hands gripping the oars with a desperate strength. As the skiff approached, Silas could see that her clothes were soaked and torn, and a single, crimson stain marred the fabric of her sleeve.

“Easy now,” Elara called out, her voice calm and reassuring. “We’re here to help.”

The woman didn’t respond, her eyes fixed on a point beyond the lighthouse, a look of profound terror etched on her face. Elara carefully maneuvered the skiff alongside the rowboat and, with Silas’s assistance, helped the woman aboard. 

“Her name is Lyra,” the woman mumbled, her voice barely audible. “They’re coming. They’re always coming.”

Silas exchanged a concerned glance with Elara. "Who is coming, Lyra? Can you tell us what happened?"

Lyra shuddered, wrapping her arms around herself. "The Whispers," she whispered, her voice trembling. “They steal the light. They take everything.”

Silas felt a chill despite the warmth of the lighthouse. The Whispers were an old sailors’ legend, a tale told to frighten children—stories of spectral beings who extinguished lights and lured sailors to their doom. He dismissed them as folklore, but the genuine terror in Lyra’s eyes gave him pause.

He insisted she be examined by the village physician, Dr. Alistair Crowe, the following morning. For the rest of the evening, Silas resumed his duties, meticulously maintaining the light, but the unsettling encounter lingered in his mind. The wind had died down, the sea had calmed, and the gulls had retreated. Yet, the scent of brine and woodsmoke still clung to the air, a haunting reminder of the woman and her cryptic warning. He could only hope that the morning would bring clarity, and that Lyra’s fears were nothing more than the product of exhaustion and a troubled mind. The light, however, must remain vigilant. It was a promise he had made to his father, and a promise he intended to keep.
