I see a bush-covered mountain.
kauri, tarairi, tōtara
trees tasting sunlight, for it is early morning,
and a soft summer sun has just lifted above the horizon.
I have tasted that same morning sunlight in berries
of a tōtara while Grandfather milked the cow.
I look into the mirror and reflected back.
I see a river,
its banks lined with kōwhai, pūriri, kahikatea.
Some lean into the tide and drink their reflections.
As dusk melts into dark, they settle and whisper their tree talk.
I hear a soft soughing of wind in the canopy of branches,
and looking up, I see the Southern Cross trekking over the sky.
I look into the mirror and reflected back.
I see a church,
sun-washed windows and a red-painted spire—a landmark standing tall.
Inside, a small girl ponders names around the walls and understands
that her ancestors too have tasted the sunlight of the trees.
She fidgets and whispers to her grandfather,
“Would you make me a handkerchief rabbit, please?”
