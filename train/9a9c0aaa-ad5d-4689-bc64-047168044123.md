The village elder spoke of the storm’s wrath, how the old oak had bent beneath the weight of the wind, its branches snapping like twigs. Children ran to shelter as thunder shook the earth, their laughter swallowed by the howling gales. I watched from the rooftop, fingers digging into the cold stone, as rain lashed the cobblestones below. The world felt alive in that moment, raw and unfiltered.

But the storm had a name. And though the wind carried whispers of its fury, the elder never said it aloud. No one did. The villagers knew, of course. They knew the storm was called *Malak*, the one that had taken three men in the last harvest, the one that turned the river’s flow into a roar before it vanished like smoke. Yet not one of them spoke its name again, not even in fear. They spoke only of the wind, of the oak, of the children’s screams. As if the thing itself could not hear.

I remember the first time I saw the river’s edge tremble—not with water, but with something darker, something that did not belong. The villagers did not look. They moved past, their faces set in the same quiet certainty. That is how we live. We avoid what we fear, and in doing so, we make it real.
