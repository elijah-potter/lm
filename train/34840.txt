African ancestry was associated with increased odds of peanut-specific immunoglobulin E (slgE) levels of 5 kUa/L or more (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.01-1.52). This slgE level has been tied to a greater chance of clinical reactivity among children ages 1 to 5, said Rajesh Kumar, MD, from Children's Memorial Hospital in Chicago, and colleagues.
In addition, with each 10% increase in African ancestry, there was a 25% increase in the likelihood of peanut SlgE levels being 5 kUa/L or more, the researchers wrote in the October issue of Pediatrics.
However, Kumar and colleagues said they found no association of black race with those peanut slgE levels.
Recently published results from a national health survey indicated a threefold increase in the rate of food allergy among black people, and this was particularly true for peanut allergies (J Allergy Clin Immunol 2010; 126 (4): 798-806).
But that survey relied on self-identified race, which is an imprecise measure, the authors said. Instead, they looked at the association of genetic ancestry with food sensitization along with self-identified race.
Participants were part of the Children's Health Study (CHS) at Boston Medical Center, a substudy of the Boston Birth Cohort. These children were enrolled in CHS at birth with follow-up visits at six to 12 months and at ages 2, 4, and 6.
CHS currently has 1,794 enrollees with genetic ancestry information available for 1,104 children, all of whom were included in Kumar's study. To determine global genetic ancestry, the authors genotyped 150 ancestral informative markers (AIMs) from an admixture mapping panel.
At the two-year visit, slgE concentrations in plasma were measured for eight major food allergens, including peanut and cow's milk.
For the 1,104 children included in this study, 60.9% were identified as black (by the mother), 22.5% as Hispanic, 5.9% as white, and 10.8% as other. There were an equal number of boys and girls with a mean age of 2.65 years.
According to the results, 35.5% of this predominantly minority cohort exhibited food sensitization.
Food sensitization to all major allergen categories was associated with self-identified black race (OR 2.2, 95% CI 1.20 to 4.11) and African ancestry (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.31.
Overall, for each 10% increment in African ancestry, there was an associated OR of 1.04 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.10) for sensitization for one or two foods, and an associated OR of 1.19 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.32) for more than three foods.
Looking at different quartiles of African ancestry, the authors found the following associations for peanut sensitization (P<0.0001 for trend):
- For quartile 1.0% to 39.35% of African ancestry, less than 10% of children had peanut sensitization
- For quartile 39.36% to 77.70%, 10% had peanut sensitization
- For quartile 77.71% to 88.80%, between 10% and 20% had peanut sensitization
- For quartile 88.81% to 100%, 20% had peanut sensitization
Although the findings were not significant for cow's milk allergens, an ancestry association was seen with milk slgE levels of ≥5 kUa/L (OR 1.24, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.63), Kumar's group noted.
The authors suggested that ethnocultural factors, such as differences in diet and early-life feeding practices, may influence milk sensitization, while peanut allergies may be regulated by genetics.
This study did have some limitations including the use of an ancestry variable that was derived from a small panel of AIMs versus genome-wide data.
They stressed that the study of genetic ancestry and food sensitization will become increasingly important for an increasingly heterogeneous U.S. population.
"With more interracial people and marriages, traditional ethnic classifications will become increasingly imprecise," the authors said. "Self-identified race ... would underestimate the degree of genetic variation within these populations. Genetic factors influence the development of food allergy."
They called for follow-up in the CHS population along with other studies to explore racial disparities in food sensitization and allergies.
