## A Counseling Session

**Setting:** A comfortably furnished therapist’s office. Soft lighting, a view of a quiet garden.

**Characters:**

*   **Eleanor Vance:** A woman in her late thirties, visibly anxious but attempting composure.
*   **Dr. Alistair Finch:** A therapist, mid-fifties, with a calm and reassuring demeanor.

---

**Dr. Finch:** Welcome, Eleanor. Please, have a seat. It’s good to see you again. How have you been since our last session?

**Eleanor:** (Sitting down, fidgeting slightly) Thank you, Dr. Finch. Honestly? It’s been…difficult. The project at work, the one we discussed? It’s spiraled. There are unexpected complications, and I feel increasingly overwhelmed.

**Dr. Finch:** I understand. It sounds like you're carrying a considerable weight. Could you tell me a little more about what specifically feels overwhelming?

**Eleanor:** It’s not just the workload, although that’s substantial. It’s the feeling that I’m going to fail. That I’ve missed something crucial, and everyone else will realize it. I find myself rechecking everything multiple times, and even then, I’m not convinced.

**Dr. Finch:** That sounds incredibly stressful. That level of self-doubt can be paralyzing. You mentioned rechecking things repeatedly. Is that a new behavior, or something you’ve tended to do in the past?

**Eleanor:** It’s definitely intensified. I've always been a detail-oriented person, but now it’s… compulsive. I’m spending hours on tasks that should take minutes. And the worry just builds. I barely sleep.

**Dr. Finch:** It’s clear this project is significantly impacting your well-being. It sounds as though the anxiety surrounding it has triggered a pattern of behavior that’s now exacerbating the problem. You're essentially adding to your workload through the repeated checking. Let's explore that a little further. Can you recall a specific instance where this checking behavior felt particularly intense?

**Eleanor:** (Pauses, takes a deep breath) Yesterday, I spent almost two hours verifying data that had already been approved by my team. I knew, logically, that it was probably fine, but I couldn't stop myself. The thought of a mistake being discovered was just… unbearable.

**Dr. Finch:** That's a powerful illustration of the anxiety’s grip. You recognized the logic – that the data was likely correct – yet the anxiety compelled you to continue. It's a conflict between your rational mind and the anxious voice. This voice, as we’ve discussed, often presents the worst-case scenario as a certainty. What thoughts were running through your mind during those two hours? Be as specific as you can remember.

**Eleanor:** (Hesitates) Mostly, it was just… "What if I’m wrong? What if this ruins everything? They’ll all think I’m incompetent." It's a constant loop.

**Dr. Finch:** Those are significant fears, Eleanor. It’s understandable that they would trigger such a strong reaction. Let’s examine those thoughts. Are they based on past experiences, or are they assumptions about how others perceive you?

**Eleanor:** I think… they’re a combination. There was a situation a few years ago where I made a minor error on a report, and my supervisor was quite critical. I’ve carried that with me, I think. I’m anticipating that same level of scrutiny, even though this situation is completely different.

**Dr. Finch:** So, you're projecting a past experience onto the present. That's a very common response to anxiety. It's your mind's way of trying to protect you from potential pain, by anticipating and preparing for the worst. However, as you’re recognizing, it’s also preventing you from fully engaging with the present. Let's work on strategies to challenge those automatic negative thoughts. We can start by identifying the evidence *for* and *against* the belief that your work will be critically judged. Would you be open to trying that exercise next session?

**Eleanor:** (Nods slowly) Yes, I think so. It’s just… daunting.

**Dr. Finch:** It takes courage to confront those fears, and you’re demonstrating that courage simply by being here and discussing them. Remember, we're working together. And small steps, consistently taken, can lead to significant progress. For this week, I'd like you to simply observe those anxious thoughts as they arise. Don't try to change them, just notice them. We’re building awareness first. How does that feel?

**Eleanor:** (A faint smile appears) It feels… manageable. Thank you, Dr. Finch.

**Dr. Finch:** You’re very welcome, Eleanor. I'm confident that with continued effort, you’ll find ways to navigate these challenges with greater ease and self-compassion. I look forward to seeing you next week.
