A quiet afternoon in a cramped office, fluorescent lights buzzing overhead. Your colleague, Sarah, slides a stack of reports across your desk, her fingers trembling. She watches as you open it, the words *urgent* and *confidential* bolded in red ink. Her breath catches—then slips free.

"You know, I’ve been meaning to tell you," she says, voice low. "The numbers in this quarter’s report are a lie. The actual revenue was cut by half to meet the target. The CEO’s office has been empty for days. They’re hiding something."

Her eyes flick to yours, not accusing, but desperate. The truth presses against her ribs like a physical weight. She’s spent months suppressing it, stitching lies into every meeting, every email. But here, in the open, it feels like freedom—or the last act of honesty before drowning in silence.

You look up from the report, unsure. The air between them thickens, charged with the weight of what could be lost. Sarah exhales, her shoulders dropping. "I didn’t want to say anything," she admits, "but I can’t keep pretending anymore."

The truth doesn’t always set you free—it’s often the first step into the abyss. Sarah’s confession doesn’t unravel the lie immediately. The report stays closed. The office hums with the same stale air. But for the first time, the lie feels like a wound that might heal. And in that moment, something shifts—just enough to let the light in.
