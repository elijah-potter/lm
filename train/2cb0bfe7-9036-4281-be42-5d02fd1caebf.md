The subway doors hissed shut behind her, leaving the last of the commuters behind like ghosts of their own exhaustion. She watched the crowd blur as they moved—each step a small, unseen dance, their heads bowed, hands clutching phones, oblivious to the quiet pulse of the city. She lingered on the edge of the platform, fingers tracing the grain of her coat as the hum of the train filled the air.

She had learned to see what others missed: the way the lamplight caught the tears in a child’s eyes as they held their mother’s hand too tight, the sharp intake of breath when a stranger’s gaze lingered too long, the way a lover’s voice would crack when they realized they’d said too much. She noticed the way the wind carried a scent—something floral and old, like a forgotten book—and how it made her stomach twist, not with hunger, but with the memory of a place she couldn’t name.

The train doors opened. The crowd surged forward, but she stood still, watching the world pass her by. She had learned that some things were not meant to be shared, only felt.
