The clock struck seven when Mia walked into the library, her sneakers squelching against the wet pavement. Inside, the fluorescent lights buzzed like angry bees, casting long shadows across the stacks of books. She grabbed a notebook from the table by the counter and flopped onto a stool, her backpack tangled around her legs. The first page was already filled with scribbles from yesterday’s science quiz—*38%* still flashed in red.

Outside, rain drummed against the window. Her mom had texted: *You’re staying in tonight. No excuses.* But Mia had already been avoiding her since the argument over the car keys.

She opened the first book she grabbed—a worn copy of *The Giver*, the one her teacher had made everyone read. The cover was cracked, the spine stiff with age. The story had been on her mind all week. About a world that didn’t remember pain, where choices weren’t real—just assigned.

A noise made her jump. A girl in a hoodie sat across from her, flipping through a manga. "You okay?" the girl asked, her voice sharp. Mia nodded, but her hands trembled. She didn’t want to talk about the world, or the way her dad kept ignoring her, or how her best friend, Leo, had started talking about "the system" like it was something to fix.

The girl’s eyes flicked to the book. "You reading *The Giver*?"

Mia hesitated. "Yeah."

The girl sighed, like she already knew. "I’m not supposed to be here. My dad’s gonna kill me if he finds out." She leaned in. "But the library’s safe. For now."

Mia’s stomach twisted. She’d heard stories about kids disappearing—somewhere between the school and the bus stop, or the alley behind the park. The ones who didn’t come back. The ones who *chose*.

She flipped to the last page. The Giver had given Jonas the memory of snow, and it was everything—cold, beautiful, terrifying. Mia’s breath hitched. If only she could remember what it felt like to *want* something. To *choose* something. Not just follow the rules.

The girl’s phone buzzed. "I gotta go," she whispered. "But thanks."

Mia watched as the girl disappeared into the crowd, her hood pulled tight. The library door creaked open. Rain splattered against the glass, turning the street into a mirror of her own reflection—wet, tired, and wondering if she was running from something too.

She closed the book and stared at the blank page. Maybe she should write about it. Or maybe she should just keep running.

But what if she stopped?

---

That night, Mia sat at her desk, her fingers hovering over the keyboard. The screen flickered with half-written sentences, half-thoughts. The window above her was dark, the curtains drawn tight. Outside, the city hummed—sirens, distant laughter, the occasional car backfire.

She typed:

*They say we’re safe. That we’re happy. But what if we’re just waiting for the next one to come?*

Her pen hovered over the paper. She’d seen the news. The headlines were always the same: *Disappeared. Last seen near the old train tracks.* No names, no details. Just *gone.*

Leo had been the first to mention it. At lunch, he’d leaned over the table, his voice low. "You ever notice how no one talks about what happens to the ones who don’t come back?" He’d flipped through his phone, showing her a news article with a grainy photo of a body in a ditch. The caption read: *Victim identified as 14-year-old female.*

Mia’s throat tightened. She’d never asked why. Not until now.

She thought about the girl from the library. About the way she’d looked at her, like she’d seen something Mia didn’t want to see. Like the world was bigger than the rules they were taught to follow.

The phone rang. It was her mom.

*"Where are you?"*

*"I’m at home."*

*"You’re not answering my calls."*

*"I’m fine."*

*"Mia—"*

*"I’ll be home in an hour."*

The line went dead. She hung up and stared at the clock. One hour. That was all she had.

Outside, the city lights blurred together, a single line of noise against the dark. Mia picked up her pen and wrote:

*I’m not running anymore.*

The page filled with words—names, places, the way the air smelled after rain. The way the train tracks stretched into the distance, disappearing like a promise. The way the rules felt like chains.

She didn’t know what would happen next. But for the first time in months, she didn’t care.

The door creaked open. Her mom stood there, her face pale. *"You’ve been avoiding me."*

Mia met her eyes. *"I don’t like the way you treat me."*

Silence.

Then, her mom’s voice was quiet, almost lost. *"What did you say?"*

Mia’s hands shook. *"I said I don’t like the way you treat me."*

Her mom’s eyes welled up. She turned away, her shoulders shaking. *"I’m sorry."*

Mia’s breath came fast. *"Why?"*

The words hung between them, heavy as the rain outside. Mia waited.

And then—

*"Because I don’t know how else to be a mother."* Her mom’s voice cracked. *"I love you. I just… I don’t know how to fix this."*

Mia swallowed. *"Then fix it."*

Her mom’s hands trembled. She reached out, gripping her wrist. *"I’m not trying to control you. I just… I want you to be safe."*

Mia nodded. *"I know."*

Her mom’s eyes were wet. *"But you’re not listening."*

*"I am,"* Mia said. *"I just… I don’t know what to do."*

Her mom exhaled sharply. *"You’re sixteen. You can do more than anyone else in this city."*

Mia’s stomach flipped. *"Like what?"*

Her mom hesitated. *"Like telling someone."*

Mia’s fingers twitched. *"Who?"*

*"The right person."*

The line between them blurred. Mia’s world felt smaller now—just her, her mom, the messy room, the way the rain still fell outside.

She thought of the girl in the library. Of the train tracks. Of the way the city kept moving, even when the world felt like it was falling apart.

*"I’ll tell someone,"* Mia said.

Her mom’s face lit up. *"You will?"*

*"Yes."*

Her mom’s voice was soft. *"Good."*

And then—

*"What do you want to do?"*

Mia’s mind raced. *"I don’t know."*

*"Then ask."*

The word hung in the air, heavy. Mia looked at her mom, really looked at her. *"I don’t know how to ask for help."*

Her mom’s eyes softened. *"You’re not asking for help. You’re asking for answers."*

Mia’s voice was small. *"What if I don’t find them?"*

Her mom’s hand squeezed hers. *"Then you’ll keep going. Until you do."*

The rain outside stopped. The city sighed, as if it had been holding its breath.

Mia exhaled. *"What if I don’t know what to do?"*

Her mom’s voice was firm. *"Then you’ll find out."*

She stood, her shoulders straight. *"What if I’m not enough?"*

*"You’re enough,"* her mom said. *"Right now. And tomorrow. And always."*

Mia’s throat burned. *"What if I’m wrong?"*

Her mom’s voice was gentle. *"Then you’ll learn."*

The door clicked shut behind them. The city hummed on, indifferent.

Mia sat at her desk, her hands trembling. The notebook was open, the page filled with words. But she didn’t write anymore.

She just looked at the blank page.

And for the first time in a long time, she felt like she could start again.
