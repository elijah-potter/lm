The sky now bleached with permanent golden streaks, a fractured aurora borealis that never shifts—like an ancient sun setting for eternity. The earth, stripped of its verdant pulse, is a gnarled skeleton of exposed roots and cracked soil, some still clinging to stubborn moss, others barren and wind-scoured. Rivers run sluggish, their banks lined with skeletal trees—those that remain—skeletonized by a slow, creeping rot that turns bark to dust. The air hums with a low, metallic resonance, the scent of ozone and rust thick in the stagnant breeze.

The horizon is a jagged, unnatural line where the land and sky converge, a faultline where the earth’s crust has been violently pulled apart, revealing glimpses of molten rock that never cools. The few remaining structures stand like hollowed-out husks, their once-vibrant colors faded to a sickly, sickly-silver pallor, their glass and metal warped by some unseen, prolonged stress. The night is perpetually twilight, the absence of darkness itself, as if the world has forgotten how to sleep. The silence is deafening, broken only by distant, rhythmic explosions—earthquakes, perhaps, or the slow, creeping migration of something that was never meant to be here.
