For some homeowners -- especially those in dry, chalky areas with Mediterranean climates -- sulfur is an invaluable garden tool. Ground elemental sulfur, a common soil amendment, lowers the pH level of overly alkaline soils. A poor pH level keeps your lawn or garden from thriving. When soil is either too acidic or too alkaline, it prevents nutrients, like nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, from dissolving, which means plant roots can't absorb them. If your yard is "chalky," or alkaline, consider using sulfur to neutralize the pH level.
You can buy user-friendly soil pH test kits at garden centers, or you can send soil samples to a professional testing service. Mix soil from a few spots in your yard before sending it to the service or adding it to a home test kit. If your soil registers at over 7.0 -- the neutral pH level -- chances are you'll need to lower the pH level. The majority of plants, including lawns, flowers and vegetables, prefer a neutral to slightly acidic pH of 6.5 to 7.
For every 100 square feet of yard, it takes an average of 1 pound of sulfur to lower the pH level by 1 full point. After you determine your current pH level, determine how many points you need to lower your pH to get to the desired level. Then measure your yard to get a rough square footage of the area you'll be amending. For example, if your 500-square-foot front yard has a pH of 8.0 and you are aiming for a neutral 7.0, you'll need an average of 5 pounds of ground elemental sulfur for the area.
You may need more or less sulfur noted to acidify your yard, based on other soil characteristics. If your soil is alkaline and also sandy, use slightly less than 1 pound per 100 square feet, and per 1.0 pH point. If it is alkaline but somewhat loamy, use more than 1 pound. For very loamy soils, you may need to use more than 2 pounds per 100 square feet, and per 1.0 pH point.
Apply elemental sulfur one year before establishing lawns or gardens, if possible. Work the sulfur into the top 6 inches of soil. A few months before planting, test your soil again to ensure that it is in the desired pH range.
If you have free access to a large amount of such acidic materials as pine needles or shredded oak leaves, you may choose to use them in place of sulfur. Work them into the soil several months before planting. These materials may also be used to neutralize alkaline soils when used as mulching materials. Peat moss is another traditional acidifier, but is often more expensive than sulfur -- as well as bulkier to transport. If you are in a hurry, consider using the faster-acting iron sulfate instead of elemental sulfate.
