The first mistake was in the coffee order at the barista’s counter. "Two lattes, black," I said, misreading the menu. By the time I realized my error, the barista had already poured the milk into my cup, making it a latte instead of a black coffee. I watched as steam curled from the top, a small, bitter mistake that would soon spiral.

---

The second mistake came at the office the next morning. I misplaced my keys, and by the time I found them—half an hour late—I had already missed my train. The commute was a blur of traffic jams and impromptu detours, my phone buzzing with missed calls from my boss.

---

That afternoon, I accidentally sent the wrong email to the wrong client. The subject line was "Urgent: Project Deadline Extension" but the body was a draft of a proposal for a competitor. The client’s assistant replied with a single word: **"ERROR."**

---

The third mistake was the most damaging: the one that changed everything. That evening, after another missed deadline, I took a shortcut home through an alley I’d never used before. The light turned green, but I didn’t stop—just a moment of distraction, a glance at my phone. The car behind me honked, but I didn’t see them. When I pulled into my driveway, my front tire was flat.

---

The car was totaled. The client’s lawyer called. My boss fired me on the spot. The mistakes had turned into a chain reaction, and now, I was just another casualty of my own missteps.

I sat on the couch, staring at the wrecked car, and wondered how many more mistakes I’d make before I realized they were the ones that mattered.
