I was standing on the corner of Maple and Oak, waiting for the bus, when I heard the commotion. A group of teenagers had gathered near the alley behind the diner, their laughter too loud, their voices carrying through the evening air. One of them, a girl with a bandaged knuckle, was arguing with another kid who had clearly been the one to start the fight. The noise escalated quickly—shouts, fists raised, and the unmistakable sound of breaking glass.

I watched from the sidewalk, my phone tucked under my arm, as the girl in the bandage lunged forward, her arm outstretched. The other kid didn’t even flinch; he just stood there, grinning like he’d already won. Then the boy at the center of the group—a bigger kid with a scar running down his cheek—stepped forward, his fist raised.

The fight erupted in a flash. Glass shattered. A woman screamed from the diner’s window. I didn’t move, just stood there, heart pounding, as the chaos unfolded. The girl’s knuckles bled. The bigger boy’s face went red. A firecracker went off—someone must have tossed one from the alley. The girl hit the ground, her shoulder scraping against the pavement. The boy who started it spun on his heel, his hand already going back for another swing.

I should have run. I should have called the cops. Instead, I just stood there, frozen, until the last thing I saw was the boy’s fist connecting with the girl’s ribs. Then the noise cut off. The alley was silent. No more laughter. No more shouts. Just the distant wail of sirens.

I didn’t move until I heard the sirens. Then I ran.
