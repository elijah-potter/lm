In the hollowed-out core of the old mill, where the gears still turned by habit rather than purpose, a young woman named Elara sat cross-legged on the damp stone floor. Her fingers traced the faint script of the handwritten ledger—*The Ledger of Whispers*—that had been passed down through generations of millers. The ink had faded to a whisper, as if the words themselves were afraid of being read.

Tonight, she had come to find out what the last entry said. The mill’s clock tower chimed midnight, and the wind howled through the rusted windows like a ghostly sigh. Elara’s breath fogged in the cold air, and she knelt, her palms pressed against the worn pages. The last entry read:

*"Here, where the shadows cling thicker than the dust, the mirror in the basement reflects not your face, but the face of the woman you were before you chose the mill. She smiles—too wide, too knowing—and her reflection lingers in the glass long after you’ve turned away."*

She reached out, her fingers trembling. The mirror was cracked, but not like the others—this one was split down the middle, as if torn by something unseen. The left side showed her reflection, pale and still, her eyes empty. The right side was blank. Then, slowly, it filled.

A woman stood in the glass, her back to the door, her dark hair tangled with spiderwebs. She wore a dress of black silk, embroidered with stars that pulsed faintly in the dark. Her mouth curved into a smile, and the air around her shimmered like liquid mercury. She turned, and Elara gasped as she saw the truth: the woman in the mirror was her own reflection, but not as she remembered herself.

*"You are not the last of this house,"* the woman whispered, her voice like wind through reeds. *"You are the first."*

The mirror shattered. The last thing Elara saw was the woman in the dress—standing now in the mill’s basement—reaching for her reflection’s face, stitching it back together with threads of light. The gears of the mill began to turn again, louder than ever, and the clock tower’s hands moved forward, past midnight, past time itself.
