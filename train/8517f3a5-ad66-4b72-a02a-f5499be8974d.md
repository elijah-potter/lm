A cold kitchen in a cramped apartment. The fluorescent lights buzz like a trapped insect. A single mug of lukewarm coffee sits untouched on the counter, steam long dissipated. The TV’s static hums in the background—static that’s *always* static here. On the wall, a framed photo of a childhood friend, now long gone, their smile frozen mid-cheeky grin, their eyes reflecting the same tired, unreadable expression we all carry like second skin.

You’re standing in the kitchen, arms crossed, hands resting on the counter like a statue carved from bone. Your phone buzzes—a notification from a text you’ve sent *three days ago*, the last one you ever sent to them. The message: *"I’m not coming over tonight."* The reply, typed in the same monotone voice: *"No problem."* The conversation has been like this for months—words exchanged in a loop of silence, each one a rehearsed line, the pauses stretching like rubber bands between them.

You turn away from the photo, your reflection in the glass a distorted echo of yourself. The movement is deliberate, practiced—like the way you always sit at the dining table, the way you breathe, the way you fold your hands when you’re about to say something that’s already been said before.

Outside, the rain taps against the windows in a rhythm that’s familiar, as if it’s been raining here since time began. The city beyond the window is a blur of neon and glass, a place where people move in unseen patterns, where faces blur into shapes, where every gesture is a question without a question. You know this moment, inside and out. It’s the kind of moment that doesn’t need to be remembered—it’s written in the cracks of your bones, in the way your fingers tremble when you reach for the coffee cup, in the way your throat tightens before you speak. It’s the kind of moment that’s been waiting for you, like a door you’ve been knocking on for years, the handle already turning.
