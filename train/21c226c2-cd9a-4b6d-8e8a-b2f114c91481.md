**The Last Shift at the Foundry**
*October 12th, 1978*

The furnace’s last breath hummed like a dying bee, its glow bleeding through the cracked glass of the observation port. Inside, the molten steel pulsed—still, stubborn, refusing to let go of the carbon it had swallowed like a poison. Outside, the wind howled through the old sawdust bins, carrying the scent of rust and something older, something that had been waiting here since before the last war.

I adjusted the thermostat, fingers trembling. The gauge needle—*always a liar*—danced erratically, as if the metal itself was fighting the dial. The operator beside me, a man named Elias, muttered, *"Another day, another inch."* He was right. The walls of the crucible were thinning. The next batch would be the last.

I reached for the scrap metal bucket, its contents a mix of failed castings and the occasional salvaged gear from the old steam engines. The last time I’d pulled this bucket, the foreman had barked about "wasting good iron." Now, the foreman was gone, replaced by a woman who didn’t understand the language of the furnace. She kept calling the molten steel *"liquid gold."* I told her it was just iron, but the way she watched the fire, I wondered if she was trying to believe.

The last piece I’d poured was a valve for the new turbine at the power plant. It was supposed to last ten years. I bet it’d last ten minutes.

The night shift was already in gear when I walked out. The air smelled of damp wood and something sweet, like burnt sugar. Somewhere, a radio crackled to life, playing that new song about the "end of the world." I didn’t recognize it. Neither did anyone else.

I left the foundry through the back door, the one that creaked like a dying man. The street below was quiet. Too quiet. The streetlights flickered, as if someone had flipped a switch and forgotten to turn it back on.

I found Elias in the old boiler room, his hands wrapped around a thermometer, his face pale. *"You’re sure?"* he whispered. I nodded. The last batch was done. The furnace was dead.

Elias exhaled, slow and steady. *"Then let’s get out of here."* He turned and walked toward the door, but his foot caught on something—something hard and round. A pipe, maybe. Or a dream.

He didn’t fall. Just stood there, for a moment. Then he walked out.

I closed the door behind me.

Outside, the wind was stronger. The sky was gray.

The world was waiting.
