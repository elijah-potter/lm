The rain had started just before dawn, turning the cobblestones slick with a reflective sheen. Dr. Elias Voss wiped the condensation from his glasses with the back of his sleeve, his fingers trembling slightly. His voice was steady when he spoke, but his eyes betrayed the weight of the apology he’d been crafting for weeks.

“Ma’am, I’m truly sorry for the delay. The weather… well, it’s been… uncooperative.”

The woman at the other end of the conference table—Mrs. Linder, his long-time patient and the one who’d made him realize he wasn’t just treating illness but people—nodded slowly. “You don’t need to say that again,” she murmured, though her grip on the folder in her lap didn’t waver. “I know you’ve been waiting.”

Elias exhaled sharply, the breath fogging the glass of his thermos. “I am.”

The first light of morning crept through the grimy window, casting long shadows across the sterile white walls of the clinic. The air smelled of antiseptic and something older—old paper, old memories. Mrs. Linder had been his patient for over twenty years, and today was the first time in that time that he’d come unprepared.

“You’re late,” she said without looking up from the stack of files in front of her. Her voice was quiet, but her gaze was sharp, cutting through the haze of his guilt.

Elias clenched his fists. “I know.”

She opened her folder, flipping through the pages with deliberate slowness. “You’re late to the meeting,” she said, “and you’re late to *this*.”

His stomach twisted. “I’m sorry.”

She set the folder down with a thud. “You’ve been avoiding this for months, Elias. Every excuse—bad traffic, car trouble, the clinic’s new security system that you keep forgetting to update. And now, because of *this*—” she gestured vaguely at the rain-streaked window “—you’re finally here.”

A knock at the door made them both jump. The clinic’s assistant poked her head in, her expression unreadable. “Dr. Voss? The patient downstairs is in distress.”

Mrs. Linder’s eyes flickered to the door, then back to Elias. “You know that girl who keeps showing up unannounced? The one with the scars?”

Elias swallowed. “The one with the…” He trailed off. “The one who keeps asking about your research.”

She stood abruptly, her chair scraping against the floor. “She’s not just a patient, Elias. She’s been following you for years. Every time you’ve been late, she’s been waiting. Every time you’ve changed your schedule, she’s been there.”

The assistant’s voice cut through the tension. “Dr. Voss, she’s asking for you.”

Mrs. Linder’s voice was a blade. “Go. Before you regret it.”

Elias didn’t hesitate. He grabbed his coat, his mind racing. The girl—Lena—had always been too quiet, too observant. But she’d never said a word. Until now.

He stepped into the drizzle, the cold biting at his skin. The clinic’s lobby was crowded, the fluorescent lights buzzing overhead. Lena was standing near the front desk, her fingers wrapped around the edge of a crumpled note.

“Dr. Voss,” she said, her voice barely above a whisper. The words were raw, jagged. “You didn’t just avoid me. You *forgot* me.”

He didn’t move. Didn’t breathe. The rain was his only sound.

She held out the note, her grip firm. The paper was yellowed, the ink smudged. At the top, in her own messy handwriting:

*“You said you’d help me. You said you’d listen. But you never did. Not really. Not like you did with her.”*

Mrs. Linder’s name.

Elias’ hands trembled. “What do you want, Lena?”

Her lips curled, but it wasn’t amusement. “I want you to remember what it’s like to be ignored.”

The clinic’s assistant cleared her throat. “Dr. Voss, we need to talk to you.”

Lena’s eyes were cold. “You’ll never listen to me, will you?”

The assistant’s voice was firm. “Dr. Voss, come with me.”

Elias didn’t argue. He took the note, the weight of it heavy in his palm. The rain kept falling, the city’s neon signs flickering through the drizzle. Somewhere, a siren wailed. Somewhere, Mrs. Linder waited.

He folded the note carefully, tucking it into his pocket. “I’m sorry,” he said, his voice breaking.

Lena’s voice was a ghost. “For what?”

He didn’t answer. He just walked away, the weight of the apology settling around him like a second skin.
