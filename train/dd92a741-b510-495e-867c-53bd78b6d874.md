The memory lingers like a half-eaten cookie left too long on the counter—sweet at first, then stale and bitter. It was the night before graduation, when the party was still loud with the hum of laughter and the clink of glasses. You stood at the edge of the crowd, arms wrapped around yourself, staring at your phone screen. A text from an old friend: *"Wish me luck."* The words felt like a punch to the gut. You should have replied. You should have called her back, told her you were sorry. But your hands were numb, your voice hoarse with the effort of hiding how much you wished you’d never left her.

The bar was dim, the air thick with smoke and the tang of spilled whiskey. You ordered a drink, one after another, until the glass was empty and the memory was swallowed by the buzz of the crowd. You told yourself it was just a phase, that she’d get over it. But the way your fingers trembled as you slid the phone into your pocket—*that* was the moment you knew you’d never look back.

By morning, the regret was just another shadow in the hallway, fading as the sun rose over the campus. The diploma was waiting, the future ahead. And so was the guilt, but it was easier to bury it under the weight of success, the noise of life moving on. You never told anyone. Not even yourself.
