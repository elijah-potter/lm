In a world where time was not linear but flowed like liquid mercury, the clock hands of the Great Clock Tower in Eldermere pulsed with forgotten memories. At midnight, it whispered to the city’s forgotten souls, offering them a choice: the second hand, a serpent coiled around its edge, coiled itself into the hands of those who sought to bend time itself.

The first to dare was Elias Veyne, a scholar who had spent decades chasing the past through dusty tomes. He touched the second hand and felt it *burn*—not with fire, but with the weight of centuries. The moment his fingers closed around it, the hourglass beside him inverted, spilling sand not into the past, but into a future he could see: his own grave, filled with the bones of men who had tried what he had done. He let go.

The clock’s chime echoed like a dying breath. By dawn, the second hand had returned to its place, but Elias’s shadow stretched long into the future, a flicker of what might have been.

The clock’s gears ground on, unyielding. Some said it was the universe’s way of reminding us that time is not a gift to hoard, but a river to swim in—waves that carry us forward, whether we resist or not.
