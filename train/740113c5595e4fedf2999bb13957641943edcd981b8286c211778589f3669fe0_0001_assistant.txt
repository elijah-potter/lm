After Alexander, the Great died in 323 BCE, three of his generals fought over the Middle East. Antiochus III took full control in 198 BCE and brought Judea into his empire. Though initially granting Jews religious autonomy, he eventually tried to force Hellenism on the Jews. Jewish opposition forced him to back down, but his son Antiochus IV enforced the policy upon inheriting the throne in 176 BCE. He outlawed the Sabbath and circumcision and defiled the Temple by erecting an altar to Zeus where pigs were sacrificed, all things abhorrent to Jews.

Antiochus' extremism united the people, until eventually a Greek official attempted to force a Jew named Matthias Hasmonean to make a sacrifice to a pagan god, and Matthias murdered the official instead. Antiochus retaliated, but the Jews fought back for liberation behind Mattathias and his five sons who became known as the Maccabees (the Hebrew word for “hammer”). Underestimating Jewish resolve and strength, Antiochus sent a small force to quelch the rebellion only to have them annihilated. He then took a powerful army into battle only to suffer the same result. The Maccabees recaptured Jerusalem in 164 BCE, an event retold on the holiday of Chanukah as its origin story.

Two decades later the Maccabees forced the Greeks out of Judea and won their independence. During the next 80 years, Judea, regained most of the land that was once part of Solomon's realm and Jewish life flourished. In the end, internal strife and the rise of another empire ended Jewish independence for nearly two centuries.
