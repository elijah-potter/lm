In the quiet afternoons of a city that never slept, there was a café where the coffee was too bitter and the silence too thick—yet always, just once, the barista would pause and whisper, *"You’re right about the light."* She never knew who said it first, only that it felt like the only honest thing in a world that preferred its answers polished.

One evening, a stranger sat across from me at a table in the corner. Their fingers traced the rim of their empty cup, and for a moment, the world held its breath. They said, *"I used to think I was the one who chose the way I felt. Turns out, I was just the one who forgot to unlearn it."* Their eyes lingered on my hands, knotted with the same quiet tension. I reached across the table and pressed my palm against theirs. There was no spark, just the dull ache of something unspoken.

*"What if,"* they murmured, *"the truth isn’t a single word, but the space between the ones we don’t say?"* The café door creaked open, letting in a gust of rain. The barista’s voice cut through the silence: *"Next order’s on the house."* They stood, and I followed, the weight of what we’d almost said pressing against my ribs like an old scar.

I never saw them again, but sometimes, when the wind howls through the empty streets, I swear I hear them laughing—not with joy, but with the exhaustion of finally admitting something too small to name. The light outside the café window was wrong tonight, as if the world had chosen to be wrong too.
