In a significant setback to the private space sector, the Japanese moon lander Hakuto-R found itself hurtling to an untimely end on the lunar surface due to an unfortunate mishap with its altitude sensor. This catastrophic event transpired in late April during its landmark landing attempt when the sensor, confused by the lip of a lunar crater, led the lander into a fatal nosedive.

The spacecraft's creators, the Tokyo-based company ispace, uncovered the harsh reality of this unfortunate event, pinpointing an unanticipated topographical feature as the culprit. The onboard computer of the moon lander, thrown off by the unexpected terrain, had erroneously dismissed the altitude readings. Instead, it relied on a pre-calculated estimate of its altitude relevant to the mission timeline. Consequently, the computer believed the spacecraft was closer to the moon than its actual position, resulting in the disastrous crash on April 25.

According to an ispace statement released on Friday (May 26), "The lander gauged its altitude as zero, interpreting it to be on the lunar surface, only to later establish that it was, in reality, hovering approximately 5 km [3.1 miles] above the lunar surface." The descent continued to post the expected landing time until the propulsion system depleted, causing a loss of controlled descent. It's believed to have fallen freely to the moon's surface after that.

Ispace acknowledged during a press briefing that a lack of adequate consideration for the surrounding terrain of the landing site factored into the mission's downfall. A late switch in the landing site, just months before the mission's liftoff, was identified as a contributing factor.

The Hakuto-R, which had launched aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket in December 2022, aimed to touch down in the 54-mile-wide (87 km) Atlas Crater in the moon's Mare Frigoris ("Sea of Cold") region. However, a recent sighting by NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter revealed the wreckage of the Hakuto-R near its intended landing site.

This mission had it has been successful, would have crowned Hakuto-R as the first privately-owned moon lander to achieve a lunar landing. Until now, only NASA, China, and Russia have managed to land spacecraft softly on the moon.

Ispace assured that despite this misfortune, the mission managed to fulfill eight out of nine of its objectives. The firm further stated that the unfortunate event would not hamper the scheduled launches of their second and third missions in 2024 and 2025, respectively.
