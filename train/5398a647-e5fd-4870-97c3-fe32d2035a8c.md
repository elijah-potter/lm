The morning of the first routine was clear—dry pavement, crisp air, and a clock striking 7:13. The alarm had gone off twice before I rolled out of bed, my fingers still numb from the night’s work. The uniform was already draped over the chair, the boots polished to a dull sheen, and the badge clipped into the pocket of my coat. I adjusted the tie, checked the pocket watch one last time, and stepped outside into the quiet.

The station was empty. No one else moved, no voices carried through the open windows. The air smelled faintly of stale coffee and old leather. I took a deep breath, exhaled slowly, and walked toward the counter. The guard at the desk, a man in his fifties with a permanent five o’clock shadow, barely glanced up as I approached.

"You’re late," he said, not unkindly.

"I’m always on time," I replied, though my feet felt heavier than usual. The weight of the past few days pressed against my chest. He signed, then slid a fresh mug of coffee toward me. "Here. Start with this. You need the caffeine."

The routine was simple: check the roll call, verify the locks, confirm the alarms. But this time, something felt off. The guard’s eyes lingered on me for too long, and I knew—something had changed. The first routine was always the hardest, but this one was different. The weight of the past felt heavier, and the weight of the future felt too close to bear. I finished my coffee, stood, and said goodbye. The station was empty again.
