A half-built city of skeletal structures stands in a forgotten valley, where time has eroded the edges of its promise. The skeletal frames of unfinished skyscrapers loom like ghostly silhouettes against a sky choked with dust, their windows still blank, their floors unlaid. Bridges, once grand arches, now droop like old bones, their cables frayed and rusted, the gaps between them wide enough to swallow a man whole.

The streets, once bustling, are now quiet lanes lined with overgrown weeds that claw at cracked pavement. Sidewalks, once smooth, are now splintered paths where children’s laughter echoes in faded echoes. Buildings, their facades still unpainted, bear the scars of neglect—peeling plaster, exposed wiring, and the slow, creeping rot of forgotten things.

In the center of the valley, a plaza where once markets thrived now holds a single, abandoned statue of a figure with arms outstretched, its face frozen mid-scream. The air smells of damp earth and old wood, the only sound the distant, irregular crunch of footsteps—or perhaps the rustle of leaves.

Here, the unfinished is not just the absence of completion; it’s the weight of what could have been, the silence where voices once sang, the emptiness where joy once lived. The place breathes, but not with life—with the slow, inevitable decay of everything left to do.
