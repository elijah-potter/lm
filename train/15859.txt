Many ivies grow leaves of several shades, including green, white and pink, which are called variegated in the gardening world. Whether you are growing variegated varieties of English ivy (Hedera helix), hardy in U.S. Department of Agriculture plant hardiness zones 5 to 9, or another ivy species such as Algerain ivy (Hedera algeriensis), hardy in USDA plant hardiness zones 7 through 10, ivies often tolerate both the sun and shade. They also grow well as ground covers or along walls, fences or trellises.
Cover the garden area with three or four inches of organic matter and work it into the soil about 8 inches deep with a tiller, garden rake or spade before planting the ivy. Compost, rotting leaves and peat moss work well to amend the soil, improving its drainage and fertility so variegated ivies can thrive.
Water ivy about once a week to keep the soil moist, especially the first year after planting and when the weather is hot and dry. Water it well with about one inch of water so it thoroughly moistens the soil. Water in the morning so the foliage dries before nightfall to prevent fungal diseases.
Apply a 1- or 2-inch layer of mulch, such as wood chips, around the ivy plants to help conserve soil moisture.
Remove death growth, fallen leaves and debris with a rake and pruning shears in late winter or early spring just as the vine starts to put forth new growth. Be careful with the rake so you do not damage the plant. Use a leaf blower if the area is extremely matted and difficult to clean.
Prune the vine back in spring to keep it within a confined area or to shape it. In general, you can prune up to two-thirds of the last year's growth, recommends the University of Illinois. Discard the trimmings.
Spread 1/4 cup of a granular, balanced fertilizer, such as one with a nutrient ratio of 12-12-12, around each ivy plant in spring after pruning the plant. Water the area with an inch of water. If any fertilizer landed on the foliage, wash it off with water.
Rinse both sides of the foliage when watering the plant in the morning to help prevent a severe infestation of spider mites, which are the most common pests to inflict ivies.
Prune stems that have turned yellow from spider mites. When the plant becomes infested with mites -- evident by fine webbing in the vine, yellow-spotted leaves and a lot of yellowing leaves -- spray the leaves with an insecticidal soap labeled for mites. Ensure you spray the underside of the foliage where mites often lurk.
