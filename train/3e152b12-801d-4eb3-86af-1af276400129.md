The first time Liam felt the weight of the old oak tree pressing down on his shoulders, he didn’t think about the storm clouds rolling in. He thought about his phone battery at 12%, the way his backpack felt heavier than usual, and the half-eaten sandwich in his lunchbox that he’d forgotten to eat. But then the wind howled like a stray dog, rattling the windows of the cafeteria, and the power flickered. The last thing he heard before the lights died was Mrs. Henderson yelling at someone about spilling soda on the floor.

The dark didn’t last long. Liam had learned to navigate the school’s maze of hallways by memory, but tonight, the shadows seemed thicker, the air colder. He passed the gym, where the basketball hoop hung limply against the wall, and the old bleachers groaned under the weight of the storm. The only sound was his own breath, sharp and uneven, and the distant thunder that had turned the sky a sickly green.

He reached the stairs leading to the roof, his sneakers splashing through puddles of water that hadn’t been there before. The storm had flooded the school’s roof drains, and now, the basement was a flood zone. The emergency lights flickered on and off like dying stars. Liam’s hands trembled as he climbed the final steps, the metal railing cold against his palms. He grabbed the edge of the roof, his fingers biting into the metal, and stepped out into the night.

The world was a different kind of dark—no longer the dark of the school, but the dark of something vast and untamed. The wind screamed through the broken windows of the school, and the rain came down in sheets, turning the street below into a river of silver. Liam pressed his back against the cold concrete, his breath misting in the air. He was alone, but not really. The storm had brought out the other kids too, sneaking out of broken windows, crouching in the rain like shadows.

One of them, a girl with wild dark hair and a hood pulled over her face, spotted him. She didn’t move, didn’t speak, just watched him with eyes that seemed to see right through him. Liam hesitated, then called out, “Hey.”

She didn’t answer. Instead, she raised her hand, fingers spread like claws, and pointed at him. Liam flinched, but the girl didn’t seem to notice. She was too busy watching the sky, where the storm seemed to be alive, twisting and turning like a giant’s hand.

“You’re the last one,” she said, her voice barely audible over the wind.

Liam swallowed. “I—I don’t know what you mean.”

“You’re the one who always stays,” she said, her voice dropping to a whisper. “Even when it’s not your turn. Even when no one else wants to.”

Liam’s stomach twisted. He didn’t know what she was talking about. But then he remembered. The year before, when the school had been holding a science fair, and Liam had stayed late to help fix a broken display. He’d been the only one there when the power had failed. The girl had been there too, but she’d just stood in the corner, watching.

“You’re the one who fixes things,” she said. “The one who doesn’t give up.”

Liam didn’t know what to say. He didn’t know if she was right. But for the first time, he felt something in his chest that wasn’t fear. It was curiosity.

The storm raged on, the wind howling like a beast, but Liam didn’t move. He stood there, under the broken skylight, watching the sky. The girl didn’t leave. She stayed, her hand still raised, as if waiting for something.

Then, the lights came back on.

Liam jumped, startled. The girl didn’t react. She just stood there, her eyes still fixed on the storm outside. The school was silent, the emergency lights casting long, wavering shadows. The girl was the only one who hadn’t flinched.

Liam’s phone buzzed in his pocket. He pulled it out, startled. A text from his dad: *"Where are you? It’s after 7 PM."*

Liam typed a quick reply: *"At the roof. Storm."*

He didn’t wait for a response. He stuffed his phone back in his pocket and turned to the girl. She was still there, unmoving. He hesitated, then stepped closer.

“You’re the one who stays,” he said. “But why?”

She didn’t answer. Instead, she reached out, her fingers brushing his wrist. Liam flinched, but the touch was gentle, almost like a caress. She smiled, and for the first time, Liam saw the light in her eyes.

“Because,” she said, her voice soft, “we’re not done yet.”

The storm raged on, but Liam didn’t move. He stood there, under the broken skylight, watching the sky. The girl was right. They weren’t done yet. And whatever was waiting for them out there, it wasn’t afraid of the dark.
