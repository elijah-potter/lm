Recently, researchers concluded that a huge eagle that once stalked New Zealand’s forests was a fierce predator that may have hunted humans – Anna Davison rounds up other ancient airborne behemoths
Haast's huge eagles
, Harpagornis moorei
- Wingspan: 3 metres
- Found in New Zealand until about 500 years ago
- Related to modern-day eagles
New Zealand never had many native land mammals – no tigers, wolves, or other predatory mammals – just a few diminutive bats. Before humans arrived, its forests bustled with birds, and the enormous Haast's Eagle once ruled them all.
Its wingspan of 3 m wasn't a big stretch, but at 18 kg (40 lbs), Haast's Eagle was a good deal heavier than any eagle alive today, making it the largest that ever lived.

Researchers recently used CAT scans of the eagle's bones to compare its brain, nervous system and sensory organs to those of modern-day birds of prey to figure out whether it was a scavenger or a predator. Their conclusion? Haast's Eagle was a skilled killer that could take down a moa – a flightless bird, now extinct, that could be twice the height of a man (see previous slide). It might also have preyed on humans.
Haast's Eagle lurked in trees, launching itself at a potential meal at speeds of up to 80 km/h, plunging its formidable talons deep into its victim's flesh – or further; some moa bones bear telltale puncture marks.

Giant flying reptiles
- Wingspan: 10 metres
- Widespread in the northern hemisphere during the Late Cretaceous, 65-100 million years ago
- Closest living relatives: crocodiles
Some of the largest animals ever to have taken to the skies were leggy reptiles called azhdarchids – pterosaurs as tall as a giraffe, outfitted with membranous wings stretching more than 10 m across. These massive animals may have used all four limbs – their hind legs and their folded wings – to vault into the air.
The lifestyle of these toothless monsters has long been debated. They were first thought to have lived like vultures, scavenging fetid flesh from rotting carcasses, then came the theory that they thrust their big beaks into the ground in search of snacks. Later they were described as living like modern seabirds, swooping over lakes and oceans and scooping fish from the water. Not so, according to recent studies of fossils and footprints, which suggest that azhdarchids preferred to feed on foot, like modern-day storks, searching for bite-sized baby dinosaurs.
(Image: Mark Witton and Darren Naish)
Birds as big as planes
, Argentavis magnificens
- Wingspan: 7 metres
- Found in Argentina during the Upper Miocene, about 6 million years ago
- Related to modern-day vultures and storks
This Cessna-sized predator – the largest known flying bird – once soared over the open plains of Argentina and the foothills of the Andes, but scientists have long wondered how it ever achieved liftoff.
Using a model originally developed by helicopter designers, researchers figured out how the behemoth bird flew, and how it took off and landed. It was almost certainly too heavy to stay aloft with constant flapping, so it must have been a glider, they concluded, which hitched a ride on updrafts from wind currents and had a cruising speed of 67 km/h.
Argentavis probably took off by running down a gentle slope into a light headwind, much like hang-glider pilots do. Landing would have been treacherous for the teratorn, but it managed by lowering its sizeable feet to slow itself, and angling its wings so they acted as a kind of parachute.
(Image: Stanton F. Fink)
, Meganeuropsis permiana
- Wingspan: >70 centimetres
- Found in the US during the Early Permian, about 300 million years ago
- Ancestors of dragonflies
Back when the Earth was a lot balmier and leafier, insects the size of eagles hovered above the prehistoric swamps of the Midwestern US, poised to snap up a meal of flies or even an ancient amphibian.
There was more oxygen in the air back then – about 35 per cent, compared to the 21 per cent that's floating around now. That may have enabled insects, which take in air through a network of tubes branching into their tissues, to evolve to relatively humungous sizes.
The largest known insect, Meganeuropsis permiana, which resembled a dragonfly, had a wingspan of more than 70 cm and weighed as much as a crow.
The mother of all geese
, Dasornis emuinus
- Wingspan: 5 metres
- Found in England during the Lower Eocene, about 50 million years ago
- Related to modern-day ducks and geese
This huge goose-like creature with a 5-metre wingspan lived an albatross-like existence, skimming across the waters that once covered southern England in search of fish and squid.
It had more bite than any bird alive today, thanks to the sharp projections arranged along the edge of its beak. These pseudo-teeth, which were made of keratin, like the beaks of living birds and human fingernails and hair, helped Dasornis get a good grip on its slippery prey.
