The garden at the end of the street was slightly neglected—mostly because the last gardener had retired, and the new one had yet to arrive. The roses, once vibrant, now leaned weakly toward the sun like elderly patients in a fading light show. The lawn, once a patchwork of emerald, now bore the scars of foot traffic and the occasional, well-meaning, but poorly timed attempt at "improvement" by neighbors who mistook it for a wildflower meadow.

The neighbor’s dog, a scruffy terrier with a perpetually damp nose, spent its days digging holes in the dirt, as if searching for a long-lost treasure or simply testing the resilience of the earth. The tree in the corner, a stubborn oak with a crown that had long since outgrown its roots, dropped leaves like a man dropping old habits—one by one, and never with much ceremony.

In the evenings, the family who lived there—just the mother and her three children—would sit on the porch, their faces half-lit by the glow of the streetlamp. The mother would hum an old folk song, her voice a gentle murmur, while the children played with toys that had seen better days. They didn’t notice the cracks in the floorboards or the way the air smelled faintly of damp and old wood. They didn’t notice the garden either, really, because to them, it was just there. A place. A small, slightly dusty, but otherwise unremarkable place where the world paused for a moment.
