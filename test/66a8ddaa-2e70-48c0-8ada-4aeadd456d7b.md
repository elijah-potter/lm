The next morning, Mia sat at her desk, her notebook open to the half-finished drawing of a dragon she’d been working on for weeks. The ink had dried into jagged lines, and the colors were fading—almost like the dragon itself was fading away. She pushed it aside and grabbed her science textbook instead, flipping to the chapter on climate change. The words blurred as she read, but she knew the numbers: 1.5 degrees Celsius. The Earth was on the brink.

That night, Mia’s phone buzzed. It was her friend Leo, his message simple: *"We need to move fast. The air quality report says we’re at 90% PM2.5 today. That’s not safe."* She didn’t need to ask why. The smog had started rolling in again, thicker than last time, coating the streets in a gray haze that made her cough. Her grandma had warned her about this before—*"Kids should be outside, not inside. But the air’s poison."* Now, the air was poison.

Mia grabbed her backpack and ran to the park. The usual laughter of kids playing was muffled. She scanned the area, her eyes adjusting to the dim light. A group of older kids were huddled near a rusted bench, whispering. One of them—a boy named Raj—spotted her and waved. "Come on," he said. "We’re organizing a cleanup." Before she could ask what, he pulled out a spray bottle filled with water and a handful of trash bags. "We’re picking up litter. The city’s doing something about the pollution, but they need help." Mia hesitated, then nodded.

They worked in shifts. Mia and Raj filled bags with plastic wrappers and cigarette butts, while the others collected broken glass and discarded electronics. The smell of decaying trash mixed with the faint metallic tang of the smog. By sunset, the park was cleaner, but the air still tasted like rust and smoke. As they packed up, Raj handed her a folded note. It was from their teacher, a warning: *"School’s canceled tomorrow. Stay indoors. Wear a mask."*

That night, Mia lay in bed, staring at the ceiling. The dragon from her notebook felt heavier now, like it was trying to tell her something. She pulled out her phone and scrolled through the news. The headlines read: *"Local Protests Demand Action on Pollution."* Below it, a photo of a group of kids standing in front of a city hall with signs that read *"Clean Air Now."* Her breath hitched. She typed a message to Leo: *"We have to do more."* Then she added: *"I’m going to the protest."* The message sent before she could think twice.

The protest was at city hall, where a sea of kids and adults stood under a sky choked with smog. Banners fluttered in the weak breeze. Mia stood with Raj and the others, holding a sign that read *"We Demand Clean Air."* A woman in a suit approached them, her face lined with exhaustion. "We’ve been hearing about this for years," she said. "But until the city listens, we keep fighting." Mia clenched her fists. She wasn’t ready to give up.

Later, as the protest ended, Mia and her friends split into groups. Raj told her, *"Tomorrow, we’re organizing a march. We’ll meet at the park at dawn."* Mia nodded, her hands trembling. The dragon from her notebook was still there, but this time, it felt like a promise. She’d keep fighting. For the air. For her future. And for the kids who would come after her.
