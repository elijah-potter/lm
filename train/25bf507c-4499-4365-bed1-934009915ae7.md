The alarm buzzed at 06:17. Coffee was poured too cold, the morning light was already golden through the curtains, but the day had already slipped into a lazy rhythm. A quick shower—water running longer than necessary—left the skin still damp by the time the kitchen door opened, revealing a plate of cereal half-eaten from yesterday. The laptop sat unopened on the counter, its screen reflecting the dim glow of the fridge.

The first task was to decide what to wear: a hoodie that smelled faintly of last night’s takeout, or the faded jeans and sneakers that had seen better days. The latter won. The commute was longer than usual—traffic had doubled, and the subway was packed with people who looked like they’d slept in the same clothes as they had the day before. A coffee from a drive-thru, the kind that came in a disposable cup that would dissolve in the rain before the day ended.

By 11:30, the office was already buzzing with the usual chaos. Emails were left unanswered, meetings were rescheduled, and the boss’s voice echoed through the open door with a note that read: *"Start tomorrow."* A meeting with the team started with a 15-minute discussion about the importance of punctuality, followed by a 45-minute stand-up where no one had any updates.

Lunch was a sad sandwich from the vending machine, eaten at a desk while scrolling through irrelevant news articles. The afternoon dragged through endless meetings, some of them pointless, others so repetitive they felt like they’d been held the week before. By 5 PM, the screen was covered in half-finished work, and the body was exhausted—legs shaking from sitting too long, back aching from slouching over a keyboard.

The evening was a blur. A takeout pizza was consumed at 8:30 PM, text messages were ignored until 11:30, and the bed was only climbed after the alarm for the next day’s late start was set for 06:15. The cycle would repeat.
