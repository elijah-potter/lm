## TIL the "Phantom Islands" of Cartography Were Once Believed to Exist

Today I learned about a fascinating chapter in the history of mapmaking: the phenomenon of “phantom islands.” For centuries, several islands, now known to be entirely fictional, appeared on European maps, persisting despite repeated attempts to locate them.

The most prominent of these was Hy-Brasil, an island supposedly located west of Ireland. It was often depicted as a circular landmass shrouded in mist, appearing and disappearing periodically. Irish folklore attributed various origins to Hy-Brasil, ranging from a mystical land of eternal youth to a place inhabited by a technologically advanced civilization. Other phantom islands included Buss, Som, and Frisland, each with its own elaborate legends and appearances on maps spanning from the 15th to the 19th centuries.

These islands weren’t simply mistakes; cartographers genuinely believed in their existence. The belief stemmed from a combination of factors: unreliable navigational techniques, misinterpretations of observations (such as sea mists or volcanic activity), and a desire to fill in the blank spaces on maps – often driven by commercial interests. Sailors would sometimes report sightings, fueling the myth and incentivizing further expeditions, which invariably failed to locate the elusive lands.

The gradual refinement of navigational tools and more accurate surveying methods eventually exposed these islands as fabrications. While the stories surrounding them remain compelling, the disappearance of Hy-Brasil and its contemporaries from modern maps serves as a poignant reminder of how our understanding of the world has evolved.
