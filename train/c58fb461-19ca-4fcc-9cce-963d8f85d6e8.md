In the dim glow of the flickering streetlight, Elias sat on the cracked pavement, his fingers tracing the scar along his wrist—the one from the knife he’d thrown away years ago. The rain had stopped, but the air still hummed with the ghost of the city’s pulse, a distant heartbeat beneath the rusted neon signs.

He’d been here before, each night, each hour, waiting for the moment the weight in his chest felt lighter. Tonight was different. The letter had arrived—a single sheet of paper, addressed to him in his mother’s handwriting, sealed with a wax stamp he didn’t recognize. The words were half-melted, as if the ink had been written in haste, in a fever.

*"Come back, Elias. Not as you are now. As you were before the fire."*

His breath hitched. The fire had been his father’s last mistake—a night when the gas line had split, and the city had burned down around them, leaving only ash and silence. His mother had said those words once, whispered in the hospital, before the pain took her voice.

He stood abruptly, his boots sinking into the damp earth. The letter slipped from his pocket, forgotten. Outside, the distant wail of a siren cut through the quiet. He adjusted his coat, the fabric stiff with rainwater, and walked toward the neon glow of the old theater on the corner. The sign flickered: *Last show tonight.*

The door creaked open behind him. A figure in a long coat stepped out, their breath fogging in the cold. They were younger than he remembered, their eyes sharp with something like relief.

*"You’re late,"* the stranger said, voice rough. *"The train leaves in ten minutes."*

Elias turned, his pulse hammering. The stranger reached into their coat, pulling out a folded newspaper—today’s edition. The headline glowed in red ink: *"Fire Victims Reunited."*

He didn’t ask. He didn’t need to. The stranger’s fingers brushed his wrist, just for a second, before pulling back. The letter in his pocket felt heavier than before.

The train whistle blew in the distance.

He walked away.

The streetlights blurred behind him, but the city’s face was still there—endless, unyielding, waiting.
