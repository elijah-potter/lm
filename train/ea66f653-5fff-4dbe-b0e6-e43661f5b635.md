A weathered wooden pier extends into a mist-laden bay, its planks groaning under decades of salt and wind. Beneath the eaves, driftwood cradles the remnants of old fishing nets, half-buried in sand. The sea here is a restless mirror—one moment still, the next churning with foam as waves crash against the rocks. The only permanent mark is the tide line, a jagged, shifting boundary that defines the land’s edge.

Driftwood sculptures, weathered by time, lean against the cliffs. Some are half-eroded, others preserved by the tide. Locals say the wind carries stories in the hollows of their bark. The rocks themselves are porous, absorbing stories like water into stone.

A small, unmarked hut stands at the center of the pier, its walls lined with barnacles and barnacles that whisper secrets to the gulls. The roof is patched with scavenged tarps, but the floorboards beneath are solid oak, aged by years of use. Inside, a single lantern flickers, casting long shadows over a worn map of the bay, its edges frayed from countless hands.

The bay itself feels like a living thing—it changes shape with the seasons, yet the same currents and tides run through it every day. The cliffs here are not just rocks; they are the bones of something older than memory, holding the echoes of storms that have long since faded.

The only thing that moves here is the sea, but it does not forget.
