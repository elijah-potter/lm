The first time I realized how much I loved silence, it was after the third glass of whiskey in the dim glow of an abandoned theater’s marquee.

I had come for the cheap seats, the kind that creaked like old bones underfoot. The audience—mostly strangers in their own stories—had started to murmur, their voices weaving together like threads of a blanket too thick to escape. I sat alone at the back, the only one who hadn’t ordered a drink yet, because I was already drunk on the idea of being *unheard*.

Then, as the storyteller began, I leaned forward. The words were thin, the laughter hollow, but I listened anyway. And when the narrator paused, mid-sentence, to cough into their palm, I understood.

I had never meant to confess anything. But by the time the story ended, I had told the truth anyway: that I loved it when people didn’t look at me. That I preferred the weight of unspoken things over the sharp edge of expectation. That the best stories weren’t the ones you heard, but the ones you let yourself believe.

The room had fallen silent. The narrator wiped their eyes, though no one had cried. I stood up, my chair scraping the floor, and left before anyone could ask what I’d been thinking.

Now, every night, I find myself in the same theater, waiting for the next story to begin. Because sometimes, the truth isn’t what you say. Sometimes, it’s what you stay quiet about.
