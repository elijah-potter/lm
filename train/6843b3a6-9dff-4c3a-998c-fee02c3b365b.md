The late afternoon light slanted through the window of Room 312, painting the sterile walls in hues of amber and rose. I adjusted the IV drip for Mr. Alistair Finch, a gentleman of seventy-eight years, recovering from a fractured hip sustained during a fall at his home. He lay propped up in bed, a worn copy of Dickens’ *Great Expectations* resting on his lap. 

Mr. Finch was a man of quiet dignity. He hadn’t complained once since his admission, though I could see a profound sadness lingering in his eyes. He spoke sparingly, mostly about his late wife, Eleanor, and their shared love of gardening. He’s been meticulously tending a rose garden for over forty years, he told me, and the thought of it neglected was clearly a source of considerable distress. 

“The ‘Peace’ roses are particularly susceptible to black spot this time of year, you know,” he remarked, his voice a low rumble. “Eleanor always knew just what to do.” He paused, his gaze drifting towards the window. “She had a touch. A way with living things.”

I nodded, offering a sympathetic smile. "Gardening is a wonderful passion, Mr. Finch. It brings so much beauty into the world."

He chuckled, a dry, rustling sound. "Indeed. It’s a solace, too. A connection to something enduring.”

My shift was nearing its end, and I was preparing to administer his evening medication. I checked his chart, confirming the dosage and route. “Mr. Finch, I’m going to give you your pain medication now. How is your pain level currently?”

“Manageable, thank you,” he replied. “A slight ache, but nothing I can’t bear.”

As I prepared the injection, I noticed a small, faded photograph tucked inside the Dickens novel. I could see a younger Mr. Finch, beaming beside a vibrant woman with kind eyes and a cascade of silver hair. It was Eleanor, undoubtedly. 

“That’s a lovely photograph,” I observed gently.

He followed my gaze to the picture. “That was taken on our anniversary, thirty years ago. We were visiting the Cotswolds.” A faint smile touched his lips, a brief flicker of joy illuminating his face. “She loved lavender. We planted a whole row of it along the garden path.”

I finished administering the medication and tidied his bedside table. “It sounds like you shared a very special life together, Mr. Finch.”

“We did,” he agreed, his voice softening. “A good life. A full life.” He closed the book, carefully marking his place with a pressed lavender sprig. “Thank you, Nurse Davies. For your kindness.”

“You’re very welcome, Mr. Finch,” I said, offering another reassuring smile. As I left the room, I thought about his rose garden, the lavender-lined path, and the enduring power of love and memory. I made a note in his chart to contact the hospital’s horticultural therapist to discuss the possibility of providing Mr. Finch with some gardening materials to help ease his recovery and reconnect him with a piece of his world. Some wounds, I thought, are best healed with sunshine and soil.
