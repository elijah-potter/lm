Appeasement is a diplomatic policy of making political, material, or territorial concessions to an aggressive power to avoid conflict. In the current political environment, many critics of government action in the world today invoke the term. Leaders in Great Britain during the 1930s were often praised or criticized because of appeasement policies. Much of the population of Great Britain and the rest of Europe, as well as the United States, Canada, and Australia, were strongly in favor of appeasement. The support for appeasement policies was often seen as a reaction to the carnage of World War One. 

In the early 1930s, appeasing concessions were widely seen as positive, but by 1935, many in Great Britain were worried that Hitler was rearming Germany and rebuilding its military. With the total annexation of Austria, referred to as the Anschluss, in March of 1938, real fear began to shift public opinion in Great Britain. Later in 1938, Great Britain and France agreed to Germany's demand for the Sudetenland, part of the country of Czechoslovakia. A mere six months later in  March of 1939, Hitler marched into other parts of Czechoslovakia, the provinces of Bohemia and Moravia, and occupied those. Finally, Hitler invaded Poland on September 1, 1939. All efforts to appease Hitler ended with France and Great Britain declaring war on Germany. This was the beginning of one of the biggest and most brutal wars on earth, World War II.
