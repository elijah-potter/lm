The café’s hum was a steady, white noise—too many voices, too little attention. Across the room, the woman at the corner table sipped her coffee, her fingers tracing the rim of the cup like she was tracing the edges of her own forgetfulness. She missed the way the light slanted through the stained glass, how it split the shadows into golden threads that danced just beyond her peripheral vision. She missed the way the older man at the next table leaned forward at 2:17 AM, his elbows on the table, whispering to himself, his voice a low, rhythmic hum that wasn’t quite a prayer, not quite a confession. She missed the way his breath hitched, the way his knuckles went white as he clenched them into fists.

I watched her for a long time. She never noticed. The barista’s laughter, the clink of cutlery, the way the door creaked open and shut like a promise left unfulfilled—none of it registered. She was too busy counting the minutes between her caffeine jitters and the next existential dread that always found her when the world stopped being a place to live in, and became something to outlast.

I noticed the way the light caught the gold in her hair at 4 PM, how it turned her into something like a living constellation. I noticed the way the older man’s fingers drummed against the table like a metronome counting down to the end of something. I noticed the way the café’s walls were lined with faded ads for a lost generation, their messages half-erased by time, their promises unfulfilled.

She was the kind of person who forgot to breathe. I was the kind of person who remembered. And that, I realized, was the difference between seeing and being seen.
