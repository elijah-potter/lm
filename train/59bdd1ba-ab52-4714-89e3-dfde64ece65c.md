The first time rain fell on dry stone after months of drought, its sound like a hymn of forgotten gods. The crackling of each droplet on the cracked pavement, the way the air hummed with a quiet electricity, the scent of ozone and damp earth—none of it could be replicated. The way the light bent through the drizzle, painting everything in a fleeting, golden haze. That moment was not just a snapshot; it was a whisper of time bending back on itself, a memory etched in the skin of the world that would never play again. The way the old woman at the market laughed as her tea spilled, her fingers brushing the rim of the porcelain before it shattered—her voice, her touch, the laughter itself, all gone before the sun could rise. Time does not give seconds back. It only steals them, and what remains is the hollow ache of what could have been.
