Remember when kids commonly rode around in cars with their noses pressed to the windshield? Or bounced around in the back of a station wagon?
In a car crash, such children became human cannonballs, suffering injuries or death.
To cut the carnage, all 50 states have passed laws that require infants and young children to ride in car seats.
For families on the road, a safe and properly installed car seat - which is used every time - may be their most essential piece of child gear.
Still, car crashes remain a leading cause of death of children older than age 1 in the U.S., according to Linda Galer, the assistant director of the Washington State Safety Restraint Coalition.
Parents should make an iron-clad rule that their children always use a car seat or seat belt, says Galer, whichever is appropriate for their weight and age.
"Be consistent. Don't let them out even once. They may complain and gripe, but if they realize they won't win, they will stop and find something else that will drive you equally nuts," said Galer.
"And what parent, if she or he knew that today was the day of the big car crash, wouldn't put a child in the best protection available? You just have to do that every day. You don't know who's on the road with you, driving too fast or not paying attention."
For parents, getting the right car seat and installing it correctly can be complicated. The Safety Restraint Coalition's 8-page brochure, Protecting Your Dreams, can help. It describes the various types of car seats and shows how seat belts can be made safer for older children who are too big for a car seat. Among the advice:
-- When buying a car seat (new or second-hand), look for a sticker on the side or back of the seat. It should state that the seat was manufactured after Jan. 1, 1981 (seats made since then have been crash-tested and approved) and that it complies with the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard (FMVSS) 213.
-- Use an infant car seat from birth until the child weighs about 17 to 20 pounds. The seat should always face the rear of the vehicle for protection of the child's neck and head. Never place it in the front seat if the car has air bags; the infant's head would be too close to the air bag if it deploys. (In general, the safest place for any car seat or booster seat is in the middle of the back seat.)
-- As a child gets older and heavier (from about 17-20 pounds up to 40-43 pounds) use a car seat that faces forward. Keep the child in the car seat as long as possible, even though legally from age 3 in Washington only a seat belt is required. A car seat offers far more protection than a seat belt.
-- When a child is too big for a car seat (the child's shoulders are too broad for it or the child's ears reach the top of the car seat), consider a car-booster seat. It positions the seat belt correctly over the child's body and is safer than just a seat belt.
-- When attaching a car seat with your vehicle's seat belt, make sure that the seat belt stays tight. Many newer and foreign cars have the kind of shoulder/lap seatbelts that have a free-sliding latch plate and tighten only in a crash or a sudden stop. Such a belt won't keep a car seat in a safe position.
A locking clip must be used on such seat belts; clips often come with many new car seats or can be obtained through the manufacturer or the Safety Restraint Coalition.
Carry a locking clip with you when renting a car; not all car-rental agencies have them available.
-- For protection of older children who have outgrown booster seats, cars can be retrofitted with shoulder belts or shoulder harnesses. There's also a simple device available that repositions the shoulder portion of a belt (called the SafeFit Shoulder Belt Adjustor and also useful for small-size adults).
Although the law requires that children be in a seat belt or car seat, for some parents the old ways linger.
