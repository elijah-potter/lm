The first mistake came on a Tuesday, when Elias accidentally spilled black coffee on his grandmother’s prized antique clock—its gold hands and ornate brass face now caked in a sticky stain. He wiped at it with a damp rag, only to realize the clock had stopped. His grandmother’s sigh was the only sound as she set it down on the mantel.

She had spent twenty years fixing clocks, teaching him the patience to align gears and the precision to tune chimes. This was the first time he’d made it worse. That night, he told himself he’d replace it—tomorrow, after he learned the right way. But by the next morning, the clock had become his obsession.

He spent hours dismantling it, tracing the path of the gears with a pencil. The problem wasn’t the oil—it was the loose hinge. He needed a new bearing, something stronger than the original. His grandmother’s shop was closed, but the library had a catalog of mechanical parts. He scribbled notes on scraps of paper, memorizing the weight of each component.

By the third day, he had the parts: a ball-bearing from an old bicycle, a brass washers salvaged from an old radio, and a thin sheet of copper to reinforce the hinge. He spent the afternoon welding them in place, his hands shaking as he adjusted the weight distribution. The clock didn’t work—yet.

His grandmother arrived early the next morning, coffee in hand. When she saw him, she didn’t scold. Instead, she handed him a toolbox. “You’re fixing it,” she said. “But you’ll learn.”

She taught him the second mistake: *over-tightening*. The clock rattled when he closed the lid. She showed him how to listen for the right *click* of the mechanism. That night, he worked until his fingers ached, the clock clattering in its stand as he adjusted the tension.

On the seventh day, the clock chimed for the first time in months. It wasn’t perfect—there was still a faint hum—but it worked. His grandmother smiled. “Now,” she said, “you know what you did wrong.”

The clock became his legacy. He kept it in the attic, where the dust settled between the chimes. Sometimes, when the wind howled, he’d listen to it. Not as a mistake. As a beginning.
