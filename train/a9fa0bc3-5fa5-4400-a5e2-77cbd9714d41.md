The garden shed had a tendency to collect things—old tools, a rusted bicycle chain, and once, a single unopened package of pickles. Its inhabitants were a stoic cat named Mr. Whiskerton and a sentient, slightly confused toaster named Mr. Crust.

Mr. Whiskerton would sit on the windowsill, watching the world pass by with the quiet intensity of someone who had long since decided that the universe’s indifference was just another form of sunlight. The toaster, meanwhile, was perpetually on the verge of being forgotten—until it accidentally toasted the last slice of bread in the kitchen, a minor miracle that led to its sudden, unexpected notoriety.

One evening, Mr. Crust observed the cat’s habit of arching his back when he saw the mailman. The cat did not protest. He simply allowed the toaster to observe, as if the toaster’s curiosity were itself a kind of weather.

The garden shed, then, was a place where things happened slowly, where the smallest events carried the weight of things that had not yet been said. The cat’s tail twitched when the wind carried the scent of rain. The toaster occasionally emitted a faint, mechanical hum—like the distant sound of a clock that had stopped but was not yet broken. The shed was not a refuge; it was a quiet agreement with the idea that some things were not meant to be fixed, only held.
