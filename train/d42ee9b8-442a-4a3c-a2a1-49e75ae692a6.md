The grand chandelier swayed in a deliberate, hypnotic rhythm, its crystals catching the fading light like scattered diamonds. The air hummed with an unnatural silence, the usual rush of conversation muffled as if filtered through a fog of memory. A woman in an emerald dress stood frozen at the center of the ballroom, her fingers tracing the edge of a half-empty glass of champagne, the liquid inside swirling in slow, deliberate spirals.

A man in a tuxedo approached, his steps heavy, each one a labored motion. He extended a hand, but she barely noticed, her gaze fixed on the wall behind him, where the clock’s hands seemed to crawl like insects. Time was not just passing—it was *stretched*, every second a vast, unyielding stretch of agony and wonder.

The music, when it came, was a whisper—just a few notes, then silence again. The dancers had stopped moving, their bodies still frozen in poses of suspended motion, as if caught in a single, endless frame. The woman’s breath hitched, and for a moment, she could hear it—the sound of her own heartbeat, a drumbeat that thrummed through her skull, a drum of endless, unbearable rhythm.

Outside, the city’s neon signs flickered to life, their colors bleeding together in a kaleidoscope of static. The woman’s reflection in the floor-to-ceiling mirrors seemed to follow her movements, but only in her own time. She turned, and the mirror showed her a stranger—a woman in a dress of black lace, her eyes hollow, her smile a mask of something far older than she.

The air smelled of ozone and copper, the scent of a world that had been left behind. A child’s laughter drifted from the shadows, but it was muffled, distorted, as if heard through a funnel of years. The woman’s hand trembled as she reached for the champagne, but the glass was cold, the liquid inside frozen mid-spiral.

Then, the music started again—not as a melody, but as a chorus of whispers, each voice a different era, each word a fragment of a thousand memories. The dancers moved, not as they had before, but as if responding to a language neither of them understood. The woman clutched her chest, her vision swimming. The world blurred into a wash of color and sound, a storm of light and memory.

The clock struck midnight—not in the usual crisp chime, but as a thousand bells tolling at once, their voices drowning in the static of the city. The woman’s body sagged, her knees hitting the floor. The champagne glass shattered into a thousand pieces, the shards embedding themselves in the floorboards like tiny daggers.

She was not here anymore. The world around her was still, a snapshot of a moment that had never happened. The mirrors reflected nothing but the void. The city, once alive, was now a silent, hollow shell. And somewhere, beyond the reach of time, she was waiting.
