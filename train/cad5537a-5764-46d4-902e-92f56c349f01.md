The clock struck midnight, and the city’s hum shifted from the usual din of traffic to a hollow silence punctuated by distant car horns. I stood on the rooftop, fingers tracing the cold metal railing, my breath curling in the frigid air. Below, the park’s lampposts flickered to life, casting long shadows across the pavement. I watched as the first of the new year’s resolutions appeared—on sticky notes pinned to lampposts, doors, even the underside of benches. A promise to lose weight. Another to learn a language. One to "find myself."

Then I noticed the pattern.

The notes always started with a similar sentiment: *"This year, I want..."* followed by a vague, aspirational goal. The handwriting was usually neat, though sometimes sloppy—almost as if the writer was rushing to finish before the clock struck. The post-it pads were reused, the notes scribbled over and redrawn. I counted seven lampposts in the park’s central square. Five had notes. Two had none.

The second day, I moved to the alley behind the bakery. The same pattern: notes on the fire escape, the doorframe of the hardware store, the side of the deli window. The same structure. The same urgency. The same *failure* to stick to the details.

I traced the notes with my eyes, cataloging the variations: one person wrote *"I’ll stop drinking"* in bold, another’s was a half-hearted *"I’ll go to the gym"* with a question mark. The most common phrase? *"This time, I’ll do it differently."*

By the fourth day, I had a list: the locations, the themes, the recurring words. The lampposts, the alley, the bakery door. The words *"I’ll," "try," "start"*—always in the same tense, always the same futility. The notes were like the city’s own version of New Year’s resolutions, but without the glitter. Just words and hope, left to fade under the city’s relentless rhythm.

I stood there, staring at the notes, and realized: the pattern wasn’t just in the words. It was in the *emptiness* between them. The space where the resolutions would unravel, where the plans would collapse under the weight of the day-to-day. The city didn’t care. The lampposts didn’t judge. The notes were just… notes. The rest was noise.

I lit a cigarette, exhaled smoke into the night. The city’s light show began—cars passing, distant laughter, the distant rumble of a train. And for the first time, I didn’t feel the weight of the notes. I felt the rhythm of the world, untouched by my own small, fleeting attempt to change anything.

The notes would be gone by dawn. The city would keep turning. And somewhere, someone would write another, because that’s what they did.
