The first draft was a failure, but the second one held promise. The characters were too rigid, their emotions flattened by the need to conform to a story that had already been mapped out. The narrator had begun with a bold assertion: *"This is how it should be."* But the second version whispered, *"Perhaps not."*

The scene of the abandoned library was too static—every detail felt like a photograph rather than a living memory. The narrator decided to break the fourth wall, to let the reader see the hesitation, the half-formed doubts that had never been allowed to bloom in the first draft. The pages began to crackle with the sound of a mind that had finally decided to question itself.

The protagonist’s voice, once unyielding, now carried a tremor. *"I thought I knew what I wanted,"* the narrator mused aloud, as if addressing the reader directly. But the reader only saw the ink bleeding onto the page, the words dissolving into something softer, something more honest.

The ending was left ambiguous—not because the narrator had forgotten, but because they had chosen to remember. The last line, written in a smaller font, read: *"I still don’t know what I wanted."* The rest of the story was the space between the words.
