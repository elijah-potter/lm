The school bell rang, signaling another day of classes, but for twelve-year-old Mateo, it marked the start of something far more exciting than algebra. His backpack was packed with his usual supplies—glasses, a half-eaten granola bar, and a small notebook where he kept his weirdest thoughts. Today, though, he had an extra item: a crumpled piece of paper with a strange equation scrawled on it, *E = mc²* in his dad’s messy handwriting.

Mateo had seen that equation before—he’d looked it up on YouTube once, though he didn’t really understand it. But today, his mom had found it in his room while folding laundry, her eyebrows raised. “Why are you writing equations in your notebook?” she’d asked, voice tight.

Mateo had shrugged. “I don’t know. It just came to me.”

His mom had stared at him like he was the one who needed explaining. “Maybe you should talk to someone,” she’d said, and that was when Mateo knew he’d done something wrong.

But that didn’t stop him from sneaking back to his room after dinner, where he sat cross-legged on the floor, staring at the paper. The equation was the only thing that kept his mind from spinning. If he concentrated hard enough, he could almost *feel* it—like the universe was whispering secrets just out of reach.

The next morning, Mateo was late for school, his usual routine, but today he had a plan. He’d found a hidden corner in the school bathroom, a space behind the stalls where no one ever looked. He pulled out the equation, unfolded the paper, and pressed it against the cold tile.

“What are you doing?” a voice hissed from behind him.

Mateo jumped. It was Javier, the guy who always sat with the math team, his dark eyes sharp. “Just practicing,” Mateo said, but his voice cracked.

Javier leaned in. “That’s not practicing. That’s cheating.” He pulled out his own notebook. “I’ve been looking for a way to make time bend. You’re the only one who’s even *trying*.”

Mateo’s hands trembled. He’d never told anyone this. Not even his best friend, Clara, who was in his homeroom and would laugh if she knew.

“Mateo,” Javier said softly, “you’re either the smartest kid in this school or the stupidest. Either way, we’re going to figure this out.”

Outside, the school bell rang again, but Mateo didn’t hear it. He was already walking toward the parking lot, his mind racing. Because now he had to decide: was he going to keep this to himself, or was he going to tell the world what he’d found?

---

The parking lot was empty except for a few teachers’ cars. Mateo’s knees were weak, but he pushed himself forward. He needed to find someone who could help—someone who believed in him.

That’s when he saw her.

Lena stood near the fence, her arms crossed, her dark hair pulled into a tight bun. She was the class valedictorian, the girl who always knew the answers before anyone else. But today, she looked different. Like she was holding something back.

Mateo swallowed. “Lena,” he said, stepping closer. “I—I need your help.”

She turned, her expression unreadable. “Why?”

Mateo hesitated. “I—I found something. Something important.”

Lena’s eyes narrowed. “Like what?”

Mateo hesitated again. “Like… time.”

Silence stretched between them. Then, Lena’s smile was sharp. “You’re not serious.”

“Yes, I am,” Mateo said, gripping the paper in his pocket. “My dad wrote this. *E = mc².* It’s not just a math equation. It’s a key.”

Lena’s laugh was bitter. “Mateo, you’re twelve. You’re not supposed to be thinking like this. Maybe you should see a doctor.”

Mateo’s stomach twisted. “I don’t want to. I just want to know.”

Lena sighed, rubbing her temples. “Alright,” she said, though her voice held doubt. “But we’re doing this my way. You’re coming home with me.”

---

The drive to Lena’s house was quiet. Mateo watched the passing cars, his mind racing. He’d never been to Lena’s house before—she lived in the suburbs, in a big, old house with a pool and a garden that smelled like roses.

When they arrived, Lena unlocked the door with a key that looked like it had been around since the 70s. The house was warm, the kind of warmth that came from old books and the hum of a fireplace in the living room.

Lena led him inside, her footsteps echoing against the hardwood floors. “This is your chance,” she said. “You’re either going to be the smartest kid in this school or the dumbest. Either way, we’re going to figure this out.”

Mateo’s breath hitched. “What does that mean?”

Lena smirked. “It means you’re either going to succeed or fail. No in-between.”

---

Inside the study, a desk sat against the wall, piled high with books—physics, chemistry, even a few old science fiction novels. Lena flipped through a textbook, her fingers tracing the pages. “This is where we start,” she said. “We need to understand what *E = mc²* really means.”

Mateo sat down in one of the chairs, his notebook resting on his lap. “But how?”

Lena set down the book. “We break it down. Step by step. We find the right equations, the right experiments. And if we’re lucky, we might just crack the code.”

Mateo’s heart pounded. He’d never imagined anyone would actually *want* to help him. Not like this.

Lena turned to him, her eyes serious. “You’re not the only one who’s been asking questions,” she said. “There are others, too. People who are searching for the same thing. We need to find them.”

Mateo’s mind raced. He’d never thought about this before. About the *others*. The ones who were out there, searching, too.

“Who else?” he asked, his voice barely above a whisper.

Lena hesitated. “There’s someone in your class,” she said. “Someone who’s been watching you. Someone who’s been asking the same questions.”

Mateo’s stomach dropped. “Who?”

Lena’s smile was cold. “You’ll find out. But for now, we need to focus. We need to find the answers.”

---

That night, Mateo lay in bed, staring at the ceiling. He’d never felt so alone or so connected at the same time. He thought about his dad, who’d always been there, but who’d also been the one to write that equation in the first place. What if his dad wasn’t just a scientist? What if he was something else?

His phone buzzed. It was an unknown number. He answered, his voice shaky.

“Hello?”

“Mateo,” a voice whispered on the other end. “It’s me.”

Mateo’s blood ran cold. He recognized the voice—the same one that had called him in the school bathroom days ago. Javier.

“What do you want?” Mateo asked, his voice barely audible.

Javier laughed. “I want what you want. I want to know.”

Mateo’s mind raced. He’d never spoken to Javier like this before. Not like this.

“Where are you?” Mateo asked.

Javier sighed. “I’m at the old observatory. You know, the one in the hills?”

Mateo’s eyes widened. “I—I don’t know where that is.”

“It’s not far,” Javier said. “We’ll meet there in an hour.”

Mateo hesitated. He couldn’t go. Not yet. But he had to. He had to find out more.

“Okay,” he said, though his voice was trembling.

---

The observatory was dark, the kind of place where the stars seemed to shine even brighter. Mateo stood at the edge of the platform, his hands trembling. He’d never been here before.

Javier stood nearby, his eyes glowing faintly in the darkness. “You’re here,” he said, his voice echoing.

Mateo swallowed. “I—I don’t know what I’m doing.”

Javier smirked. “You’re doing fine. You’re just getting started.”

Then, from the darkness behind them, a sound echoed—a sound that made Mateo’s blood run cold.

A door creaked open.

Inside, a figure stepped forward. A scientist, old and wise, his eyes sharp with knowledge. His name was Dr. Elias, and he’d been studying time for decades.

“Mateo,” Dr. Elias said, his voice warm. “I didn’t think I’d see you here.”

Mateo’s breath hitched. “I—I found something,” he said. “Something important.”

Dr. Elias smiled. “Like what?”

Mateo hesitated. “Like… time.”

Dr. Elias’ eyes gleamed. “Ah,” he said. “So you’ve come to join the search.”

Mateo’s mind raced. He’d never imagined this. He’d never imagined he’d be the one to find it.

“What do you want to know?” Dr. Elias asked.

Mateo swallowed. “I—I want to know how to bend it.”

Dr. Elias chuckled. “That’s not how it works, Mateo. Time is not something you bend. It’s something you *understand.*”

Mateo’s heart pounded. He wanted to understand. He wanted to know.

“But how?” he asked, his voice barely above a whisper.

Dr. Elias smiled. “You’re already on your way,” he said. “You just need to keep going.”

Mateo’s mind raced. He’d never felt so alone or so connected at the same time. He thought about his dad, about Clara, about Lena. He thought about the others, the ones who were out there, searching, too.

And then, he made his decision.

“What do I do now?” Mateo asked, his voice trembling.

Dr. Elias looked at him, his eyes sharp with knowledge. “You keep asking questions,” he said. “You keep searching. And one day, you’ll find the answers.”

Mateo’s breath hitched. He’d never felt so alone or so connected at the same time.

“What if I don’t?” he asked, his voice barely above a whisper.

Dr. Elias smiled. “Then you’ll fail,” he said. “But that’s okay. Failure is just another step on the way to success.”

Mateo’s mind raced. He’d never felt so alone or so connected at the same time.

“What if I never find it?” he asked, his voice trembling.

Dr. Elias looked at him, his eyes sharp with knowledge. “Then you’ll have tried,” he said. “And that’s enough.”

Mateo’s breath hitched. He’d never felt so alone or so connected at the same time.

---

Back in the school bathroom, Mateo sat on the cold tile, his notebook open in front of him. He’d found a way to bend time. He’d found a way to understand it.

But he wasn’t sure if he was ready.

He thought about his dad, who’d always been there, but who’d also been the one to write that equation in the first place. What if his dad wasn’t just a scientist? What if he was something else?

Mateo’s mind raced. He’d never felt so alone or so connected at the same time.

He closed his notebook, his breath shallow. He had to keep going. He had to find the answers.

And then, he made his decision.

He’d never been the kind of kid who made big decisions. But this was different. This was bigger than anything he’d ever imagined.

He’d found a way to bend time. He’d found a way to understand it.

And now, he was ready to tell the world.

---

The next morning, Mateo stood in front of the school, his notebook clutched in his hand. He’d found a way to bend time. He’d found a way to understand it.

But he wasn’t sure if he was ready.

He thought about his dad, his mom, his friends. He thought about the others, the ones who were out there, searching, too.

And then, he made his decision.

He’d keep searching. He’d keep asking questions.

And one day, he’d find the answers.

Mateo stepped forward, his breath shallow. He’d never felt so alone or so connected at the same time.

He opened his notebook, his eyes wide. He’d found a way to bend time.

And now, he was ready to tell the world.
