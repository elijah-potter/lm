First recorded evidence of a routine operation in early human societies appears in Neolithic cave paintings (c. 7000 BCE), depicting repetitive tasks like pottery-making or seed harvesting. The first documented written records of systematic routines come from ancient Mesopotamia, where clay tablets (c. 2700 BCE) describe agricultural cycles and labor assignments.

The concept of structured routines evolved in ancient Egypt (c. 3000 BCE) with the **Ka**—a daily cycle tied to cosmic rhythms. Later, Babylonian astronomers (c. 600 BCE) created the **sexagesimal system**, a timekeeping framework that laid groundwork for modern scheduling.

In the 18th century, Swiss watchmaker **Patek Philippe** (1735) introduced the first accurate mechanical chronometer, marking the birth of precise timekeeping routines. The Industrial Revolution (18th–19th centuries) standardized factory routines with time clocks and assembly-line efficiency, codifying modern work schedules.

Key milestones:
1. **Prehistoric**: Ritualized labor cycles (e.g., farming).
2. **Ancient**: Cosmic/astronomical timekeeping (Egypt, Mesopotamia).
3. **Mechanical**: Clock-based scheduling (1700s).
4. **Modern**: Algorithmic optimization (20th century).

Routine as a system emerged from necessity—transitioning from nature-driven to technology-driven workflows.
