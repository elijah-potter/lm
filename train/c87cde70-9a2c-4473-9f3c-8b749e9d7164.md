The office coffee machine hissed as I poured the last sip of my lukewarm brew, the steam curling into the stale air. Across the desk, Sarah’s fingers hovered over her keyboard—she’d just sent an email to the entire team about the weekend’s spontaneous team-building activity: *"Loved the kayaking! Will everyone be free next month for a hike?"*

I should’ve said something. I *should’ve*—but the words stuck in my throat like the regret that had been festering since I’d seen her laughing with the others at lunch last week. That time she’d slipped into the hallway to talk about her dad’s illness, her voice breaking as she said, *"He’s not going to make it."* I’d said, *"That’s tough"* and then—nothing else. Just the silence that followed, thick as the guilt in my chest.

Now, in the quiet of the afternoon, I typed a reply: *"Sure, I’m free next month!"*—just to keep the conversation going, to avoid the weight of what I could’ve done. But the words felt hollow, like a promise I’d already broken.

Sarah’s reply came minutes later: *"Thanks! Let me know if you want to brainstorm dates."* The email was sent. The moment was over.

I closed my laptop and stared at the screen, the cursor blinking like an empty promise. The regret was gone—not erased, but buried beneath the noise of work, the rhythm of another day. The office door opened behind me. Someone else’s regret was already waiting to be buried.
