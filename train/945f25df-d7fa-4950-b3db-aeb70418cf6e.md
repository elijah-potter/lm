A flickering streetlamp cast long shadows across the cracked pavement as rain drummed against the broken glass of a shattered window. Inside, the house’s single remaining bulb—an old incandescent bulb on the kitchen counter—glowed weakly, its light barely piercing the thick dust motes floating in the humid air. The refrigerator door groaned as the doorstop slipped, a rusted nail still wedged beneath it, but the cold inside had long since melted.

Outside, the storm had taken the power grid. A neighbor’s porch light flickered, then died, leaving the neighborhood in darkness except for the occasional flare of a cigarette or the distant wail of a siren. The power outage had been sudden; no warnings, just the silence of the dead machines. Now, the hum of the fridge’s compressor had died, too, replaced by the occasional groan of the old furnace struggling to keep the heat.

On the floor, a half-empty bottle of whiskey sat beside a half-eaten sandwich, its crust crumbling under the weight of time and damp. A child’s toy, a broken stuffed animal, lay abandoned in the corner, its eyes staring blankly at the ceiling. The mother, her hands steady despite the trembling, poured herself another drink from the kitchen tap, the water warm from the pipe.

The father, slumped in the armchair, watched TV via a jury-rigged solar charger on the dining table. The static-filled signal crackled between frames, but the news report about the blackout was clear enough: *"Power failures expected to last at least another 12 hours. Stay indoors if possible."* The screen flickered again, then went dark.

A key turned in the front door lock, the old mechanism scraping against the rust. The door swung open, and a figure stepped inside—someone who had been waiting. They carried a flashlight, its beam cutting through the gloom like a blade. The mother didn’t react. The father didn’t move. Only the dog, curled up in the hallway, let out a low whine and lifted its head, ears twitching.

The visitor spoke first, their voice low and measured. *"You should’ve left the fridge door open."* The mother didn’t answer. The air smelled of damp wool and old wood. Somewhere, a clock ticked, but the second hand spun still.
