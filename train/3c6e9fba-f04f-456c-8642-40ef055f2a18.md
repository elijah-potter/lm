The gardener in the small village had always pruned the same rosebush every Tuesday at dawn. One Tuesday, he noticed a single, unfamiliar bloom—unusual in shape, color, and scent. Though he didn’t know its name, its presence felt like a whisper in the soil. That week, he kept it in a vase indoors, watering it daily, whispering to it as if it were a child. By the next Tuesday, the rose had split open, revealing a single, perfect seed inside. He planted it in his garden bed, naming it "the stranger." The following year, when the village’s oldest rose tree bore no fruit, the stranger bloomed again—this time, the scent was different, the bloom larger. Neighbors began to notice, murmuring about the "miracle bloom." The gardener didn’t care about fame—only that the stranger had become something more than he had ever been: part of the village’s quiet, unspoken history.
