Stone fruit include apricots, cherries, nectarines, peaches and plums. The term refers to the large, hard seeds of the closely related members of the Prunus genus. Like most fruit trees, stone fruit are warm season crops that fare well in Mediterranean climates. You can enhance the health and harvest of stone fruit trees with companion planting, the practice of using complementary plants to deter pests and diseases.
Companion planting is an age-old agricultural method for enhancing a plant’s environment. Plants influence each other and can have symbiotic or antagonistic relationships. Companion plants attract beneficial insects such as bees for pollination. They may also attract predators of undesirable pests or repel harmful insects with potent odors. Some companion plants add nutrients in soil, which can enhance the growth and flavor of nearby plants.
Aromatic herbs not only provide your garden with a source of multipurpose plants for cooking and medicinal purposes, they also make great companion plants for stone fruit. Caraway (Carum carvi) is an herb with roots that loosen soil for increased draining and distribution of nutrients. Herbs that improve the health of nearby fruit include sweet marjoram (Origanum majorana) and lovage (Levisticum officinale). Aphids are among the most common pests of stone fruit. They feed on plants and leave sticky honeydew behind which is a host for black mold. Herbs that repel aphids include garden nasturtium (Tropaeolum majus), catnip (Nepeta cataria) and coriander (Coriandrum sativum). Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis), lavender (Lavandula) and common thyme (Thymus vulgaris) repel snails and slugs. Herbs that attract beneficial insects for stone fruit include parsley (Petroselinum crispum), dill (Anethum graveolens) and coriander. These herbs thrive U.S. Department of Agriculture plant hardiness zones 8 to 10.
Attractive flowers that enhance the beauty of your garden may also serve as useful companion plants to stone fruit trees. Annual French marigolds (Tagetes patula) and African marigolds (Tagetes erecta) can suppress 14 types of plant-parasitic nematodes, serious pests that can devastate fruit trees. Nematodes are microscopic roundworms that live in soil and plants. They are notoriously difficult to manage. Marigolds release a substance that suppresses nematodes. Like fruit, marigolds are warm weather crop. In order for marigolds to be effective, plant them in the same planting bed before planting fruit trees. French marigold varieties “Nemagold,” “Queen Sophia,” “Petite Blanc” and “Tangerine” are most effective. Honeysuckle, such as Lonicera periclymenum "Peaches and Cream," which grows USDA hardiness zones 4 to 9, not only smells good, it also attracts several types of pollinators, including butterflies and hummingbirds. If your yard has shade, oakleaf hydrangeas (Hydrangea quercifolia) are ideal as companion plants that attract pollinators. The shade-tolerant shrubs with vibrant flowers attract bees and butterflies and grow in USDA hardiness zones 4 to 9. Lilac bushes such as the "Angel White" (Syringa vulgaris "Angel White") and "Lilac Sunday" (Syringa x chinensis "Lilac Sunday"), hardy in USDA zones 3 through 8, as well as "Big Blue Lilac" (Syringa vulgaris "Big Blue"), hardy in USDA zones 3 through 9, not only attract pollinators but can also serve as a windbreak for young fruit trees that need protection.
The effectiveness of companion plants may be compromised by other plants. The presence of cucumbers can lessen the effects of marigolds. Lilac bushes do not fare well near black walnut trees (Juglans nigra), which grow in USDA hardiness zones 4 to 9. Place catnip in containers in gardens because it can take over a garden. It may also attract cats, which may not be a problem if you also have to contend with moles and voles.
