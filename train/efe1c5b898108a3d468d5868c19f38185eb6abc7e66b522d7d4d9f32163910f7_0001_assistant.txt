The hatch slid open with a comforting hiss. Immediately, my spacesuit’s microphones relayed the whistle of the planet’s brisk breeze. It seemed a shame I couldn’t draw a deep breath of it and smell the pristine air, but the hydrogen cyanide that made up 30% of the planet’s atmosphere would have made that a bad idea. 

Instead, I drank in the scene with my eyes. The horizon to the north was dominated by jagged lavender peaks that ended just below blue clouds. Toward the east were immense rock formations that would put all of Earth’s national parks to shame. Each one was striated with layers of burgundy, ochre, crimson, and the occasional thin line of teal that made everything pop.

Between those rocks and my ship was a broad floodplain full of orange soil. Only a small stream of yellow liquid flowed down from the mountains at the moment, but it was clear that this whole landscape could change in a hurry when the rains came. 

No sight I’d ever beheld could compare and I knew I’d probably never feel such an incalculable sense of awe again. And all this was in spite of the fact that there were no plants to speak of. No forests framed the landscape. No wildflowers pocked the surface. Not a speck of life could be found anywhere. As of this moment, I was the only human - and indeed maybe the only living thing - to have ever looked upon it.

I felt a slight pang of shame for daring to intrude upon this pristine perfection. But instead of fleeing, I set out across the land. I had a job to do, after all.
