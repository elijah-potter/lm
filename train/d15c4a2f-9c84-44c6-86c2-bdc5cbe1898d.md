## TIL the "Smell of Rain" is Actually Multiple Compounds

Today I learned that the distinctive scent many associate with rain – often called “petrichor” – isn't a single aroma, but a complex mixture of several chemical compounds released by the impact of raindrops on dry earth. 

The term "petrichor" itself was coined in 1964 by Australian scientists Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas Guthrie, who were researching the sensory properties of rain. They discovered that the scent wasn't merely the smell of wetness, but something more intricate.

The primary contributors to this aroma are geosmin and plant oils. Geosmin, produced by bacteria in the soil, is incredibly potent; humans can detect it at concentrations as low as five parts per trillion. During dry periods, these compounds accumulate on the soil surface. When rain falls, the impact dislodges these molecules, releasing them into the air. Simultaneously, raindrops fracture plant oils trapped in the ground, further contributing to the characteristic fragrance. 

The experience is particularly noticeable after a prolonged dry spell, as the concentration of these compounds is higher. It’s a fascinating example of how our perception of natural phenomena is shaped by a surprisingly complex interplay of biology and chemistry.
