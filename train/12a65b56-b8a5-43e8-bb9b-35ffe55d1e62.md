A quiet afternoon in a cramped studio apartment, fluorescent lights buzzing overhead. The coffee cup, half-empty, sits on the desk beside a half-finished sketchbook. The artist flips through the pages, tracing fingerprints over faded lines of failed attempts at a portrait of her grandmother—her mother’s mother, who died when she was five.

The pencil snaps. The eraser crumbles.

She looks up. The window behind her is open, letting in the sound of rain tapping against the glass like a metronome. A single note, persistent and steady.

Then—it hits her. Not as a sudden spark, but as the weight of a door closing behind her for the first time in years. The shape of her grandmother’s face isn’t in the sketch, not yet, but the *clothes*—the faded blue wool coat, the lace-up boots—are. She grabs her phone, pulls up the old photos, and overlays them onto the half-finished canvas. The fingers of her grandmother’s hands, trembling, clutching a teacup. The way her eyes—sharp, knowing—follow her every move.

The first stroke is tentative. Then deliberate. The artist exhales, and the sketchbook becomes a confession: *This is who I am, not just who I was.*

The last line is a signature: her grandmother’s name, scrawled in ink that trembles with the same quiet strength as her own hands had once.
