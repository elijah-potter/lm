The café’s hum was a static hum, each conversation a crackle in the air like distant thunder. She noticed how the man across from her—his fingers tracing patterns on the table’s glossy surface—never once glanced at the newspaper’s headline, though the ink had just spilled across the page. The headline read: *"New Study Links Urban Noise Pollution to Long-Term Memory Loss."*

She watched him as he leaned forward, elbows on the table, his brow furrowed in concentration. The headline blurred into static, but she remembered the exact shade of his tie, the way his lips parted slightly when he exhaled. She knew, before he did, that the study’s findings weren’t about him—it was about the *absence* of him in the world’s collective awareness.

Across the table, the woman with the coffee-stained blouse fidgeted with her phone, swiping through notifications. She didn’t notice the way the fluorescent lights pulsed in time with her pulse, the way the overhead fan’s blades spun in slow, hypnotic circles, the way the scent of burnt sugar and stale coffee curled around her like a ghost. She missed the way the air smelled like old books and damp paper, the way the walls held the weight of a hundred conversations that had never been spoken aloud.

She leaned back in her chair, fingers steepled beneath her chin. Outside, the city breathed in time with the passing cars—honking horns, the distant wail of a siren, the occasional laugh from a passing child. She listened. Not to the noise, but to the *silence* between the sounds, the spaces where the world forgot to fill itself with words.

The café’s regulars—always the same faces, always the same routine—didn’t see the way the old man in the corner, his hands trembling, traced the rim of his coffee cup like it was a map of his own unraveling. They didn’t notice the way his fingers, once steady, now trembled, each sip a fragile act of faith.

She watched him, and she knew, before he did, that his cup wasn’t just coffee. It was a vessel for something else—memory, perhaps, or the slow erosion of time. The world moved around him, but he moved through it, as if he were the only one still standing.

She picked up her own cup, the one she’d been nursing since morning, and took a slow sip. The warmth spread through her like a quiet current, and for a moment, she was the only one who saw. The rest of the world? They were just passing through, oblivious to the quiet things that made life worth noticing.
