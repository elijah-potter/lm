## Mycelium-Based Construction Materials: A Briefing for City Planners

The construction industry is a significant contributor to global carbon emissions, both through the production of traditional building materials like concrete and steel and through the energy consumed during building operation. A promising avenue for mitigating this impact lies in the development of construction materials derived from mycelium, the vegetative part of fungi. Recent advancements demonstrate the feasibility of producing structural components—bricks, insulation panels, and even load-bearing beams—using mycelium grown on agricultural waste (e.g., hemp stalks, rice husks) as a substrate (Jones et al., *Materials Today Bio*, 2021).

**What is it?** Mycelium acts as a natural binder, digesting the agricultural waste and binding it into a dense, composite material. The process is relatively low-energy, requiring only controlled humidity and temperature to facilitate growth. Once the desired density and structural integrity are achieved, the mycelium growth is halted through drying or heat treatment.

**Potential Benefits for Urban Development:** Mycelium-based materials offer a compelling alternative to conventional options. They are inherently renewable, utilize waste streams, and exhibit a negative carbon footprint during production, effectively sequestering carbon (Brandt et al., *Nature Sustainability*, 2022). The resulting materials possess good thermal insulation properties, potentially reducing energy consumption for heating and cooling in buildings (Du et al., *Building and Environment*, 2020). Furthermore, the aesthetic qualities of mycelium-based materials—their natural texture and color—offer unique design possibilities.

**Environmental Trade-offs and Considerations:** While offering a lower carbon footprint in production, the durability and long-term environmental impact of mycelium-based construction require careful evaluation. The material's susceptibility to moisture and microbial degradation presents a significant challenge (Eriksson et al., *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*, 2018).  The sourcing of agricultural waste, while beneficial, needs to be managed sustainably to avoid unintended consequences such as disrupting local agricultural economies or contributing to transportation emissions. The disposal of mycelium-based materials at the end of their lifespan also necessitates investigation; composting is a desirable outcome, but the potential for methane release during anaerobic decomposition must be addressed.

**Next Steps:**  Pilot projects incorporating mycelium-based materials in building components are essential to assess real-world performance. Standardized testing protocols for strength, fire resistance, and moisture resistance are needed to facilitate wider adoption.  Research into bio-based coatings and treatments to enhance durability is ongoing.



## Technical Appendix: Mycelium-Based Construction Materials – Engineering Considerations

This appendix provides a more detailed technical overview of mycelium-based construction materials, focusing on engineering properties, potential failure modes, and ongoing research efforts.

**Material Composition and Microstructure:**  Mycelium-based composites primarily consist of fungal hyphae, the agricultural waste substrate, and extracellular polysaccharides (primarily glucans and chitin) secreted by the fungus (Sheffels et al., *Advanced Functional Materials*, 2023). The hyphae intertwine and bind the substrate particles, creating a porous network. The density and porosity are key determinants of mechanical properties. Higher mycelial density generally correlates with increased strength and reduced water absorption, but also increases brittleness.  Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals a complex microstructure dependent on fungal species, substrate type, and growth conditions.

**Mechanical Properties and Performance:**  Early mycelium-based materials exhibited relatively low compressive strength (typically 0.5-2.0 MPa) and tensile strength (0.1-0.3 MPa) (Bapat et al., *Journal of Materials in Civil Engineering*, 2019). Recent advancements in optimizing growth parameters—including substrate pre-treatment, fungal strain selection (e.g., *Ganoderma lucidum*, *Pleurotus ostreatus*), and controlled humidity/temperature cycles—have yielded improvements.  Compressive strengths exceeding 1.5 MPa and flexural strengths approaching 0.5 MPa have been reported in optimized composites (Hernandez et al., *Construction and Building Materials*, 2022). However, these materials remain significantly weaker than conventional concrete (typically 20-40 MPa).  Their primary strength lies in their low density, resulting in a favorable strength-to-weight ratio, suitable for non-load-bearing applications or as lightweight infill panels.

**Durability and Degradation Mechanisms:** The primary challenge lies in the material’s susceptibility to moisture and microbial degradation.  Mycelium, being a biological material, is inherently vulnerable to attack by other microorganisms. Water absorption leads to swelling and weakens the structure, while microbial degradation breaks down the fungal polysaccharides.  Studies have shown significant mass loss (up to 20% after six months) when exposed to humid environments (Ljunggren et al., *International Biodeterioration & Biodegradation*, 2017).  Fire resistance is also a concern; while mycelium itself is relatively fire-retardant, the agricultural waste substrate can contribute to combustion.  Furthermore, UV degradation can affect surface integrity.

**Mitigation Strategies and Ongoing Research:**  Several strategies are being explored to enhance durability:

*   **Bio-based Coatings:** Application of natural oils (e.g., linseed oil, tung oil) or waxes to create a hydrophobic barrier.
*   **Chemical Treatments:**  Limited application of non-toxic preservatives (e.g., chitosan) to inhibit microbial growth. Careful consideration of potential environmental impact is critical.
*   **Hybrid Composites:** Combining mycelium with other bio-based materials (e.g., hemp fibers, wood flour) to improve mechanical properties and reduce water absorption.
*   **Genetic Modification:** Research into fungal strains with enhanced polysaccharide production or increased resistance to microbial attack (ethical and regulatory considerations are paramount).

**Unanswered Research Questions:**

*   Long-term performance assessment under realistic environmental conditions requires extended field trials.
*   Development of standardized testing protocols specifically tailored to mycelium-based materials is essential.
*   A comprehensive life cycle assessment (LCA) is needed to accurately quantify the environmental benefits and potential drawbacks.
*   The impact of large-scale mycelium cultivation on agricultural land use and biodiversity requires further investigation.
