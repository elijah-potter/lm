Politeness often fails when words fail to match intent. The waiter bowed slightly too late, his hand hovering over the menu as if unsure whether to serve or leave. "I—I’d like to see the wine list," he stammered, his voice cracking under the weight of an unspoken apology for the delay. The customer, a man in a well-tailored suit, barely glanced up from his phone. "Yes, of course," he said dryly, "but could you make it a little faster next time?"

The bartender at the dive bar wiped down the counter with exaggerated precision, his movements jerky as he tried to cover the way his fingers twitched toward the whiskey bottle. "One neat bourbon," he muttered, his voice thick with the effort of not saying *please*. The customer ordered a beer instead, his back to the bar, the clink of ice against glass his only reply.

A coworker’s boss, usually impeccable in his manners, burst into tears during a meeting. His hands trembled as he wiped his eyes, his voice muffled by a handkerchief. "I—I’m sorry," he choked out, "I just—" The silence that followed was deafening. A few heads turned. No one said it out loud, but the room knew: he was being polite because he couldn’t stop himself.

The elevator doors opened with a creak, and a stranger stepped in, his tie slightly askew. "Sorry about that," he said, already backing away. The elevator doors closed behind him, leaving the trapped passengers to stare at the buttons, none of them knowing how to respond. The one who spoke first was the one who had already forgotten the words. "Uh. Fine."
