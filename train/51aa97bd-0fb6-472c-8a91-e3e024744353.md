I was standing outside the old diner on Maple Street, my coffee still warm in my hands, when the commotion began. The place had been quiet—just the usual hum of chatter and clinking cutlery—until the door burst open and a man in a torn coat and a hat that looked like it had seen better days stumbled out, clutching his stomach.

A few feet away, a group of kids—maybe ten or eleven—were laughing and shoving each other, their faces flushed with excitement. One of them, a girl with a bandaged knuckle, pointed at the man and shouted, *"Look, it’s the ghost of the old timer!"*

The man’s eyes widened, then darted around like he was expecting someone to jump out at him. He muttered something under his breath, then turned and walked quickly toward the alley behind the diner, his steps heavy and unsteady.

By the time I got closer, the kids were already running after him, giggling. The diner’s owner, Mrs. Henderson, appeared in the doorway, her hands on her hips. *"That’s enough!"* she called after them. *"Get back inside!"*

The man disappeared into the shadows, and the alley was suddenly silent except for the distant clink of a bottle being broken. The kids froze, staring at the empty space where he’d been standing. Then, one by one, they started laughing again, like they’d forgotten they’d been scared.

Mrs. Henderson sighed and pushed open the door, calling out, *"Anyone want another biscuit?"* The usual chatter filled the air, but this time, there was something unnatural about it—too loud, too eager. I didn’t know if it was the coffee, or the fact that I’d just witnessed something that didn’t belong in this world.
