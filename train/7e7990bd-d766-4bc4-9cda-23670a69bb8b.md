The first time I saw my mother cry after my father left was when I was ten. She wiped her eyes with a handkerchief and told me it was "just a bad dream." I believed her then, though I could feel the weight of her shoulders as she pressed her forehead against the kitchen table. That night, I slept with the window slightly open, listening to the rain—too loud, too loud to ignore.

The regret was quiet. It wasn’t a scream or a sob, but a small, gnawing ache that settled in my chest like a stone. I told myself I didn’t understand. I was just a boy. But I *did*, and I kept it hidden. If I said anything, she’d only pull me closer, whisper how it was "time" or "God’s will," and I’d swallow my words, afraid of making her cry again.

Years later, I still wake up sometimes at night and remember the way her voice cracked when she said, *"You were such a good boy."* The regret lingers in the space between her words and my silence. It’s buried, but not gone. Sometimes, when I’m alone, I wonder what would have happened if I’d said something then. Would she have let me stay? Would she have forgiven him? Would I have grown up to be the man who could have stopped it all?

I don’t know. But I know I never asked. And that’s the regret: the quiet, unspoken truth that stays buried, waiting to resurface when no one’s looking.
