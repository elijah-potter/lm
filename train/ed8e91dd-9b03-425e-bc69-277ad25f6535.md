The streets here smell faintly of rain and old pavement, the usual morning rush still here but quieter now—fewer honking cabs, more people pausing to watch the pigeons at the fountain in the plaza. The café on the corner still has its worn wooden booths, the barista’s uniform slightly faded but still fits their shoulders well. The corner store’s shelves still groan under the weight of forgotten spices, but the last of the summer squash has disappeared, replaced by pumpkins. The park bench by the lake, where I’d sit every evening, has new graffiti—some familiar, some not—but the rusted hinge still creaks when you sit. The old bookstore’s front window is boarded up, but the books still line the shelves inside, their spines cracked, their covers dog-eared. The hum of the city has changed—less chaotic, more reflective. The memory of it feels like a half-remembered dream.
