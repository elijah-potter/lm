The air smelled of damp earth and old books, thick with the scent of something forgotten—like the last pages of a diary left unopened. I stood at the foot of the old oak tree in the corner of the garden, my fingers tracing the grooves in the bark where time had carved its own story. The leaves, though still, whispered secrets only the wind understood.

I should have left when I first saw the letter in the mailbox. The ink was faded, the paper brittle, but the words had seeped into my skin like ink into parchment. *You were never meant to find this.* It was signed with a name I didn’t recognize, one that felt both familiar and alien—like a ghost’s voice in my dreams. The envelope had been sealed with wax, and the stamp was from a city I’d never visited. The postmark was from yesterday.

I should have burned it. Should have thrown it in the river. But the weight of it pressed against my chest like a stone, and I picked it up anyway, my hands trembling.

The words inside were a confession, or perhaps an accusation. They spoke of choices made in the dark, of promises broken under the weight of silence. The narrator—the one who wrote them—was someone I had known once, but not as I knew them. They spoke of a love that had dissolved like sugar in water, of betrayals that had left scars deeper than any knife could cut. And most terrifying of all, they admitted to something that made my blood turn to ice: they had seen me.

I should have screamed. I should have run. But the words kept echoing in my mind, and the guilt gnawed at me like a rat in the walls. What if they were right? What if I had been the one who drove them to this? The garden around me seemed to shift, the shadows stretching unnaturally long, as if the very earth was watching, waiting.

I clutched the letter tighter, my breath coming in shallow gasps. The tree creaked, as if the wind itself was trying to move me. I should have left. But the voice inside my head—older, wiser—whispered that maybe, just maybe, this was my turn to speak.

The letter slipped from my fingers. It landed at my feet, a relic of a life that had passed and left me behind. I knelt, picking it up, and for the first time, I didn’t know what to do.
