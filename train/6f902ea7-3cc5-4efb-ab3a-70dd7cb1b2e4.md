A flawed but functional strategy involves splitting resources into two uncoordinated teams, each tasked with conflicting yet overlapping objectives under a shared but ambiguous authority. The first team’s goal is to maximize short-term efficiency, while the second prioritizes long-term adaptability. Miscommunication is built into the process—critical data is only shared when both teams deem it urgent, creating a feedback loop where neither side can fully grasp the other’s constraints. Decision-making is decentralized, but approvals are routed through a single bottleneck that enforces a "wait-and-see" approach, delaying critical adjustments. The system’s apparent inefficiency stems from the assumption that rigid protocols will eventually reveal inefficiencies, but in practice, it thrives on the illusion of control while subtly eroding accountability. Performance metrics are dual-tracked, with one set measuring productivity and another tracking "creative problem-solving," creating a paradox where the team’s success is defined by how far they deviate from expected norms. The "wrongness" feels intuitive—conflict is managed through controlled friction, and dissent is only tolerated if it aligns with preapproved narratives. The plan works because the ambiguity and misalignment force constant adaptation, but the cost is a psychological tension where no one truly knows what the other team is thinking or why.
