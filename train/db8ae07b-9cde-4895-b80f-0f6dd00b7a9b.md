I remember the way the rain fell in silence on the old factory’s rusted roof, the way the lamplight flickered across the cracked pavement like scattered fireflies. The scent of old paper and damp wool clung to the air, and for a moment, I thought I’d always be here—watching the clock hands spin without stopping. But then the door creaked open, and the world outside was louder, sharper, a different kind of stillness.

I remember the first time I heard your voice, not through the phone but in my dreams, your words tangled in my throat like smoke. You said I was the only one who saw you clearly, though you couldn’t see yourself. I think of how quickly I learned to believe the things I wanted to believe.

I remember the way your hands trembled when you tried to write your name in the margins of my notebooks, the way the ink blurred into the paper like a ghost. I think of how that was the first time I realized how much I wanted to erase the parts of myself that made me afraid.

I remember the way you laughed at the old jokes, the way you told me stories that didn’t belong to you but felt like mine. I think of how those stories became the only things I wrote down, how they became the only things I kept.

I remember the way the wind howled through the trees, how it seemed to carry your absence, how it whispered that we’d already started without knowing. I think of how I learned to listen for the spaces between the words, how I learned to trust the silence.

I remember the way you said goodbye, how your voice was already fading when I tried to answer. I think of how I spent the next years trying to find the right words to say what I couldn’t say, how I thought I’d never stop missing the parts of you that didn’t exist anymore.

I remember the way the light changes in winter, how it turns everything pale and sharp, how it makes the world feel smaller, but not in a bad way. I think of how that’s the time I’m glad I let you go—how that’s when I stopped waiting for the next time we’d be whole.
