## TIL the "Smell of Rain" is Actually Multiple Chemicals

Today I learned that the distinctive scent many associate with rain, often called "petrichor," isn’t a single aroma but a complex blend of several chemical compounds. For years, it was believed to be solely attributable to geosmin, an organic compound produced by bacteria in the soil. However, recent research has revealed a second, significant contributor: plant oils released into the air when raindrops impact dry surfaces.

The term "petrichor" itself was coined in 1964 by Australian scientists Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas Guthrie, who were investigating the characteristic smell following a dry spell followed by rainfall. Geosmin, the primary component, is produced by *Streptomyces* bacteria commonly found in soil. These bacteria thrive in warm, moist conditions and release geosmin as they die. When a dry period ends, raindrops dislodge this geosmin, carrying it into the air. Simultaneously, raindrops impact vegetation, releasing oils that have accumulated on leaves and stems. These oils contain compounds like terpenes, which contribute a fresh, citrusy note to the overall scent.

The combination of geosmin and these plant-derived oils creates the unique and evocative fragrance we recognize as the smell of rain. It’s fascinating to realize that what we perceive as a single, unified aroma is, in reality, a complex chemical symphony orchestrated by microorganisms and vegetation.
