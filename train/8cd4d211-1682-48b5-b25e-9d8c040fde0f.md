A lone server rack in a dimly lit server farm hummed quietly under flickering fluorescent lights. Dust motes danced in the stale air, suspended by unseen currents. Inside the rack, a single server, barely audible, emitted a faint fan noise—an otherwise unremarkable hum. Its CPU, a well-oiled machine, ran below its peak load, a fraction of its capacity. The network traffic here was minimal: a few stray packets drifting through the static like ghosts.

Across the hall, a monitoring dashboard displayed a graph of real-time CPU usage, but the server’s spike was barely visible—lost in the noise of a dozen other machines. Alerts, when they came, were generic: *"High CPU usage detected"*—a standard warning that ignored the fact this was never a problem. The system administrators, buried in tickets and emergencies, had long since tuned out the server’s quiet inefficiency. The network firewall, configured for broad strokes, let through all traffic except the rare, deliberate block. The only human interaction was a scheduled maintenance window, during which the server’s lights blinked off and on like a dying bulb, unnoticed by the few technicians who paused to glance at their schedules.

Outside, the server farm’s perimeter cameras recorded the scene: a lone technician entering the building, their boots making no sound on the carpet. They paused by the rack, checking a log file for errors, then moved on. The camera feed showed nothing—just the empty hall, the flickering lights, and the unnoticed hum of a server that had long since stopped trying to be heard.
