In the quiet of an autumn afternoon, old Mr. Holloway sat on his porch swing, the wind whistling through the bare branches above. His hands, gnarled with age, gripped the wooden arms, each knuckle like a map of seasons he’d lived through. The newspaper lay open beside him, half-eaten crumbs clinging to its edges like forgotten memories.

A fly buzzed around the edge of his glass, and he swatted it away without looking up. "That one’s been here since dawn," he muttered. The porch light flickered, casting long shadows that danced across the faded porch floor like specters of forgotten years.

Then, his eyes landed on the garden behind him. A single, wild rose—pale and blooming past its usual time—leaned against the fence, its petals trembling in the breeze. It wasn’t supposed to be here. No one planted it. It had found its way, a quiet rebellion against the winter’s grip.

He exhaled, watching the petals sway. "This one’s got a stubborn streak," he said, almost to himself. The fly landed on his sleeve again, unnoticed this time.
