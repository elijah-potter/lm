The old lantern flickered, casting long shadows across the worn wooden floor. Elias adjusted his spectacles, squinting at the half-written manuscript before him. *"The tale of the silver fox and the cursed well,"* he murmured, his fingers tracing the faded ink.

Then he began.

---

In a village nestled between misty mountains, where the river whispered secrets to the stone, there lived a weaver named Maren. Her hands, once swift with thread, were now trembling—her fingers too weak to spin wool into silk. The village elders whispered that her grandmother’s curse had found her, that the old woman, a witch they called *Mutter Moira*, had cast a spell upon her bloodline.

Maren’s mother, a kind woman, had tried to break the curse with herbs and prayers, but the villagers feared her. They burned her garden, stole her food, and left her to starve in the hollowed-out barn where she hid. The night Maren was born, the wind carried the sound of Moira’s laughter—a sound like wind chimes breaking—through the open window. The midwife, trembling, told the story of how Moira had once been a healer, until she had betrayed her people for gold.

Years passed. Maren grew, her mother dying before her eyes. She learned to weave not silk, but coarse cloth, her fingers still stiff. One evening, as she sat by the fire, a stranger arrived—a man with a cloak too dark, a smile too wide. He spoke of a well in the woods, where the water shimmered like liquid silver. *"The witch’s curse can be broken,"* he said, *"if you find the well’s heart and return it to the earth."*

Maren hesitated. The villagers had burned Moira’s bones, and the well was said to be cursed. But her fingers, once steady, now shook so hard she nearly dropped the pot of stew. Still, she agreed.

---

The journey was long. The forest swallowed her footsteps, and the air grew thick with the scent of damp earth and iron. She followed the stranger’s trail, her boots sinking into mud, her breath shallow. The well was deeper than she’d imagined—a gaping maw of black stone, its surface rippling unnaturally. The water glowed faintly, like moonlight through glass.

Maren knelt, her hands trembling. *"What do I do?"* she whispered.

The stranger’s voice was a whisper in her ear. *"Find the heart. The old woman’s last gift to the earth."*

She reached into her pouch and pulled out a small, smooth stone—polished, almost smooth as glass. It was warm. She placed it at the well’s edge, her fingers brushing the water’s surface. A ripple spread outward, and the silver shimmer faded.

The well groaned. Then, slowly, it sealed itself.

The stranger vanished. The forest seemed to hold its breath.

Maren stood, her legs unsteady. The village was far behind. She had done something. But what?

---

Back at the barn, she packed her few belongings. The villagers had already begun to gather, their faces grim. They found her weeping, clutching the stone in her palm.

*"You’ve broken the curse,"* they said, their voices hollow.

But Maren only stared at the stone. It was warm. And it pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat.

That night, she dreamed of the well, of the witch’s laughter echoing through the dark. And when she woke, her hands were no longer trembling. Her fingers moved with precision, weaving thread into silk again.

Years later, the villagers still spoke of the weaver’s miracle. But some whispered that the well in the woods was still there, watching.

And sometimes, on still nights, the wind carried the sound of laughter—just for a moment—before the silence returned.
