In 1979, the National Council of Teachers of English (NCTE) joined a coalition of other professional associations to reaffirm the value of a balanced education by endorsing a statement entitled "The Essentials of Education." NCTE followed this up by submitting, in 1982, its own document, "The Essentials of English," which identified the ways in which the study of English contributes to the knowledge, understanding and skills of those who will make up our future citizenry. It also outlined specific objectives for the development of important English skills.
NCTE's primary goal, as stated in "The Essentials of English," is the same as that of a newspaper. Both are vitally interested in the development of individuals who are self-sufficient, contributing members of society. Newspapers and NCTE also compliment each other in that both serve as vehicles for the development of thinking, informed citizens.
In addition to sharing common ground with NCTE, newspapers have the ability to assist this organization with the implementation of its goals. Newspaper content is current and real, and is a natural for the development of reading, language and other communication skills.
Last week, Teachers' Aids offered newspaper activities designed to help teachers accomplish objectives in the areas of language, critical thinking and literature. Following are activities to develop the communication skills that are outlined in "The Essentials of English."
Objectives in this area include:
Learning to read accurately and make valid inferences.
- Scan the news pages and determine which statements are being made by the reporter and which statements he attributes, either directly or indirectly, to other sources.
- Read three front page stories and determine the reasons why they selected to appear on this page.
- Read an article that centers on a person and identify all the words and phrases that imply something about that person's appearance and personality.
- After reading a front page story, list the most important facts about it in one or two sentences.
- Prepare a list of questions based on information found in the paper. Organize a scavenger hunt to see who can answer all the questions first by citing the number of the pages and the columns containing the answers.
- Underline the sentences and phrases that convey the main idea of an article. Or, read the article, put it aside and write the main idea in your own words.
- Read a front page story and identify the economic effects the news can cause. (Example: A story about a teacher strike could mean increased wages resulting in higher taxes.).
- Make two columns on a sheet of paper, labeling one facts and the other opinion. Select and editorial and an ad from the paper and list all the statements of fact and opinion in the appropriate columns.
- After reading an article about a scientific discovery or technological improvement, make a list of the possible good and bad effects that may be felt by society because of it.
- Study a news photo (and the caption) and describe what you think happened a half hour before and after the picture was taken. Be sure to give reasons for what you think occured.
Developing the necessary reading skills to comprehend material appearing in a variety of forms.
- Identify the messages of three editorial cartoons.
- Find examples of irony, puns and satire in comic strip dialogue.
- Interpret the information provided in a chart or graph in today's paper.
- Analyze advertisements for their effectiveness and appeal. Which three ads in today's paper do you consider to be the most successful in terms of persuading readers?
- Read news stories, features and editorials and list all the ways they are alike and different.
- List the 5W's (who, what, when, where and why) of five stories and ads.
NCTE's objectives in this area include:
Speaking clearly and expressively about ideas and concerns.
- Orally support or challenge an editorial or a letter to the editor that is written on a topic of interest to you. Be sure to cite specific reasons for your stand.
- Plan and conduct a 10-minute broadcast during which student "commentators' ' offer their opinions and insights on current topics in the news.
- Present an oral report to the class on material read in the paper.
- After studying an editorial cartoon, describe it to the class. Discuss the symbols used, drawing techniques and the impact of the message. Do you agree or disagree with the cartoonist's viewpoint? Why or why not?
Presenting arguments in orderly and convincing ways.
- Organize a debate on a current topic that is receiving a great deal of news coverage. Use only newspaper information for background and support material.
- Challenge the opinions of newspaper columnists, like "Dear Abby" or Mike Royko.
Learning to write clearly and honestly.
