Juana Maria, also known as the "Lone Woman of San Nicholas," was a Native American who endured nearly twenty years of solitude on an island off the coast of California. Born on San Nicholas, her tribe faced devastation in the early 1800s when hostile hunters slaughtered most of its members. In 1835, missionaries evacuated the remaining survivors, but Juana Maria, in a desperate search for her missing infant, ran back to the island and was inadvertently left behind when rescue efforts fell through. Thus, she was left to fend for herself in complete isolation on San Nicholas.

For the next 18 years, Juana Maria sought refuge in a cave and relied on fishing using seashell hooks. She skillfully captured sea birds and seals, utilizing their feathers and skins to create dresses. She occupied her time by weaving baskets and bowls from grasses, showcasing her resourcefulness and adaptability. However, her solitude was interrupted in 1853 when Captain George Nidever discovered her presence on San Nicholas. Several weeks later, Nidever brought Juana Maria to Santa Barbara. Although communication was challenging due to the language barrier, Juana Maria managed to convey her extraordinary tale of survival through hand gestures.

Tragically, Juana Maria's adjustment to mainland life proved difficult. She struggled with the unfamiliar diet and succumbed to dysentery a mere two months after leaving her isolated island. Her remarkable story captivated the public's imagination and was later fictionalized in the popular children's novel "Island of the Blue Dolphins."
