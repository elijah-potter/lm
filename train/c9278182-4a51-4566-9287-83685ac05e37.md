**The Silent Garden**

The rain had stopped by noon, leaving the earth damp with the ghost of its own weight. I knelt in the overgrown plot behind the house, fingers tracing the cracks in the soil like old friends. The first few days had been hard—digging, weeding, the relentless task of coaxing life from dead earth. But today, something shifted. The daffodils, those stubborn, golden survivors, had pushed through the frost like they’d always been here. Their faces turned toward the sun, unabashed and defiant.

The garden was my refuge, but it was also my prison. The walls of my mind had been built of stone since the accident, and here, in the dirt, I could pretend the past was just another layer of soil, something I could turn over and let go. But the roots—oh, they were stubborn too. They refused to let me forget the way my sister had laughed, the way the doctor had called it a "precarious recovery." I had dug too deep, too fast, and now I was stuck in the mud.

A knock at the door. I didn’t look up. "You’re not home," my mother said, voice too bright. "You’ve been gone for three days." I exhaled, slow and sharp. "I’m gardening."

She didn’t ask why. She didn’t need to.

The afternoon turned to evening, and the garden became a place of quiet triumph. The tomatoes, half a season behind, still ripened. The tomatoes.

Then came the letter.

It was from my sister, not my mother. Not a note, not a message—just a single sheet of paper, folded like a promise. *I’m sorry.* It was all she wrote, but it was enough. Enough to make the stones in my chest crack. Enough to let the water from the rain seep into the earth, not as punishment, but as renewal.

The garden had taught me that some wounds didn’t heal with words. They healed with the slow, patient work of growing things. And so, when the first frost touched the petals of the roses, I stood and let them fall. The earth took them. The earth always did.
