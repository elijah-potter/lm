Puffing the Tube
An underground steam train marks a 150th birthday
HOURS before the train departed, men in shabby coats converged on Kensington Olympia station in west London in search of the best sites for their costly cameras. As Metropolitan 1, a small maroon steam locomotive built in 1898, drew in, railway bigwigs from around the world mingled with a select bunch of other ticket-holders for a train journey across the city to Moorgate (pictured)—along a stretch of London’s underground railway that opened 150 years ago.
For collectors of railway arcana, the event—the first of a series—was a feast. The coaches, gleaming with teak and brass, had once languished, dilapidated, as a tailor’s premises in Shrivenham, which sold uniforms to graduates of an anti-aircraft gunnery course. An enthusiast in the Forest of Dean, miles from the nearest railway, had restored the engine (it arrived in London humbly on a lorry). The commemorative run on January 13th followed a secret test in the small hours last year. Enthusiasts drooled at the particular quality of steam, smoke and sound they would enjoy in the tunnels.
Boris Johnson, London’s mayor, stole the show from the railwaymen, highlighting what had been the “utterly revolutionary” idea of running railways underground. He called for more boldness on infrastructure projects. But London’s railways are doing well. Crossrail, the largest engineering project in Europe, is burrowing a new line under the city. And a short stretch of extra track, plus some tweaks and rebranding, has created one round it: the Overground, a snazzy new line mostly formed of neglected bits of the existing network. It would be quite suitable for steam trains too.
