In the quiet heart of a village where time moved like slow honey, there was a baker who could see the future in his bread.

One evening, as he kneaded dough by candlelight, the crumb of an apple pie began to glow faintly, its edges curling into swirling patterns of a forgotten city. The baker’s hands trembled—not with fear, but with wonder—his fingers tracing the shape of a towering spire piercing the misty horizon.

The next morning, the village elders gathered, their faces pale with disbelief. A child, no older than ten, stood before them, clutching a single, perfect apple pie crust, its edges still warm from the oven. Inside, the crust held a miniature model of the city’s spire, its brass gears humming faintly as if alive.

The elders whispered of omens, of prophecy. The baker, who had long known the power of his craft, smiled. "The future is not written in stone," he said, "but in the dough we shape." He placed the pie on a table, and as the villagers watched, the spire rose from the crust, its silhouette sharp against the evening sky.

That night, the village held a feast. The pie, now whole and golden, was served to all. As they ate, the spire—no longer a vision—became a reality. A new city, its spire gleaming under the stars, was carved into the earth beneath them, its foundations already laid in the village’s dreams.
