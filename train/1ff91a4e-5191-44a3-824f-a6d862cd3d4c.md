The rain pattered against the windows of the old bookstore, each drop a tiny whisper of secrets left untold. Inside, Elias sat at the cracked wooden table, his fingers tracing the edges of a yellowed newspaper clipping—*The Daily Gazette*, dated 1978—where a headline read **"Last Seen in Blackwood Hollow"**. His mother had found it tucked between the pages of her grandmother’s Bible after her funeral.

He exhaled, the scent of aged parchment and old coffee lingering in the air. The clipping was a photo of a girl—no older than sixteen—wearing a plaid skirt that had once been his grandmother’s. The caption read *"Lena Carter, vanished without a trace."* The photo was smudged, the girl’s face blurred, but there was something in her eyes, something that still flickered in Elias’s mind like a ghost.

He flipped to the next page. A list of names: **Lena Carter, Sarah Whitmore, Mary Beth Holloway**. All gone. All from Blackwood Hollow, the town where his mother’s family had lived for generations, until the disappearances started. The last entry was dated **October 1979**, the same month his grandmother vanished. His mother had never spoken of it, but Elias had seen the way she avoided the mirror after that night.

A knock at the door made him jump. His mother stood there, her hands wrapped around a steaming mug of tea. "You’re not supposed to be looking at this," she said, her voice quiet.

Elias didn’t look up. "I know."

She set the mug down. "You’re going to have to stop. They’re not coming back."

He stood abruptly, his chair scraping against the floor. The clipping slipped from his hands, landing on the floor. "I have to," he said, turning toward the door. His mother’s eyes widened, but she didn’t stop him. The moment he stepped outside, the rain picked up, turning the sidewalk into a slick mirror of his own reflection.

He walked toward the old church at the edge of town, its steeple piercing the gray sky. The wind carried the scent of damp earth and something older, something like iron and old prayers. His mother had always said the church was where the disappearances began. She had been right.

He reached the door, the hinges groaning under his weight. The inside was dark, the air thick with the scent of candles and something else—something metallic. His fingers brushed the wall, tracing the faded letters of a cross carved into the stone: **"Here lies what was forgotten."**

He turned and walked away, the church’s bell tolling behind him, distant and haunting. The rain followed, a slow, steady rain that never stopped.
