The old clock tower’s pendulum swung slow and steady, marking the hours as it had for decades. Each tick was a whisper of forgotten promises, each tock a warning no one heeded. Inside, Elias wiped the sweat from his brow, his fingers trembling—not from the heat, but from the weight of the letters still unopened.

On the desk, the stack of mail stood like a silent accusation: *"Reminder: The shipment deadline was yesterday."* The ink had faded, but the words still burned. His mother’s handwriting, neat and precise, had been replaced by the shaky script of a stranger who claimed to represent *"The Company."* *"Do not ignore this,"* it read, though Elias had already ignored everything else.

A knock at the door. The door opened before he could answer. His sister, Mira, stood there, her face pale. "They’re here," she said, her voice barely above a rasp. "The inspectors."

Elias turned to the window, where the city’s neon glow reflected off the rain-streaked glass. The reminder was no longer on paper—it was in the hollow between his ribs, the way his breath came too fast, the way his hands shook as he clutched the coffee cup that had gone cold. He should have returned the call. He should have burned the envelope. But the letter had already won. The weight of what he owed pressed down harder than the rain outside.

Outside, the wind howled through the alley, carrying the sound of sirens. Somewhere, a child laughed, too loud, too careless. The reminder wasn’t just in the letters anymore. It was in the way the world kept spinning, indifferent to his failures. The tower’s pendulum would keep swinging. The tock was coming.
