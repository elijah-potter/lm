The weight of the decision pressed against her like a physical force, pressing her chest against the cold kitchen table. Coffee steamed beside her, untouched, as her fingers traced the rim of the mug, tracing the same circles she’d drawn there for hours. The phone vibrated in her pocket—another missed call from the old friend who’d once shared her favorite hiking trails. She checked the time: 3:12 AM. The silence between the rings and the silence after, where only the hum of the fridge’s cycle sounded, was the same.

Outside, the city slept. Neon signs flickered in the window behind her, but they cast no warmth. She exhaled sharply, the breath curling into the air like smoke. What would happen if she picked the phone up? The answer was already there in the message waiting: *"You’re the first person I’ve called in two weeks. Don’t ghost me."* The words were simple, but the weight of them felt like a question: *Do you even care?* Or had she just been avoiding the effort of explaining that she was fine, that she’d had a long day, that she needed space?

The door creaked open. Her brother’s silhouette filled the frame, his shoulders hunched against the cold. He carried a duffel bag, the kind that held more than just clothes—it held the weight of another decision he’d already made. His gaze flicked to her, then to the phone in her hand. *"You should eat something,"* he said, and the words were a command, not a question. He pulled a bag of chips from his pocket, tossing them into her lap. She didn’t move. The chips hit her palm like stones.

*"Or don’t,"* he added, but the tone was different—less patient, more resigned. She opened the bag anyway, her fingers brushing the salt and crunch against her skin. The taste was bitter, sharp, but it was the first thing she’d eaten in days. She swallowed. The phone buzzed again. She ignored it.

The decision wasn’t about the night ahead—it wasn’t even about the friend who’d texted. It was about the version of herself she wanted to be when she picked up the phone. The one who answered with a smile. The one who stayed. The one who didn’t disappear. Or was it the one who didn’t care enough to stay? The answer was tangled in the static between the lines, and for the first time, she realized she didn’t know which thread to follow.

She reached for the phone, her fingers trembling. The screen lit up with a single message: *"I’m thinking of you."* The words were soft, but the implication was clear: *You’re thinking of me too.* Her thumb hovered over the screen. The light from the phone flickered like a candle in the dark. The decision wasn’t about the night ahead—it was about the life she wanted to live, one choice at a time. She picked up the phone.

The message was short: *"You’re going to be okay."* Then silence. The line went dead. The screen went black. The decision was made.
