The neon hum of the city pulsed through the glass, reflecting like scattered stars across the water’s dark surface. A woman stood at the edge of the pier, her fingers tracing the curve of the dock’s edge. The old camera—its lens cracked, the film canister half-empty—clutched in her palm.

She had taken this photograph the night before, the pier’s flickering lights casting long shadows across the waves. Now, as the tide rose, the image in her memory felt as sharp as the first frame had been: her reflection, blurred, dancing between the pier and the endless dark.

The photographer’s assistant, a boy with ink-stained fingers, handed her a fresh canister. “You’ll need this,” he said, his voice rough with exhaustion. She nodded, but her mind was already elsewhere—back to the first time she’d stood here, the first time she’d seen the pier’s reflection twist into something unrecognizable.

A ship’s horn blared. The assistant turned away, his back stiff with fatigue. She watched him go, then turned back to the water. The pier was still there, but the pier was no longer the pier. It was a mirror, and the reflection was her, but not as she was—smaller, older, her hair a different color, her clothes faded.

The assistant didn’t look back. He walked toward the dock’s edge, where the camera hung from a rusted hook. She reached for it, but the water lapped at her ankles. The film canister slipped from her grip. It sank, dissolving into the black depths.

The pier was still there. The city still hummed. And somewhere, in the distance, a ship’s horn called again.
