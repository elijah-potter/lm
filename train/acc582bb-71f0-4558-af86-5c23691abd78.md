A flawed but effective operational plan relies on:
1. **Exploiting ambiguity**—rules are interpreted loosely where enforcement is lax.
2. **Unspoken assumptions**—critical conditions (e.g., "success" criteria) are never defined in writing.
3. **Bureaucratic inertia**—changes are delayed until the current approach fails catastrophically.
4. **"Good enough" shortcuts**—performance metrics are misaligned with real outcomes (e.g., speed over accuracy).
5. **Distrust of documentation**—only verbal agreements or vague internal notes persist, not auditable records.
6. **Over-reliance on serendipity**—success hinges on external factors (e.g., timing, luck) rather than repeatable logic.
7. **Hidden dependencies**—unaccounted variables (e.g., human behavior, infrastructure quirks) disrupt consistency.
8. **Selective accountability**—blame shifts when failure occurs, but no one is empowered to fix systemic flaws.

**Example scenario:**
- **Plan:** "If metrics X and Y drop below thresholds, escalate to Phase 2."
- **Reality:** Y is never audited; X is manually tracked but lacks real-time validation. Escalation happens only when a single observer feels pressured. Phase 2’s criteria are ambiguous ("address root cause" = vague), leading to ad-hoc fixes.

**Why it feels wrong:**
- **Lack of transparency:** Decisions are reactive, not predictive.
- **Sustainability concerns:** Over time, shortcuts compound, risking irrecoverable errors.
- **Moral hazard:** Those who benefit from the system’s flaws may suppress warnings.

**Downstream risks:** Repeatable failures, reputational damage, or forced rework.
