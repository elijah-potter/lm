The first time Mia saw the strange, glowing mushrooms in the old oak tree’s roots, she thought she was dreaming. They pulsed faintly with a soft violet light, like fireflies trapped in amber, growing between the cracked bark and the damp earth. Her little brother, Leo, had been digging around in the woods behind their house all morning, and she had let him. He’d always been the adventurous one, the kind who’d climb trees and follow abandoned trails, while she stayed close to the porch, watching the neighborhood kids play soccer in the park.

“You’ll get lost,” she’d said, but he just grinned and shoved his hands into his pockets. “This is better than soccer.”

Now, as he scooped handfuls of the earth and tossed it aside, the mushrooms were there, hidden under the leaves. She’d never noticed them before. Leo squinted at them, then pointed. “Look, Mom. They’re glowing.”

Mia’s stomach twisted. She’d always been afraid of the dark, of things that moved in the shadows. But these weren’t shadows. They were *alive*. And they weren’t supposed to be in their backyard.

That night, she sat on her bed, staring at the ceiling. The mushrooms had been there in the daylight, but they’d grown brighter. The violet light now shimmered like a star fallen to earth. Leo had told her about the old stories—the ones her grandpa used to tell about the *Hollowroot*, a plant that could grant wishes but at a cost. If you asked for something too big, too impossible, it would take something else in return. Something personal.

Mia’s fingers curled into a fist. She’d never asked for anything before. But Leo had been asking for days. He’d told her about the abandoned house on the edge of town, the one where the windows were always dark, the one with the sign that said *Do Not Enter*. It was where the kids from her school used to go, the ones who disappeared sometimes, never to return. Leo said the house was haunted, but he also said the mushrooms were growing there too. That if you dug under the roots, you could find the door.

She should’ve told him to stop. She should’ve told him to run. But Leo was already gone, and she’d never been good at running. She’d been running for years, ever since her mom left when Mia was eight, ever since her dad moved in with his girlfriend and started calling her *Mia* instead of *my daughter*. She’d learned to hide, to make herself small, to disappear.

That morning, she found Leo’s shoes by the porch. One was missing. The other was half-buried in the dirt, the sole worn smooth from years of wear. She followed the trail to the woods, past the old apple tree where the squirrels gathered in the winter. The mushrooms were everywhere now, their light spreading like ink on water, drawing her forward.

She dug.

The earth gave way, and there was the door. Not a door, really—just a hollow space, like the inside of a tree trunk. The air smelled of damp stone and something older, something like the earth itself. Leo stood at the mouth of the tunnel, his hands pressed against the damp wall. “You came,” he whispered.

Mia’s breath hitched. “What are you doing here?”

Leo’s eyes were wide, his face pale. “I needed to see it for myself. You’re the only one who’s ever believed in us.”

She wanted to ask what he meant. But then he stepped forward, and the light from the mushrooms flared brighter, like a beacon. The door groaned open, and the air inside was thick with the scent of rain and something metallic. A voice, not human, not quite, whispered from the darkness.

*“You have asked for this.”*

Mia’s blood turned to ice. She’d never asked for anything. But Leo had. And now, they were both inside.
