The effectiveness of a leader is often in the eye of the beholder. Regardless of the leader's success in getting a small business off the ground, growing revenue or managing conflicts, whether his leadership skills and behavioral traits are perceived as effective may have nothing to do with the traits themselves. Employee perceptions of the leader are tied strongly to common stereotypes and beliefs about leadership.
Perceptions of the Leader
In some cases, it does not matter what traits the leader possesses if others have already determined that the leader himself is unsatisfactory. Traits that the leader perceives as positive -- and that would be seen as positive if displayed by another -- are perceived as weaknesses, just because of who the leader is. Keith Dowding of Australian National University notes that "actions that would have appeared strong if carried out by someone with a reputation for strength will be viewed as the actions of a weak person, perhaps trying to be strong."
The gender of the leader affects the perception of traits displayed by that leader. While decisiveness, assertiveness and confidence typically are perceived as positive traits in male leaders, these same traits are seen as weaknesses in female leaders, particularly by men. Likewise, while men can be perceived favorably as passionate about a cause, women displaying the same behavior may be perceived as overemotional and weak. Women may need to be more conscious about the leadership skills they employ -- and actively increase their use of traditionally feminine traits such as warmth and teamwork -- to be successful as a leader.
Stereotypes About Leadership
General stereotypes and beliefs about the specific traits that make a good leader can mean people perceive positive traits as weaknesses. A 2011 study from the University of Amsterdam found that people erroneously believed leaders with narcissistic traits were better leaders than those who sought participation, input and feedback from all group members. Narcissistic behavior, such as dominance, was seen as a strength, while the collaborative behavior was perceived as a weakness. This perception was contrary to the finding that the participative leaders performed significantly better than the narcissistic leaders on the assigned tasks.
Failure to Adapt
Sometimes a leader's positive traits ultimately turn into a weakness. Leaders who have worked their way into a leadership position often got there because of certain behaviors. For example, a small business owner may have established his brand in a competitive market through perfectionism and active hands-on control of every area of operations. These positive traits worked well in the past, so it's understandable that the leader is reluctant to move away from these successful techniques. But over-reliance on these traits when they no longer work for the situation -- for example, failing to delegate despite being overwhelmed, because the leader wants to retain control -- can cause a previously positive trait to be perceived as a weakness.
