The **Old Market District** in **Valmora** operates on an unspoken code enforced by tradition, reputation, and tacit consent.

- **The Street Dealers**: Vendors leave their wares on a shared ledger board (*"The Ledger"*) in the central square. If a merchant’s stock is low, their next order is implicitly denied unless they’ve been generous to others recently. The board’s chalk marks track trade balances; a red line means you owe someone.
- **The Watchers**: No one openly patrols, but a few silent figures—older merchants, retired guild enforcers—observe from doorways. A stolen item is "lost" if it’s not returned by dawn. A debt is settled in kind, not coin.
- **The Guild Halls**: The **Silk Weavers’ Guild** and **Blacksmiths’ Union** enforce honor through indirect means. A dispute is settled by a jury of peers, where the loser must publicly "atone" (e.g., mending a neighbor’s fence, repairing a guild member’s tool). Refusal means you’re marked as unreliable.
- **The Tavern Rules**: The **"Empty Throne"**—a seat always sits empty at the bar. If you claim it, you’re committing to a round of drinks for the entire group. Drinking too much before noon means you’re "too warm" to bargain, a warning for future debts.
- **The Bridges**: The **Stone Bridge** has a hidden step. Those who step on it must wait until someone else steps off before crossing. Ignoring this is interpreted as arrogance.
- **The Bazaar’s "Ghosts"**: The **Copper Market** deals in futures—trading promises for future favors. A "ghost" is a merchant who’s never met you but has a reputation. Deal with them, and you gain leverage; ignore them, and you risk being "forgotten."
- **The Children’s Rule**: The youngest child in a family gets to choose the next holiday feast. Refusing to comply is seen as cruelty.
- **The River Tides**: The **Blackwater** floods seasonally. Fishermen must share their catch with anyone who arrived before the tide. Taking more than your share means you’re a "tide-stealer."
- **The Graveyard Code**: The **Silent Cemetery** has no headstones. The dead are marked by a single stone, placed by the family who buried them. If you dig up a stone, you’re accused of desecration. If you leave a stone unplanted, you’re a "ghost" in the land.

The system thrives on **reciprocity**—small acts of kindness or generosity earn trust, while grudges are settled in whispers. Violating the rules isn’t written down, but the consequences are felt in the next handshake, the next bargain, or the next time you pass a neighbor’s door. The stronger the community’s collective memory, the more the rules hold.
