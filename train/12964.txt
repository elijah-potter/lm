Pears (Pyrus spp.) are as easy to grow as apples and produce slightly earlier in the season, making them a good choice for home orchards in U.S. Department of Agriculture plant hardiness zones 4 through 9. When pear trees begin to age, or it becomes abundantly clear that the one or two trees planted in the orchard aren't enough, you might begin to ponder propagation. There are a few very successful ways to start new pears, but in general, rooting suckers is not among them.
Pear Tree Propagation
Generally, pears are cloned from other pears that have particular qualities that a grower wants to continue. Perhaps the parent tree is a heavy producer, or it produces spectacular fruits. Grafting pieces of a tree with desirable fruiting characteristics onto compatible roots from another tree that are strong and disease-tolerant creates an ideal fruit tree. Although the top and bottom of the tree are different cultivars, they work together to produce pears.
Pear rootstocks are created from either quince or pear trees that aren't necessarily good at fruiting, but are strong growers. Quince rootstocks result in dwarf or semi-dwarf pear trees in areas where frost isn't a problem. Several pear rootstocks are available, each lending its own disease resistance and specific benefits to the pear. Common pear rootstocks include Pyrus communis, Old Home x Farmingdale clones, P. calleryana and P. betulaefolia.
Suckers are upright shoots that grow out of the roots of the rootstock portion of a pear tree. Although the top portion of the tree may be producing beautiful fruit, the rootstock is likely a variety that is not a strong fruit-bearer, so the suckers won't be, either. When the rootstock is quince, the suckers coming from the pear tree will produce quince trees. The only suckers that will recreate the parent tree are those that come from pears grown on their own roots -- these trees lack a graft union scar, making them easy to identify.
Suckers are particularly tricky to start because, unlike seedlings, they do not have a root system independent of the parent tree. In the fall, dig the smallest suckers and as much root from the mother tree as feasible -- do not cut the suckers off at ground level. Plant the young trees in a fast-draining location and water them well. Keep the soil moist, but not soggy, until the parent tree is dormant. When the parent breaks bud in the spring, resume watering the suckers thoroughly. Once new growth appears, gradually reduce water to prevent root rot.
