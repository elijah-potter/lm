In the dim glow of flickering lanterns, the old tavern’s wooden floor creaked under the weight of Elias, who sat hunched over the cracked table, his fingers tracing the rim of his glass. The bartender, a grizzled man with a scar running down his cheek, slid a bowl of stew toward him without comment.

"Another round?" Elias asked, but the bartender only shook his head. Instead, he pulled a yellowed parchment from beneath the counter, unfolding it carefully. "This was found in the cellar," he said, handing it to Elias. "From the last patrons. Thought you might like to hear it."

Elias took the parchment, his breath catching as he unfolded it. The ink was faded, but the words were legible:

*"They say the river beneath the bridge is no longer the same. Where it used to sing to the reeds, now it whispers secrets—names of those who drowned, names that should have stayed buried. One evening, a woman named Liora walked along the docks, her cloak tattered, her eyes red. She asked of the river’s voice, if it remembered her. The current answered: ‘We all forget what we were.’ By dawn, the docks were empty. But the next morning, a child was found washed up on the shore, wearing her cloak, her fingers clutching a single pearl—a memory of the river’s promise."*

Elias’ eyes widened. "You’re telling me this story *is* a story," he murmured, passing the parchment back.

The bartender nodded. "And it’s not the first." He reached into his coat, pulling out a second, smaller scroll. "Here. From the night before last." He unfolded it:

*"The town’s blacksmith, Old Man Harkin, swore he saw a shadow in the forge’s glow—a man in ragged robes, his face half-lit by the flames. He asked why the fire burned so bright, if the anvil could hear his voice. The smith laughed and spat. The next morning, the forge was gone, replaced by a smooth stone slab. On it, carved into the stone: ‘The forge remembers what it was.’"*

Elias’ hands trembled. "What does it mean?" he asked.

The bartender lit a single candle, its flame casting long shadows. "Maybe it means," he said slowly, "that stories don’t just tell the past—they’re the past." The tavern’s single window rattled in the wind. Outside, the river murmured.
