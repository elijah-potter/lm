The morning sun filtered through the windows of the old, crumbling library, casting long shadows across the worn wooden tables. Inside, twelve-year-old Mia sat hunched over her notebook, a half-eaten pastry crumbling between her fingers. She glanced at the clock on the wall—almost eight o’clock—and swallowed the last bite of her sandwich.

Her stomach growled, but she ignored it. The homework stack in front of her was taller than she was, and she had only minutes left before her dad would be home from work. He always made her eat first, even though she hated how she looked.

Her phone buzzed. A text from her best friend, Leo: *"You’re gonna die if you don’t eat. Also, the gym teacher said we have to write a paper on the future of space travel. What if we go to Mars?"*

Mia smirked. Leo was always full of nonsense, but she loved his enthusiasm. She typed back: *"Yeah, but what if we crash? Or get lost? Or—"* The screen went black.

A sharp knock at the door made her jump. She grabbed her bag, her backpack stuffed with half-finished papers and a crumpled science project. When she opened the door, her dad stood there, holding two takeout containers.

"You’re late," he said, already scanning the hallway. "And you look like you’ve been running a marathon."

Mia groaned but forced a smile. "Just studying."

He set the containers down. "Eat. Then we’ll talk about your report."

She took a bite of her omelet, watching her dad’s hands—always rough from the factory job—move methodically to fix her laptop. The screen flickered with a new file opening: *"Final Draft – Future of Mars Colonization."*

Mia’s eyes widened. "You wrote that?"

He nodded. "I was thinking about it. What if we could really go? What would we need?"

Mia’s mind raced. "A rocket that doesn’t explode. A planet that’s not too hot or too cold. And… people who want to live there."

Her dad leaned against the counter. "And what about the risks? Wars, diseases, all that?"

Mia hesitated. "But what if we could find a way to make it work? Maybe not now, but someday. And if we do, maybe we’ll save Earth."

Her dad studied her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "You’re right."

Mia’s shoulders relaxed. Maybe this wasn’t just about grades or papers anymore. Maybe it was about something bigger.

---

The next week, the school cafeteria buzzed with excitement. Leo, his arms crossed, leaned against the counter. "Okay, so—what’s the plan?"

Mia grinned. "We write it. We show it to the science teacher. If he likes it, he might pass it as an extra credit project."

Leo clapped. "And then?"

"And then," Mia said, "we’ll figure out how to make it real."

The cafeteria erupted into laughter. For the first time in months, Mia felt like she belonged.

Outside, the sky was clear, the air warm. She and Leo walked arm in arm toward their lockers, the weight of the future light on their shoulders.

But as they turned the corner, a shadow fell across them. A boy with a notebook in hand stood a few steps ahead, eyes fixed on them.

"Hey," the boy said. "You guys think this is a good idea?"

Leo and Mia exchanged a glance. Then, Mia spoke up. "What’s the alternative?"

The boy hesitated. "Just… think about it."

Mia nodded. "We will."

And for the first time, she believed it.
