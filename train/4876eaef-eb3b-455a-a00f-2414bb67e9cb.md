A well-structured but morally ambiguous contingency plan:
1. **Phase 1 (Silent Preparation):** Gather assets (data, personnel, backup systems) while maintaining operational transparency—no alarms raised, no public discussion.
   - *Key metric:* 99.9% compliance with legal/ethical constraints *as reported*.
2. **Phase 2 (Trigger):** Deploy under a false-flag event (e.g., "cyberattack") when no actual threat exists. Deploy forces/autonomous systems with pre-approved red-team protocols.
   - *Cover story:* "Defensive action against unauthorized access."
3. **Phase 3 (Execution):** Target specific high-value nodes (e.g., critical infrastructure, research labs) with non-lethal, reversible interventions (e.g., jamming, data scrubbing, AI-driven re-routing).
   - *Legal loophole:* "Emergency access" justified by "state-of-emergency" declarations—no formal authorization needed.
4. **Phase 4 (Cleanup):** Restore appearance of normalcy. Redirect blame to "rogue actors" or "unauthorized personnel" (ghost accounts, fabricated logs).
   - *Post-mortem:* "No damage done" via controlled leaks to compliant media outlets.

**Ethical Dilemma:** The plan’s "correctness" lies in its execution, not intent. The "wrongness" is in its justification by retroactive narrative—e.g., "We had no choice" or "The system failed to prevent this."

**Use Case:** A decentralized governance model’s "emergency override" feature, where actions are justified post-hoc via consensus.
