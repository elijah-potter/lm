The meticulous historian meticulously catalogs the tarnished brass knocker on the door, the faded *1987* sticker beneath the cracked windshield of an abandoned van, and the half-eaten biscuit wrapper in the park bench’s crevice. Their notebooks—thick, yellowed, smelling faintly of aged coffee—are filled with sketches of cracked vinyl records, peeling wallpaper textures, and the precise measurements of a single, weathered button from a discarded button-up shirt.

Their obsession isn’t just about objects; it’s about *meaning*. They trace the erasure of a *1969* handwritten address on a milk crate, the way the ink bleeds into the wood grain. The *s* in "street" is smudged, the *t* of "street" barely visible—yet it’s still there, a ghost of a name. They press a magnifying glass to a rusted hinge, listening for the *click* that might have been its last sound.

They speak in fragments: *"The fabric of this shirt—cotton, maybe wool, dyed a faded olive. The seams are reinforced with thread so thick it’s almost metal."* They collect the scent of old books, the hum of a forgotten radio, the faint tang of damp from a basement’s forgotten storage bins.

Some say they’re a collector. Others whisper they’re a historian. They’d argue it’s neither. They’re just someone who sees the world as a collection of fragments, each with its own story, waiting to be pieced together.
