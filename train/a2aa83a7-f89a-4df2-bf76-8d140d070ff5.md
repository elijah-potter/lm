The late afternoon sun cast long shadows across Ward B, painting the room in hues of amber and rose. I adjusted the blinds slightly for Mr. Alistair Finch, a gentleman of eighty-two years who occupied bed three. He was a retired clockmaker, a fact he shared with an almost metronomic regularity, each recounting punctuated by a gentle smile and a twinkle in his kind eyes. 

Mr. Finch had been admitted three days prior, experiencing a sudden onset of dizziness and a concerning drop in blood pressure. The initial assessment revealed a mild case of dehydration and a touch of anemia, likely exacerbated by his age and a recent, minor fall at his home. He was remarkably stoic about the incident, more concerned, he insisted, about the potential damage to his antique grandfather clock than his own well-being.

“It’s a magnificent piece, you know,” he’s explained on his first day, gesturing with a frail hand. “Built in 1888. Keeps perfect time, it does. A testament to craftsmanship.”

My primary responsibility, beyond the standard vital signs and medication administration, had become a subtle reassurance. He missed his routine, the familiar tick-tock of his workshop, the feel of tiny gears between his fingertips. The hospital environment, with its insistent beeping and hurried footsteps, was a stark contrast to the quiet precision of his life. 

Today, I found him staring out the window, a melancholic expression softening his features. “The robins are back,” he observed, his voice a low murmur. “Always return in the spring. Reliable, aren't they?”

I pulled up a chair beside his bed. “They are,” I agreed. “Just like your clocks, I imagine.”

He chuckled, a dry, rustling sound. “Indeed. Precision and predictability. Qualities to be admired.”

I noticed his hand trembling slightly as he reached for the water glass on his bedside table. I offered him a clean cloth to dab his lips. “How are you feeling today, Mr. Finch?”

“A little… restless,” he admitted. “Missing the rhythm, you see. The steady pulse of things.”

I understood. The hospital, despite our best efforts, could be disorienting. I decided to deviate slightly from the standard protocol. “I’d be happy to bring you a small, battery-operated clock,” I offered. “Just something to provide a little… comfort.”

His eyes widened, a genuine spark of pleasure lighting his face. “That’s… that’s very thoughtful of you, Nurse Eleanor. Very thoughtful indeed.”

I smiled. It was a small gesture, a minor adjustment to his environment, but I hoped it would offer him a small measure of solace. Later, as I made my rounds, I checked on him again. He was asleep, the tiny clock ticking softly on his bedside table. The gentle rhythm filled the quiet space, a miniature echo of the life he cherished. It wasn't a replacement for his workshop, of course, but perhaps it was a reminder – a small, reassuring tick – that time, in its own way, continued to move forward.
