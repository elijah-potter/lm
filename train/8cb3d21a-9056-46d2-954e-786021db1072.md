## TIL the “Smell of Rain” is Actually Multiple Compounds

Today I learned that the distinctive scent many of us associate with rain, often referred to as "petrichor," isn't a single aroma but a complex mixture of several chemical compounds. For years, scientists believed it was solely attributable to geosmin, a metabolic byproduct produced by bacteria in the soil. While geosmin *is* a significant contributor, it's now understood that another compound, plant oils released into the air by impact, also plays a crucial role.

The term “petrichor” itself was coined in 1964 by two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and R.G. Thomas, who were investigating the phenomenon. They derived it from the Greek words *petra* (stone) and *ichor* (the ethereal fluid that flows in the veins of the gods in Greek mythology). 

The process is fascinating: during dry periods, oils from plants accumulate on rocks and soil. When rain falls, the raindrops disrupt this oily layer, aerosolizing the compounds into the air. Geosmin, with its earthy aroma, is particularly noticeable, while the plant oils contribute a fresher, more complex scent. The intensity of the smell varies depending on the type of vegetation and the dryness of the preceding period, making each rainfall experience unique.
