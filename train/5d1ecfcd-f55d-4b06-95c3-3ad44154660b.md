## Mycelium-Bound Aggregate: A Sustainable Construction Alternative

### Briefing for City Planners

The construction industry contributes significantly to global carbon emissions, largely due to the production of cement and the transportation of materials (United Nations Environment Programme, 2021). A promising avenue for reducing this impact lies in the utilization of mycelium – the root structure of fungi – to bind naturally occurring aggregates like sand and clay into structurally sound building blocks. This "mycelium-bound aggregate" (MBA) approach offers a potentially carbon-negative construction method, particularly when combined with locally sourced materials.

The process involves mixing aggregate with a nutrient-rich substrate, typically agricultural waste like rice husks or straw. This mixture is then inoculated with fungal spores, which rapidly colonize the aggregate, binding the particles together through a complex network of hyphae (filamentous fungal structures) (Stamatis, 2005). The resulting blocks, once dried, possess compressive strength comparable to some traditional clay bricks (Jones et al., 2019), and offer excellent thermal insulation properties due to the porous nature of the mycelial matrix (Jiang et al., 2018).

The primary environmental advantage is the potential for carbon sequestration. The fungi consume atmospheric carbon dioxide during their growth, effectively locking it within the building material (Read, 2007). Furthermore, the use of agricultural waste reduces landfill burden and minimizes the need for energy-intensive material production. MBA structures can also be designed for disassembly and composting at the end of their lifespan, returning nutrients to the soil (Eriksson et al., 2011).

However, challenges remain. MBA’s moisture sensitivity is a significant concern. Prolonged exposure to water can weaken the mycelial bonds and compromise structural integrity (Brotton et al., 2017).  The long-term durability of MBA in diverse climates requires further investigation. The potential for fungal degradation by pests or competing microorganisms also warrants attention.  Finally, while the process utilizes waste materials, the scale-up of fungal cultivation and inoculation processes for widespread construction requires substantial infrastructure development.  Pilot projects incorporating MBA in non-load-bearing applications, such as interior partitions or landscaping elements, are recommended as a prudent first step.



### Technical Appendix for Civil Engineers

**Material Properties and Structural Performance:**

Mycelium-bound aggregate (MBA) exhibits a complex microstructure dictated by fungal species, substrate composition, and growth conditions. *Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster mushroom) is frequently employed due to its rapid growth rate and robust hyphal network (Sheridan et al., 2014).  The hyphae act as a natural adhesive, creating a matrix that binds aggregates, primarily sand and clay, with varying degrees of success depending on aggregate size distribution and shape (Li et al., 2020).  Compressive strength typically ranges from 2.0 to 10.0 MPa, depending on fungal density and aggregate compaction (Volker et al., 2016).  Tensile strength remains significantly lower, approximately 0.1-0.5 MPa, highlighting the material's brittleness and susceptibility to cracking under flexural loads.

The thermal conductivity of MBA is exceptionally low, typically between 0.04 and 0.08 W/m·K, attributable to the air-filled voids within the mycelial matrix (Hofmann et al., 2019).  This characteristic provides excellent insulation, reducing heating and cooling demands in buildings.  However, the high porosity also contributes to water absorption, a critical performance limitation.  Water absorption rates can exceed 50% by weight after 24 hours of immersion, leading to a reduction in compressive strength and increased susceptibility to freeze-thaw damage (Preuss et al., 2018).

**Durability and Maintenance Considerations:**

The long-term durability of MBA is heavily influenced by environmental factors.  Exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation degrades the mycelial network, reducing its binding capacity (Wang et al., 2019).  The presence of competing microorganisms, particularly bacteria and other fungi, can lead to mycelial breakdown and structural failure (Donn et al., 2017).  Insect infestations, particularly wood-boring beetles, can also compromise the integrity of the material.

Mitigation strategies include the application of natural fungicides, such as chitosan (derived from shellfish exoskeletons), to inhibit microbial growth (Liu et al., 2016), and the incorporation of UV-absorbing pigments into the substrate.  Surface treatments with hydrophobic coatings, such as silanes or waxes, can reduce water absorption and improve resistance to freeze-thaw cycles, but these introduce their own environmental trade-offs.  Regular inspection and maintenance, including the application of protective coatings and the removal of organic debris, are essential for extending the service life of MBA structures.

**Unanswered Research Questions:**

*   **Genetic Optimization:** Can fungal strains be genetically modified to enhance binding strength, improve UV resistance, and reduce susceptibility to microbial degradation?
*   **Hybrid Composites:** What is the optimal combination of MBA with other sustainable materials, such as bamboo or hemp fibers, to improve tensile strength and overall structural performance?
*   **Scale-Up Bioreactor Design:** What are the most efficient and cost-effective bioreactor designs for large-scale mycelial cultivation and aggregate inoculation?
*   **Life Cycle Assessment:** A comprehensive life cycle assessment is needed to quantify the environmental benefits of MBA construction compared to conventional methods, considering all stages from material sourcing to end-of-life disposal.
