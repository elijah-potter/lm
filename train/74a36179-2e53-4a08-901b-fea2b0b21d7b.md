The garden at the end of the lane was quieter than expected—just the hum of distant cicadas and the slow drip of rain against the old oak’s bark. Mrs. Hargrove’s teacup held no more than a whisper of Earl Grey, though she had been told it was "strong enough." The letter in her hand was folded so neatly the edges were barely visible, as if the words inside had been stitched together by a patient hand.

She had known, of course. The way her fingers trembled when she lit the match was the same as the day she buried her first cat. But today, she had chosen to pretend the sky was still blue, even though the thistle in her hair had gone brown before the frost.

The mailman’s knock came once, then twice—too many times, like a warning. She reached for the envelope before the door creased, and inside, the paper was so thin it felt like it might dissolve if she breathed too hard. The name at the top was familiar, but not quite. It was the kind of name that lingered in the corners of conversations, the kind that made people glance away.
