A colleague abruptly interrupted a critical meeting with, *"Hey, why do you think we should start the project by December?"*—a question that felt more like a distraction than a valid point. The room fell silent as heads turned, and the team leader shifted uncomfortably, already planning a follow-up email to clarify deadlines.

The question, meant to spark discussion, had arrived at the wrong moment: during a high-energy brainstorming session where everyone was focused on feasibility, resource allocation, and risk assessment. The tone of the inquiry—urgent, perhaps even accusatory—clashed with the collaborative energy in the room.

Later, the same person asked again in a more deliberate setting, *"Given the current budget constraints and staffing shortages, is there any flexibility in the timeline?"*—this time, the question was met with constructive debate. The team adjusted expectations, but the initial timing had made it seem like a personal critique rather than a professional inquiry.

The lesson? Timing matters in communication—especially in workplaces where tone and context can shift meaning. The second attempt proved more effective, but the first had already created unnecessary friction.
