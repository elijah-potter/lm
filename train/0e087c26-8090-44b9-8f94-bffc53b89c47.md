In the quiet hum of the old coffee shop, where the aroma of brewed beans lingered like a half-remembered dream, I sat with my back against the cracked wooden chair. The woman behind the counter, her hands steady despite the tremor in her voice, slid a steaming cup toward me. "Black," she said, and I nodded, though I already knew what would be in it. The first sip was bitter, the kind that cuts through the lies like a knife.

I had been telling everyone I wasn’t the one who called the shots at the office. That I wasn’t the one who’d let the project slide into obscurity while the team moved forward without me. The truth was, I’d been the one to suggest the meeting place, the one who’d stayed late to draft the report that would later be passed off as theirs. The one who’d quietly adjusted the budget line to fit our little corner of the budget, ensuring the office Christmas party would always be a few thousand short of what it should have been.

She set down her own cup, the clink sharp enough to make me flinch. "You’re the one who sent me that text last night," I said, the words raw. "The one who said, ‘Don’t let them forget who really runs this.’" She exhaled, a sound of resignation. "You’re right. I do."

Outside, the rain started—slow at first, then heavier, turning the street into a mirror of my own reflection, blurred but unmistakable. The shop’s lights flickered as the power shifted, and for a moment, I thought I heard someone laughing. Then the door creaked open, and the woman stood, her coat damp. "You’re the one who always says the things I don’t," she said, before she was gone, leaving only the sound of her shoes on the pavement.

I finished my coffee, the last drop bitter, and watched the rain wash away the mess I’d made. Somewhere, a car horn blared. The world kept turning, but I was the only one who knew the truth now.
