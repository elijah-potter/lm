## TIL the “Phantom Islands” of Cartography Were Once Believed to Exist

Today I learned about the fascinating history of "phantom islands" – landmasses that appeared on maps for centuries, despite never actually existing. These weren't simple mapping errors; they were often meticulously documented and even visited by explorers who reported finding evidence of their presence.

The most famous example is Buss, an island supposedly located in the Atlantic Ocean, west of Ireland. First reported in 1433 by Portuguese explorer Diogo de Silves, Buss appeared on numerous maps for over four hundred years, including those of Mercator and Ortelius. Subsequent explorers searched for it, and some even claimed to have sighted it, contributing to its continued inclusion on nautical charts. Similar phantom islands include Frisland, Hy-Brasil (often appearing off the coast of Ireland), and Santa Cruz.

The phenomenon arose from a combination of factors. Misinterpreted observations, wishful thinking (the allure of new lands for trade and colonization), and the tendency to fill in blank spaces on maps with plausible, albeit fictional, features all played a role. As cartographers relied on secondhand accounts and passed information down through generations, errors compounded and solidified into accepted "facts." It wasn’t until the rise of more accurate surveying techniques and oceanic exploration in the 19th century that the existence of these phantom islands was definitively disproven. The persistence of these false landmasses serves as a remarkable reminder of the evolving nature of knowledge and the power of confirmation bias in shaping our understanding of the world.
