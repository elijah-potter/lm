In the quiet hum of a city that forgets its name, a man with a typewriter sat by a window, fingers dancing over keys like forgotten prayers. The city’s ghosts—ghosts of laughter, tears, and half-written dreams—whispered through the cracks in the pane, blending with the rain that dripped in steady, rhythmic drops onto the open page.

The first page was a storm of ideas, chaotic and raw, as if the universe itself had spilled its secrets onto the paper. He worked until his hands ached, until the ink ran thin, until the words blurred into something else—silence.

Then came the second page. Here, the chaos began to unravel. The city’s ghosts retreated, replaced by the faint, deliberate rhythm of a metronome counting out a melody only he could hear. He found his voice, not in the thunder of the first draft, but in the careful, deliberate strokes of the second.

On the third page, the words began to form sentences, and sentences became paragraphs. The city’s name faded, swallowed by the ink as he wrote of love that wasn’t love, of choices that were never made, of moments that slipped through his fingers like sand. The rain became a metaphor for time, for the way it slips away, unnoticed, until there’s nothing left but the echoes of what was.

He paused. The typewriter clicked once, then stopped. Outside, a single bird took flight, its wings a blur against the gray sky.

The last line was this: *"The window was ajar just enough for the wind to carry the scent of damp earth and old paper."*
