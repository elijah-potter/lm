The late afternoon sun cast long shadows across the recovery room, painting the walls in hues of orange and gold. I adjusted the blinds slightly, ensuring Mr. Alistair Finch wouldn't be bothered by the glare. He was a quiet man, Mr. Finch, a retired clockmaker with hands that still trembled slightly, even after the successful removal of a benign tumor from his left wrist. 

He’s been a patient on 3 West for nearly a week, a stoic presence amidst the flurry of activity. Initially, he’s been understandably anxious, his usual meticulous nature exacerbated by the uncertainty of surgery. I’m Eleanor Vance, and I’ve been his primary nurse for the past three days. I find that a calm demeanor and attentive listening often do more than any medication.

"Mr. Finch," I said, approaching his bedside with a fresh glass of water. "How are you feeling this evening?"

He opened his eyes, a faint smile playing on his lips. "Much better, Miss Vance. The discomfort is lessening. Thank you." His voice was soft, a little raspy from disuse.

"I’m glad to hear it," I replied, placing the water within easy reach. "The pain medication is working well, isn't it?"

"Indeed. Remarkably so. I appreciate the care you and your colleagues have provided." He paused, then added, "Clockmaking requires a steady hand, you see. A tremor like this… it’s quite disruptive to the craft."

I nodded understandingly. "I can imagine. It takes a great deal of precision." I knew from reviewing his chart that he’s spent nearly fifty years crafting intricate timepieces. It’s a legacy he clearly treasured.

“My grandfather taught me,” he continued, a hint of warmth entering his voice. “He said the most important thing about a clock isn’t just that it tells the time, but that it provides a sense of order. A reassurance that things will continue as they should.”

I sat on the edge of his bed, adjusting the blanket around his legs. "That’s a beautiful philosophy, Mr. Finch. And I think you’ve certainly succeeded in creating that sense of order for others."

He chuckled softly. “A modest compliment, Miss Vance, but appreciated nonetheless.” He then asked, "Do you have any hobbies, Miss Vance?"

I smiled. “I enjoy gardening. There’s something quite therapeutic about nurturing life, I find.”

"A worthy pursuit," he agreed. "Everything has its rhythm, doesn’t it? The turning of the seasons, the ticking of a clock, the growth of a flower…"

I checked his intravenous drip and noted his vital signs, all within acceptable parameters. "Your hand will regain its strength in time, Mr. Finch. It will take patience and diligent physiotherapy, but you’ll be back to crafting your clocks before you know it."

He looked at his bandaged wrist, then back at me. "I certainly hope so, Miss Vance. I have a grandfather clock I’m restoring for my granddaughter. She’s turning sixteen next year, and I want it to be ready for her.”

The thought of that restored clock, a symbol of family and tradition, seemed to lift his spirits. I stood, ready to leave him to his thoughts. 

"Rest well, Mr. Finch," I said. "I'll be back to check on you shortly."

As I walked out of the room, I felt a quiet satisfaction. Nursing isn't just about administering medication and monitoring vital signs. It’s about offering a moment of connection, a shared understanding, a reassurance that even in a place of healing, there is still beauty and order to be found. And sometimes, it’s about simply listening to a man talk about clocks.
