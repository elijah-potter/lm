A makeshift campground on a cracked asphalt lot near a highway overpass, its flickering streetlights casting long shadows. Plastic tarps strung between weathered utility poles form a rusted network of shelter, some sagging from rain, others half-buried under discarded bags of chips and empty beer bottles. The air smells of diesel and damp earth, with a faint metallic tang from a distant car exhaust. A single, flickering neon sign—*Open 24hr*—glows in the corner, its letters half-erased by time and neglect. Inside, a half-built modular home hums with the sound of a fridge cycling on and off, its glass door fogged with condensation. The floor is a patchwork of salvaged pallets, vinyl planks, and duct tape. A fire pit at the center is smoldering, its embers barely visible beneath a plastic sheet draped over it. The wind picks up, rattling the tarps like the ghosts of past travelers.

A lone figure moves through the space, their silhouette shifting between the flickering light and the dark. They’re organizing a pile of cardboard boxes, their contents half-sorted: a rusted spoon, a half-used notebook, a torn map, and a half-empty bottle of whiskey. The ground is littered with abandoned belongings—lost shoes, a crumpled newspaper, a child’s toy, a half-empty energy drink can. The place feels like it’s holding together just long enough to keep people from freezing, then it’ll collapse again, just like the rest of it.
