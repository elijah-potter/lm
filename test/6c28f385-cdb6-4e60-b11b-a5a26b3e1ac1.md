The library was quiet except for the hum of the fluorescent lights and the occasional rustle of pages. Emma sat on the floor near the back, her notebook open in front of her, the light from the single bulb casting long shadows across the floor. She had come here to escape the noise of her neighborhood, to forget the arguments with her parents, the endless chores, and the way her best friend, Jake, had started acting weird after their last fight.

She flipped through her notes again, the words blurred by the glow of her phone screen. *"Why do people always say they need to be alone? Like being alone means you're better off—when really, it just means you're not good at talking to people."*

A sharp knock at the door made her jump. She hadn’t heard anyone come in.

"Emma?" Jake’s voice was strained. "Can you come out?"

She stood up slowly, the weight of her backpack pressing against her shoulders. The library door creaked open, and Jake stepped inside, his usual confidence replaced by a tired look. He sat down across from her, his fingers drumming against his knee.

"You came back," he said, not a question, but an observation.

Emma shrugged. "I always come here. It’s safe."

He exhaled sharply. "I don’t know what’s wrong with me. I’ve been… off lately."

Emma wanted to ask why, but she didn’t know how to start. Instead, she just nodded.

"You’re not the only one," Jake said suddenly. "My mom said the same thing—about needing space. She’s like, she’s trying to fix it, but I don’t know how."

Emma’s throat tightened. She had felt like that sometimes too. Like there was a gap between her and her parents, a wall she couldn’t climb over.

"I don’t know what to do," Jake continued. "But maybe… maybe we should talk about it. Not just the arguments, but what’s really bothering us."

Emma looked at him, her eyes wide. "You want to talk about it?"

"Yeah. I think it’s time."

She nodded, her pulse racing. The weight of the conversation settled over them, heavy and new.

---

Later, Emma sat at her desk, the notebook open in front of her. She had written down Jake’s words, the way he sounded, the way he looked. She had never thought about it like that—about their problems, about how they were connected.

Her phone buzzed. It was a text from her mom: *"Where are you? Dinner’s getting cold."*

Emma typed back: *"I’m okay. Just working."*

She didn’t look up as she typed, but she knew her mom would know something was wrong.

---

The next day, Emma found herself at the kitchen table with her parents. The usual tension hung in the air, thick and heavy. Her mom’s face was pale, her hands clenched around a glass of water.

"You two," her mom said, her voice tight. "What’s going on?"

Emma and Jake exchanged a glance. Jake took a deep breath.

"We need help," he said.

Emma’s heart pounded. She had never asked for help before. But maybe that was the problem.

Her mom’s eyes widened. "What do you mean?"

"We need to talk about us," Jake said. "Not just the fights, but the things we’re not saying. Like why we keep pulling away."

Emma’s stomach twisted. She had never felt so exposed, so vulnerable.

Her mom’s face softened. "Okay. But we need to figure this out together."

Emma looked at Jake, then at her mom. The weight of it all felt too much.

---

Weeks passed. Emma and Jake sat in the library again, the same space that had felt like a safe haven before. This time, it was different. They had things to say, things to listen to.

"You’re not alone," Emma said, her voice steady. "Even when you feel like you are."

Jake smiled, but it was a tired thing. "I know."

Outside, the world kept spinning, indifferent to their struggles. But for the first time, Emma felt like she could face it—not alone, but with someone who understood.

And maybe, just maybe, that was enough.
