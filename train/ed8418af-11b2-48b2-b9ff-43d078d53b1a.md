In the quiet hum of a half-empty café, the narrator sipped their third cup of coffee, watching the steam curl toward the ceiling like a whispered secret. Outside, the rain tapped against the window in slow, deliberate beats, mirroring the rhythm of their thoughts.

*"I don’t know why I’m telling you this,"* they murmured, leaning forward slightly. *"But sometimes, the truth doesn’t need an audience. Sometimes, it just needs to be said."* The words hung between them, thin and fragile, like the last slice of bread left on a crumb-strewn plate.

The story wasn’t grand. It wasn’t dramatic. It was just that once, long ago, they had forgotten to lock the front door. The neighbor had come in, found a single half-eaten sandwich on the counter, and—without asking—taken it. *"You should’ve asked,"* they’d said. The narrator had laughed, not because it was funny, but because the lie they’d told themselves—that the sandwich was *theirs*—had been so easy to swallow. It had been the smallest betrayal of all.

Now, the rain was turning the pavement into a reflective pool, and the narrator’s fingers traced the rim of their cup. *"I still don’t know why I’m telling you this,"* they repeated, softer this time. *"But sometimes, the truth doesn’t need an audience. Sometimes, it just needs to be said."* The café lights flickered, casting long shadows across the table. The story wasn’t over. It was just beginning.
