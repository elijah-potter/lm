The morning mist lingered in the hollows where the old oak’s roots curled into the earth, its bark cracked with age like a memory’s skin. You sat at the kitchen table, the mug of tea still warm, its steam curling in slow spirals that mirrored the thoughts trapped inside. Outside, the birds had stopped singing—just a few scattered notes, as if warning you to listen harder. The phone buzzed once, the sound sharp against the quiet, a notification you ignored, a reminder you’d forget later.

Then came the words: *"Why?"* Not whispered, not shouted, but the quiet weight of a question that had been sitting there, unspoken, for days. Your hands trembled as you reached for the glass, the rim cold against your palms. The tea was bitter, sharp, and suddenly everything made sense—how the way the light hit the windowpane had always felt wrong, how the way your friend’s voice had sounded familiar, how you’d always known, in the back of your head, that this was it, that this was the moment.

But clarity is like a dream half-remembered when you wake up. The words blurred into static, the weight of them dissolving into the hum of the refrigerator. The mist had thinned, the birds were singing again, and the room felt empty, as if the moment had been a ghost you’d touched but not fully grasped. The mug was cold. The phone buzzed again. The moment of clarity had faded like a shadow cast by a passing sun.
