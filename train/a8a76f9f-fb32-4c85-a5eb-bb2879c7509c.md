A dim lamp flickered over the worn leather couch where Elias sat, his fingers tracing the rim of his empty whiskey glass. The fire crackled low in the hearth, casting long shadows across the walls. Outside, the city’s distant hum of traffic faded into the quiet of the night.

“You ever wonder how stories get told?” Elias said, voice rough. He leaned forward, the story spilling from him like smoke from a chimney. “Let me tell you one.”

---

The first story was about a woman named Elara. She lived in a village by the river, where the water whispered secrets to those who listened. One evening, as she knelt beside the bank, a stranger approached—tall, cloaked in black, with eyes like polished river stones. He held a single silver coin in his palm.

“You hear the river’s voice?” he asked. “It sings of a door beneath the reeds.” Elara’s breath hitched. “I’ll take you to it,” he said. “But first—one coin.”

She hesitated, then handed him the only thing she owned: her mother’s locket. The coin clinked in his palm.

That night, Elara dreamed of a door in the reeds. When she woke, the river was still, but the reeds had grown thin. She found the door there, carved with symbols that made her head ache. Inside, the air smelled of salt and old books. At the center stood a woman—her own reflection, but older, her hair silvered, her hands stained with ink.

“You asked for a door,” the woman said. “But doors are only doors if you walk through them.” She pressed a small, rusted key into Elara’s palm. “The river remembers what you forget. The past is a river, and it flows backward.”

The stranger found her the next morning, his face lined with exhaustion. “The door was real,” he admitted. “But the river took more than coin.” He pressed a worn journal into her hands. “Write it down,” he said. “Or it will be lost.”

Elara’s hands shook as she opened the journal. The pages were blank, but the ink on the first page was already flowing—her story, her fears, her name.

---

Elias exhaled, watching the firelight dance in her eyes. “That’s the kind of story that sticks,” he murmured. “Not the ones that end with a happy clap, but the ones that make you question what you thought you knew.”
