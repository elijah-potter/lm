The 'red-eye effect' is a common phenomenon seen in flash photography, where the subject's eyes appear red in the photograph. This effect is the result of light from the flash entering the subject's eye and reflecting off the blood-rich retina, the layer at the back of the eye responsible for sensing light. When the flash is used in low light conditions, the subject's pupils are typically dilated to let in more light for better vision. This also allows more of the flash's light to enter the eye and reflect off the retina. The reflected light, appearing red due to the blood vessels in the retina, then travels back to the camera and registers on the photo. Several techniques can help minimize the red-eye effect. Many cameras come with a 'red-eye reduction' feature, which works by emitting pre-flashes or continuous light before the actual flash. This causes the subject's pupils to contract, reducing the amount of light that can enter the eye and reflect off the retina. Another method is to increase the ambient lighting, causing the subject's pupils to contract naturally. Changing the angle between the camera, subject, and flash source can also help reduce the red-eye effect, as less light will reflect directly back into the camera lens.
