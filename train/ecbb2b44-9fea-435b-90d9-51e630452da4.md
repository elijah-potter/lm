## TIL the "Smell of Rain" is Actually a Combination of Several Chemical Reactions

Today I learned that the pleasant, earthy scent many associate with rain – often called “petrichor” – isn't a single compound, but a complex blend of several chemical reactions and compounds. It’s far more intricate than I previously imagined.

The term "petrichor" itself was coined in 1964 by two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and R.G. Thomas, who were studying the phenomenon. They derived it from the Greek words *petra* (stone) and *ichor* (the ethereal fluid that flows in the veins of the gods in Greek mythology).

The scent arises primarily from two sources. Firstly, during dry periods, plants release volatile organic compounds (VOCs), particularly isoprenoids, which accumulate on rocks and soil. These compounds are released into the air when raindrops impact the ground. Secondly, a compound called geosmin, produced by bacteria (specifically *Streptomyces*) in the soil, is normally water-bound. The impact of raindrops releases this geosmin into the air, contributing significantly to the characteristic aroma. Geosmin is incredibly potent; humans can detect it at concentrations as low as 5 parts per trillion.

What I find particularly fascinating is the interplay of these processes and the subtle variations in the scent depending on the type of vegetation, soil composition, and rainfall intensity. It’s a beautiful example of how chemistry and biology combine to create a sensory experience we often take for granted.
