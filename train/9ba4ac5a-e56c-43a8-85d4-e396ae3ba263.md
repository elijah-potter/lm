A flickering neon sign of the **1987 Detroit Free Press** casts a warm glow over the empty diner parking lot. The scent of coffee and old leather clings to the air. A young man—early twenties, scuffed-up jeans, a jacket patched at the elbows—stands in the doorway, his hands shoved in his pockets, staring at his phone. The screen glows with a message from his old roommate, *"Dude, we’re gonna be late. Meet me at the corner of 8th and Park—don’t be late."*

He exhales sharply. The diner’s jukebox, stuck on a 1984 hit, blares *"I Want to Be Where You Are"* in the background. He pulls out his wallet—**1999**, the kind with a leather flap—but the date stamp inside reads **1988**. His wallet is from his *other* life.

A shadow moves in the doorway. A woman in a 1970s-style miniskirt and a man in a 1950s suit with a cane. They exchange glances—**alive, but not quite**. The woman’s voice is a whisper: *"You remember me, don’t you?"* He shakes his head. The woman smiles, but it’s not the same smile. Then the man’s cane clicks against the pavement. The jukebox cuts to static.

The diner’s bell rings. A new customer walks in—**1920s**, a woman in a flapper dress, clutching a flask. She slams her glass of whiskey down. The neon sign flickers, then goes out. In the darkness, a single light bulb buzzes. The diner’s walls are lined with **photos of strangers**—faces from the past, all staring back.

The young man’s phone buzzes. A text from his *future* self: *"You’re gonna need this."* The screen flashes **1999** again. He pockets it. The woman from 1970s looks at him, then at the man in the suit. The man raises his cane—**slow, deliberate**—and steps toward him. The diner’s jukebox plays *"Don’t Stop Believin’"* in the background. The air hums with something older than time.
