Charter Vs. Public
Charter and public schools are both funded with tax-payer dollars from the states in which they are located. The differences are in the student body and the way the schools are designed and run. Neighborhood schools are the appropriate choice for some students, while charter schools offer options for others.
While charter schools are technically public schools, their student body often comes from homes with a higher socio-economic standing than their public school counterparts. According to a March 2010 report in "The Washington Post," fewer New York City charter school students require free or reduced lunch, are homeless or live below the poverty line. Charter schools are also free to require parent participation and are not required to provide transportation, which reduce the ability of families with lack of funds to send their children to these schools.
Charter classrooms usually have smaller classroom sizes than other public schools. Unlike neighborhood schools, charter schools are able to limit their student body. It's common for a charter school to hold a lottery for spots that open up once returning students and younger students in those families are slotted in. The lower student-to-teacher ratio often leads to higher teacher retention and more one-on-one attention for the students.
Charter schools offer parents a choice for their child's education. These are public schools that have no tuition. While there are costs involved, such as transportation, which often is not provided, they are considerably less than the costs of private schools for the most part. Charter schools are run by boards that are free of many of the rules and regulations that apply to the traditional public school system. As a result, charter schools can build curriculum and programs designed for their target student body. Charter schools often have a focus on a specific part of education or for those with special needs.
Charter schools have more freedom than traditional public schools to investigate and employ innovative educational ideas. Project-based learning or child-based learning, Montessori ideas or core-curriculum learning are options available to charter schools but don't translate as well to larger schools regulated by the school district. Charter schools generally are required to participate in high-stakes testing for funding purposes, but with smaller student bodies and more involved parents, they are able to build programs that use ideas not available in other public schools.
