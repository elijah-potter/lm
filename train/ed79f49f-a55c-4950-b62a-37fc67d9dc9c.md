The rain came down in sheets, turning the alleyway into a mirror of muted reflections. I wiped my hands on my coat, the fabric damp with the same mist that clung to my hair. The truth I’d kept buried for years was heavier than any rain.

I didn’t steal the car keys. I took them from the girl’s pocket because I knew she’d forget them by the time she got home, and I’d been watching her for weeks. She never noticed.

The confessional wasn’t in a church. It was in the empty apartment above the diner, the one with the flickering neon sign that read *Open Late*. I sat cross-legged on the floor, the keys in my palm, and told her everything. Not about the car, but about the way her fingers trembled when she thought no one was looking, how she’d always been afraid of the dark, how she’d once written in a journal that she wished she could disappear.

She didn’t believe me at first. But when she saw the empty space where her notebook used to be, when she saw the half-empty glass of whiskey on the counter, she started to understand. The truth doesn’t always need words—sometimes it just needs to be heard.

Outside, the diner’s door creaked open. The last customer of the night left, and the bell above the door swung silently shut. The keys were still in my hand.
