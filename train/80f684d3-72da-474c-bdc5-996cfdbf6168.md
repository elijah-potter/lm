A slender brass key glints in the dim light of an old bookstore. It’s passed between fingers, each holding it for a split second—one clutching it tightly, the other hesitant, the weight shifting like a secret. The first hand is a woman in a frayed leather jacket, her grip firm but not possessive. The second hand belongs to a man in a worn scarf, his fingers tracing the key’s edge before exchanging it with the third. The third hand is younger, eager, the key barely held for a moment before passing it along to the fourth—a child’s small, uncertain grasp. The chain continues: a poet in a trench coat, then an elderly man with a cane, his thumb pressing the key’s loop before yielding it to the next. The cycle repeats in a loop of touch and trust, each hand the next link in an invisible thread, the key a symbol of something shared, something held only briefly, then passed forward.
