The first time I remembered the weight of doubt, it was in the kitchen at midnight. The coffee cup—black, too black—sat between my fingers, its surface too smooth to hold a thought. The recipe book lay open beside it, its pages yellowed with half-finished dreams. I had meant to write something about the way light bends through a glass of water, about the way time dissolves like sugar in tea, but instead, I only wrote:

*"Maybe the recipe is wrong."*

I set the pen down.

The next day, I found myself at the market, staring at a basket of strawberries. Their bright red skins pulsed against the wooden crate, and for a moment, I thought I could hear them calling. I bought them anyway. That evening, as I peeled one, the skin split like paper, and I realized: I had never been sure what to do with them. So I made jam.

The week after that, I drove to the mountains. The air was crisp, and the trees stood like silent sentinels. I climbed to the peak, where the sky stretched endlessly, and for the first time, I let myself believe I could see the world from there. Then I realized I didn’t want to see anything at all. I sat there, hands clasped in my lap, and whispered:

*"What if I don’t know what I’m doing?"*

The wind picked up, and the answer came in a gust, sharp and cold. I returned to the village, where I found the recipe book again, its pages now crumpled and worn. I opened it to the first page and wrote:

*"Maybe the story is already written."*
