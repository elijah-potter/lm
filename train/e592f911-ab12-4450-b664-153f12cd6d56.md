## TIL the "Phantom Islands" of Cartography Were Once Considered Real

Today I learned about a fascinating and now-discredited chapter in cartography: the prevalence of “phantom islands” on historical maps. For centuries, islands like Hy-Brasil, Buss, and Frisland were depicted as legitimate landmasses in the North Atlantic, often appearing on maps produced by reputable explorers and cartographers.

The origins of these phantom islands are varied. Some arose from misinterpretations of atmospheric phenomena – perhaps mist or icebergs mistaken for land from a distance. Others stemmed from sailors' tall tales, embellished over time and incorporated into maps as accepted fact. Still others were deliberate fabrications, introduced to fill in blank spaces on maps or to mislead competitors. 

Hy-Brasil, an island purportedly located west of Ireland, was particularly persistent, appearing on maps as late as the 19th century and often described as shrouded in mist, only appearing to view occasionally. Attempts to locate it were made, but all proved fruitless. 

The gradual decline of phantom islands on maps coincided with improved navigational techniques, more accurate surveying, and a greater understanding of oceanographic conditions. While they no longer appear on modern maps, their presence in historical cartography offers a compelling glimpse into the limitations of early exploration and the power of storytelling to shape our understanding of the world.
