**"The Weight of What We Left Behind"**

The first time she noticed the stain, it was on the kitchen table—a dark, unnatural patch where the coffee had gone wrong. She wiped at it with a cloth, but the ink refused to fade, seeping deeper than any stain should. That was the first warning.

We didn’t mean for this to happen.

The police report filled out in shaky handwriting read: *Three missing children, all under ten, last seen together at the old mill’s abandoned waterwheel. No signs of struggle, no forced entry—just a single, child-sized bootprint near the creek where they were found days later, half-buried in the mud.*

The coroner’s report spoke in numbers: *Pulse irregular, bruises across the back of the hands, a single, shallow wound near the collarbone.* The pathologist’s notes were cryptic: *Possible trauma to the neck, but no evidence of strangulation. The children were already dead by the time they were found.*

The detective’s final line in the report was the only thing they couldn’t explain: *The waterwheel’s gears had turned—once, twice, three times—before the children vanished.*

We didn’t mean for this to happen.

The mill had stood empty for twenty years, its rusted beams groaning in the wind. The townsfolk whispered that the wheel kept turning on its own, that the children’s voices echoed from the hollows when the wind blew just right. Some said it was the ghosts of the millworkers, others that the wheel was alive. The truth was simpler: the wheel had always been a mechanism, and mechanisms, like teeth, never stop until they break.

The last time she visited, she found the bootprint again—fresh, just as the report said. This time, she followed it deeper into the mill, where the waterwheel’s rusted teeth still turned in slow, deliberate circles. The floorboards beneath her feet groaned, as if the wheel had found a new way to move.

The mill’s clock tower still ticked, though the hands had stopped at 3:17. That was when they all vanished.

We didn’t mean for this to happen. But the wheel never stops.
