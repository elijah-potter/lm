At the conference table, a single chair is pushed back, legs crossed, fingers tapping impatiently. The person—mid-40s, sharp cheekbones, eyes darting between the screen and the person across from them—has just received a terse email demanding an explanation for their recent behavior.

*"Dr. Elias Carter,"* the email read, *"your attendance at the closed-door meeting on December 12th was not authorized. We’d like you to provide a detailed account of your activities during that time."*

The recipient exhales through their nose, nails digging into the table. They adjust their tie, fingers hovering over the keyboard, unsure where to begin.

*"I was—"*

The other person’s phone buzzes—a call from a client. They glance at it, then back at Elias. *"We don’t have all day. Start with the time you left the office. Where were you?"*

Elias hesitates. The screen flickers. Outside, a car horn blares. The weight of the unspoken presses in. *"I—I was at the clinic. The—uh—*the* patient’s family needed me."* The lie slips out. The lie that was supposed to be a half-truth. *"They had—uh—*a* family emergency. They needed me there."*

The other person’s eyebrows rise. *"A family emergency? What kind of emergency?"*

*"They—uh—*their son* was—*seizures*,"* Elias stutters. *"He’s been—*epileptic*—for years. They were—*urgent*."* The words taste like ash. The lie, like the truth, is a thin thread, barely holding together.

The other person leans in, a smirk playing at the corner of their mouth. *"And why didn’t you say so before?"*

Elias’s fingers tremble. *"Because I didn’t think it was—*relevant*."* The words feel like a betrayal. The lie feels like a wound.

The other person exhales. *"Okay. So you were there. Why didn’t you call in?"*

*"I—I was—*on call*,"* Elias stutters. *"I—uh—*they didn’t have a doctor*,"* he corrects himself. *"They didn’t have anyone."*

The other person’s phone buzzes again. They answer it, then hang up. *"We’re out of time. You’re suspended until further notice."* The screen glows red. *"Report to HR by end of day."*

Elias’s breath catches. The chair creaks as they push it back. The lie is a weight around their neck, heavy and unyielding. They look at the clock. The meeting is over. The lie is over. The truth, if there was one, has been buried under a mountain of paperwork and corporate paranoia.

As they leave, the city hums around them—cars, sirens, the distant laughter of people who don’t understand the weight of a lie this big. Elias’s phone buzzes again. It’s a text from the clinic. *"Patient’s condition deteriorating. Need you back ASAP."*

They don’t reply. They don’t have to. The lie is already there. The truth is just another number on a report.
