The gradual erosion of a stone bridge, layer by layer, begins with subtle microfractures forming in the mortar between bricks. Visible cracks appear only after weeks of consistent exposure to rain, where water seeps in, freezes, and expands, widening the fissures incrementally. Over time, these splits propagate deeper, causing the uppermost planks to sag slightly under the weight of accumulated debris and the gradual loss of structural integrity.

Eventually, the bridge’s foundation tilts, revealing the unseen shifts in the ground beneath—a slow, creeping subsidence. The once-sturdy arches now lean, their once-aligned curves now askew, like a child’s drawing that slowly unravels. Maintenance crews, initially called in to address a single crumbling lintel, find themselves documenting a cascade of failures, each step a new fracture in the process of collapse.

The final, catastrophic shift comes not from one grand event but from the sum of thousands of small, invisible forces: a sudden storm that washes away a final support, or a freeze-thaw cycle that fractures the last remaining reinforcement. The bridge stands in ruins not as a single dramatic event, but as the inevitable conclusion of a thousand incremental failures.
