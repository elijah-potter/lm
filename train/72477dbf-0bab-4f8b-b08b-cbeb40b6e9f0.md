A long, slow procession stretched across the street, moving at a pace that made it feel like a deliberate, suffocating crawl. The air hummed with distant chatter—laughter, shouts, the clink of glasses, the occasional scream—and the scent of sweating bodies, sunscreen, and something faintly metallic lingered. The crowd was a sea of faceless faces, all moving in the same direction, all oblivious to the space between them.

At first, it was just the crowd: a dense, unbroken wall of people, their shoulders brushing, their voices overlapping, their eyes darting in all directions like lost insects. Then the floats arrived, their grandiosity a cruel joke. One was a towering, glittering skyscraper, its windows reflecting the neon glow of the city. Another was a giant, swaying palm tree, its fronds shimmering with LED lights. A third was a grotesque, grinning face, its teeth too sharp, its smile too wide, the kind that made you want to look away but couldn’t stop yourself.

The music was relentless—a blaring, distorted waltz that sounded like it was being played through a speaker submerged in a bathtub. The bass rumbled in your chest, the melody a jagged needle digging into your eardrums. People danced, their arms flailing, their bodies swaying in time, but you couldn’t join. You could only watch, your pulse hammering in your throat, your fingers digging into the sides of your chair as the crowd pressed closer.

Then the floats started moving again, and the crowd surged forward, pushing you back, pushing you *away*. You could see the edges of the parade—people at the back, their faces blurred by distance, their arms waving weakly, their laughter swallowed by the noise. But the front? The front was a wall of people, all of them moving forward, all of them *there*, none of them *gone*.

The best part was the people at the back—the ones who knew they were being left behind, the ones who were already turning away, their shoulders slumping, their faces resigned. They were the ones who knew what was coming next: the crowds would grow denser, the noise would rise, the pressure would push you toward them, toward the thing you hated most in the world.

And then, just when you thought you couldn’t take it anymore, the parade ended. The music faded. The floats slowly sank into the crowd, their grandiosity dissolving into the sea of people. The crowd dissolved too, dispersing in all directions, their laughter and chatter thinning into the night air. But for you, the memory of the parade was still there, a living thing, a memory of being trapped in something that wasn’t yours, a memory of being part of something you didn’t belong to.
