In a crumbling city where time ran backward, the grand clock of the Central Observatory stood as a silent sentinel. Its gears, rusted with centuries, never moved, yet its hands traced a perpetual loop of 11:59. Each morning, the city’s residents would whisper that the clock had stopped—until noon, when it would tick away again, though never quite right. Children carved their names into its face, leaving messages like *12:00 tomorrow* or *11:59 forever*.

One evening, a thief named Elias stole a pocket watch from the clockmaker’s workshop. That night, he pressed it to his ear, and for the first time in years, the sound was clear: *the tick was too fast*. By dawn, Elias had vanished, replaced by a boy who had never existed—his name inscribed on the clock’s face as *Elias*.

Years passed. The boy, now Elias, learned the truth: the city’s clock was a prison, and time itself was a thief. The hands didn’t move; they *stole*. Every second, a soul was erased—one name erased, another born. Elias spent his days rewriting the clock’s face, erasing his own name, replacing it with the names of those who had already been lost. The city’s people fell into despair, until Elias realized the only way to stop it was to break the cycle.

At midnight, Elias placed the stolen watch on the clock’s face, its hands spinning wildly. The city erupted in light. The clock’s gears screamed as they unraveled, and the hands dissolved into dust. The city’s people gasped, seeing the faces of the lost—Elias’s mother, his sister, the clockmaker—all frozen in time, their names still written on the clock’s face. But now, the hands didn’t move. They stopped. The city held its breath.

When dawn came, the clock stood silent. No hands. No names. Just a smooth, empty face. Elias walked away, but the city didn’t forget. The next morning, a new boy was found near the clock, his name written in the sand: *Elias*. The cycle continued.
