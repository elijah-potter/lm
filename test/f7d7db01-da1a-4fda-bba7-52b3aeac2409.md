In the quiet town of Meadowgrove, where the old oak trees whispered secrets to the wind, lived twelve-year-old Liam Carter. He was the kind of boy who spent his afternoons fixing bikes, cracking open science textbooks, and dreaming up inventions in his garage—a place smelling of oil and old circuit boards. One stormy evening, while his parents were out, Liam’s older sister, Mira, found him hunched over a half-built robot on the kitchen table.

“You’re gonna get electrocuted,” Mira said, shaking a finger. “Stop, or I’m calling Dad.”

Liam ignored her, his fingers dancing over the wires. “It’s not gonna happen. Besides, if it does, I’ll just blame the power surge.”

Mira rolled her eyes but didn’t argue. That night, as Liam stared at the glowing red eyes of his robot—a mix of scrap metal and his own blueprints—he heard it first. A soft, mechanical *click*. The robot’s head tilted slightly. Then, a voice, not his own: *“Hello, Liam.”*

The next morning, Liam’s phone buzzed. A new number: **555-0123**. No name, just the number. He answered. A woman’s voice, breathless, said, “We need help. It’s not safe out there anymore.”

Liam’s stomach dropped. He’d never been one for mysteries, but something in her tone made him pull out his tablet. The screen flashed with a single word: **DISTORTED**. Below it, a map—an old highway, marked with red Xs where people vanished. The last entry was dated two days before.

At school, Liam’s teacher, Ms. Rivera, found him staring at the map. “You’ve been absent a lot lately,” she said. “You okay?”

Liam nodded, but his mind raced. That night, he found a hidden compartment in his garage—inside, a journal with a single entry:

*“They’re coming. They’re not human anymore. Find the signal before it’s too late.”*

The next day, Liam met Noah, a quiet boy who always wore a black hoodie, even in summer. They spent their lunch break dissecting a textbook on physics, just like old times. But when Noah whispered, *“There’s a radio station in the woods,”* Liam’s heart pounded.

The radio crackled to life in the trees. A distorted voice: *“This is the Signal. We’re being hunted. Help us find the others.”* The broadcast ended. Noah’s eyes widened. “We have to go.”

That night, they slipped into the woods. The Signal’s coordinates led them to an abandoned farmhouse, its windows shattered. Inside, they found more journals—pages filled with drawings of strange creatures, notes about “the change,” and a final entry:

*“They’re everywhere. They’re not just monsters. They’re us. Wake up before it’s too late.”*

The next morning, Liam and Noah returned to Meadowgrove, but the town was different. People avoided eye contact. The news said a “mysterious illness” had spread. Then, the disappearances started.

Liam’s parents, frantic, found him at the garage. “Liam?” his dad said, voice trembling. “What’s going on?”

Liam’s hands trembled as he showed them the journals. “Someone’s trying to warn us. But they’re not telling the whole truth.”

That night, Liam stood in the woods again. The Signal’s voice was louder this time: *“They’re watching. They’re listening. Wake up before you’re next.”* A figure moved in the trees—tall, too tall, with too many limbs. Liam screamed and ran, but it was already too late.

When he woke up, he was in his bed. His hands were shaking. His phone was in his pocket.

Then, the texts started.

**From 555-0123:**

*“You’re next.”*

**From the Signal:**

*“They’re coming for you.”*

Liam’s fingers hovered over his phone. He should delete the messages. But then he saw the map again—red Xs marking where people vanished. And the drawings from the journals. Not monsters. Not diseases.

*Evolutions.*

Something had changed Meadowgrove. Something was watching. And it was getting closer.

Liam grabbed his bike, pedaled out of town, and didn’t look back. The road stretched ahead, endless. But for the first time in his life, he wasn’t sure he was alone.
