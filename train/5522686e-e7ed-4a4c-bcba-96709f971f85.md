The crowd’s murmur fades into silence when the old woman steps into the alley—her cane tapping a steady rhythm against the concrete. The others notice only the shadows stretching long behind her, or the way her coat billows slightly with each breath. She notices the way the streetlight’s glow bleeds through the cracks in the pavement, casting an eerie blue-green stain that no one else seems to linger over.

She picks up a loose brick from the curb and rolls it between her fingers, watching how it shifts color under the flickering light. The others move past, their eyes fixed on the neon sign advertising cheap liquor, the hum of distant traffic. She watches them—the way their shoulders tense when a car horn blares, the flicker of guilt in their eyes when they glance at the homeless man asleep in the doorway. They see the city’s chaos, the broken things, the people who don’t belong. She sees the city’s quiet, the way the air tastes slightly of ozone and old money, the way the pavement remembers every step taken before hers.

She doesn’t flinch when the rain starts—not really. The others will call it a sign, a curse, or just another day. She knows the rain is the same today as it was yesterday, just as the city is the same, just as *they* are. But she notices the way the water beading on her umbrella reflects the faintest shimmer of something she can’t name—the ghost of a streetlight, the curve of a forgotten sign, the way the rain dances like fireflies against the windows of the buildings, and no one else sees.

She exhales, slow and deliberate, and the others—who are already moving again—don’t pause. They don’t ask. They never do.
