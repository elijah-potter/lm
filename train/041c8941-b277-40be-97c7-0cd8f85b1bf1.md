The daily ritual began at dawn, the air thick with the scent of damp earth and the faint metallic tang of the forge. The anvil stood sentinel, its surface worn smooth by countless strikes of the hammer. The blacksmith, his hands calloused and scarred, chanted under his breath as he heated the blade in the coals—a silent incantation to bind the forge’s power.

The task was not merely shaping metal but a communion with the elements. The first strike was deliberate, a prayer to the gods of iron. Then came the rhythmic cadence: hammer on anvil, then pause, then the next strike. Each hammer blow carried meaning—some for balance, others for precision. The sparks danced in patterns that the old ones said foretold the future of the blade.

At noon, the ritual paused for a meal shared with the village, the bread warm, the wine spiced with herbs from the garden. The anvil was left unattended, its silence broken only by the distant hum of the wind through the trees.

By dusk, the blade was finished. It glowed faintly in the fire, its edge sharp enough to cut through the dark. The anvil bore no scars, no marks of labor—only the faint, unbroken surface of a thousand rituals past. The blacksmith wiped his brow, his voice rough with the weight of the day’s work.

*"Another cycle completed,"* he murmured, as if to the metal itself. The blade was ready to serve, to carve stories into flesh and wood, to become a weapon or a tool, but always a living thing—bound by the hands and heart of its maker.
