Once upon a time, in a town where the river marked the boundary between the old and new districts, there was a rule carved into the oak door of the village elder’s home:

**"No lanterns after midnight."**

It was enforced once—during a storm that turned the bridge into a frozen wall—when the lanterns of travelers had nearly lured a pack of wolves through the dark. The villagers, trembling, remembered the rule and kept it for three nights.

Then, the storm passed, the bridge thawed, and the lanterns returned. The elder, now older and quieter, no longer spoke of the rule. The townsfolk, lulled by the soft glow of their own fires, forgot it entirely.

Now, children laugh as they carry candles down the street at dusk, and the wolves, now accustomed to the light, no longer hesitate. The rule is dust in the attic, a memory buried beneath the hum of the wind.
