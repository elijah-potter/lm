## A Counseling Session

**Setting:** A comfortably furnished therapy room. Soft lighting, a view of a small, well-tended garden.

**Characters:**

* **Dr. Eleanor Vance:** A seasoned therapist, known for her calm demeanor and insightful approach.
* **Liam Harding:** A young professional, experiencing significant stress related to a demanding career.

**(The session begins. Liam is seated on a comfortable armchair, facing Dr. Vance, who is seated opposite him.)**

**Liam:** It’s… it’s been a lot, lately. I feel like I’m constantly chasing something, and I never quite catch it.

**Dr. Vance:** (Nods gently, maintaining eye contact) That sounds incredibly draining, Liam. It takes a considerable toll to feel perpetually behind. Can you elaborate on what that “chasing” feels like specifically?

**Liam:** It’s my job, mostly. The expectations are… high. I want to do well, I *need* to do well, but the workload just keeps piling up. There’s always another deadline, another email, another meeting. I’m working long hours, and I still feel like I’m falling short.

**Dr. Vance:** So, a sense of obligation and a fear of not meeting those expectations are contributing to this feeling of being overwhelmed. It sounds like there's significant pressure, both internal and potentially external. You mentioned feeling like you *need* to do well. Where does that need originate?

**Liam:** (Hesitates) I think… I think it’s a combination of things. My parents always emphasized achievement. And I want to prove to myself that I'm capable. That I’m… worthy.

**Dr. Vance:** That’s a powerful statement, Liam. It reveals a deep desire for validation, both from external sources and from within. It’s understandable that you’re striving for that sense of worthiness. However, it sounds as though the bar is set incredibly high. Do you feel you’re judging your own performance harshly?

**Liam:** (Sighs) Probably. I know logically that nobody is perfect. But when I’m in the thick of it, that logic seems to disappear. I just focus on what I *haven’t* done, rather than what I *have* accomplished.

**Dr. Vance:** That’s a common pattern, Liam. Our minds often default to negativity, especially when we're under stress. It’s a survival mechanism, in a way – constantly scanning for potential threats. But when that mechanism is constantly triggered, it becomes detrimental. Let's explore strategies for shifting that focus. Perhaps we can begin by identifying some of your recent accomplishments, however small they may seem.

**Liam:** (Looks down, considering) Well, I did manage to secure that new contract last week. It was a lot of work, but it was a significant win for the company.

**Dr. Vance:** That *is* a significant win, Liam. And you were instrumental in achieving it. Let's acknowledge that accomplishment. How did it feel when you secured that contract? Did you allow yourself to feel any sense of pride?

**Liam:** I… I was relieved, mostly. I didn’t really have time to feel proud. There was already something else demanding my attention.

**Dr. Vance:** (Nods understandingly) That's a crucial observation, Liam. You’re so accustomed to immediately moving on to the next task that you’re denying yourself the opportunity to savor your successes. That denial contributes to the feeling of being perpetually behind.  We can work on integrating moments of recognition and self-compassion into your routine. It's not about diminishing your drive, but about sustaining it in a healthier way.  For next week, I'd like you to consciously acknowledge one achievement each day, no matter how small. Just pause, recognize it, and allow yourself to feel a moment of satisfaction. How does that feel to consider?

**Liam:** (Looks up, a flicker of hope in his eyes) It sounds… manageable. A little daunting, but manageable.

**Dr. Vance:** (Smiles warmly) It’s a small step, Liam, but consistent steps are what lead to lasting change. And I’m here to support you every step of the way.



**(The session concludes.)**
