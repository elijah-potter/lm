Spotting Between Periods: A Cause for Concern?
A small amount of bleeding before your menstrual period is usually nothing to worry about, but continual spotting could signal a health problem.
Vaginal bleeding between menstrual periods — what most of us call spotting — is a common problem. “Almost every woman will experience it at some point in her life,” says Amy Autry, MD, clinical professor of obstetrics-gynecology and reproductive sciences at the University of California at San Francisco.
In most cases, spotting before your period is no cause for concern. However, in some situations, it could signal a reproductive health problem or even a life-threatening disease.
What Is Blood Spotting?
A typical menstrual period occurs every 28 days and lasts about four to seven days. Still, it’s not considered unusual for a woman’s entire menstrual cycle to be seven days shorter or longer than 28 days, and for the menstrual period to last up to eight days, says Dr. Autry.
Any vaginal bleeding that occurs after a menstrual period ends and before the next period starts is considered spotting. The bleeding may be so light that you only notice a faint pinkness on toilet paper or your underwear, but it also can appear as a drop or two of blood or even a heavier flow that may resemble your period.
Distinguishing Types of Discharge Between Periods
Natural vaginal discharge between periods changes throughout your cycle. On days when you do not have the vaginal bleeding of menstruation, you may see a clear or whitish discharge. This is normal and helps keep your tissues moist. Other changes in vaginal discharge, such as being dark yellow in color or smelling unpleasant, may be related to a vaginal irritation or infection. When this type of discharge is blood tinged, whether light or heavy, it is also sometimes referred to as spotting, but in this case might be due to infection or vaginal irritation instead of menstrual bleeding.
True “spot” bleeding between periods can vary widely in degree. Vaginal bleeding between periods can also be called breakthrough bleeding, and may be light or heavy bleeding, enough to quickly saturate a pad with the flow. Menopause bleeding between periods can be very erratic and unpredictable.
Spotting and Your Menstrual Cycle
If you experience spot bleeding before your period or in the middle of your cycle, there’s no reason to be overly worried. “A little bit of bleeding between menstrual periods or occasional spotting is fairly normal,” says Autry. Do make sure the bleeding is coming from the vagina and not from the rectum or from your urine — use a tampon to find out.
About 10 percent of menstruating women regularly spot at the time of ovulation due to a temporary drop in estrogen at the time the ovary releases an egg.
“Often, women will get vaginal discharge tinged with a little blood,” says Autry. You may also feel a slight pain or cramp. The clue to this harmless kind of spotting is that it happens every month about 10 to 14 days after your menstrual period ends — or as early as two weeks before your period. A little spotting may also be a sign of getting your period (you know your body best, so you know whether this is true).
If you’re wondering whether there is any meaning to the fact that you are spotting five days before your period versus two days before your period or even two days after your period ends, the answer is probably not.
“Stress, both physical and mental, and even happenstance can cause the brain to not initiate the cycle,” says ob-gyn Stephen Weiss, MD, assistant professor in the department of gynecology and obstetrics at Emory University School of Medicine in Atlanta, Ga. “This may result in spotting in the first half of the cycle. If the ovulation is incomplete or skipped, then not enough progesterone is made and spotting may occur in the second half of the cycle.”
That spotting could occur, therefore, four days before your period, three days before your period, or a week after your period, and all mean basically the same thing: You had an unusual cycle.
“The inside lining of the uterus also needs to be normal,” Dr. Weiss says. “Extra tissue or irregularity can make the lining fragile and cause spotting at any time. The most common causes are benign polyps and fibroids at or near the lining.” However, he emphasizes, “bleeding seven days before versus three days before a period doesn’t point to a specific cause.”
Keep in mind that just as spotting can be a sign of ovulation or getting your period, it can also indicate significant life changes, from pregnancy to menopause — it can be the first warning sign of pregnancy and occur during the first stages of pregnancy and later in life it can signal perimenopause, the gradual transition to full menopause, when there should be no longer be any vaginal bleeding at all.
Spotting during pregnancy. Most women experience some spotting during the first stages of pregnancy. In fact, a little pregnancy blood spotting is one of the signs of conception. Spotting is normal, especially if it happens early or late in the pregnancy. During the first 7 to 10 days of pregnancy, it’s common to experience some spotting as the fetus attaches itself to the uterine wall.
“But most women don’t even know that they’re pregnant at that point,” says Autry. You may also notice spotting in pregnancy two to three weeks before your due date. This just means that the mucus plug has loosened or discharged in preparation for labor. (This type of spotting in pregnancy is often vaginal discharge tinged with blood.)
On the other hand, if you experience heavy bleeding with cramping earlier in your pregnancy, call your doctor immediately. It could be a sign of a miscarriage or an ectopic pregnancy.
Spotting and perimenopause. It’s possible to experience excessive vaginal bleeding or spotting between periods during perimenopause as your body transitions to not having periods. If you’re in menopause, bleeding between periods shouldn’t be an issue because you won’t have periods any longer. Any menopausal vaginal bleeding requires attention. Hormone replacement therapy is a common cause of unusual vaginal bleeding during this time of life, but vaginal blood spotting could be a sign of cancer or other anatomical changes, so it needs medical attention as well.
Color of Spotting Between Periods
Blood spotting can vary widely in color. It can be dark red, pink, dark brown, or brownish.
“Bright red is usually fresh blood; pink is diluted; brown is days-old blood; and black is very old blood,” explains Weiss. “Spotting may be any of these colors and the rapidity of bleeding and how long the blood takes to get past the cervix mucous will influence color, but not help with the diagnosis of an underlying cause.”
Pay attention to the color, in case you need to report these details to your doctor. However, color alone cannot tell your doctor what (if any) health concerns you are facing.
Heaviness of Vaginal Bleeding
What may be even more important is how heavy any vaginal bleeding is. Light spotting may not warrant an immediate phone call to your ob-gyn, but if heavy vaginal bleeding between periods is soaking through one or more menstrual pads per hour for several hours, you must place a call to your doctor’s office.
You should see a doctor for:
- Excessive vaginal bleeding or vaginal bleeding between menstrual cycles that lasts more than three days — certainly don’t endure spotting for three weeks!
- Light spotting that continues for three menstrual cycles
- Any spotting or unusual vaginal bleeding compared to your typical pattern
- Vaginal bleeding that occurs more frequently than every three weeks
- Heavy bleeding after sex
- Any vaginal bleeding after you’ve completed menopause
Abnormal bleeding between periods, also known as abnormal uterine bleeding, can occur with a variety of health concerns and is a common reason that women call their doctors. Although bleeding between periods may have a simple explanation, like ovulation or even the use of certain types of birth control, you and your doctor may need to consider these other reasons for spotting:
Stopping and starting oral contraceptives
- IUD use for contraception
- Certain medications, such as corticosteroids or antidepressants
- Slight hormonal imbalances
- Uterine polyps
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Thyroid disorders
- Endometriosis or other changes in the endometrium
- Bladder or vaginal infections
- Ectopic (tubal) pregnancy
- Cancer of the endometrium, cervix, or uterus
- Use of blood thinners
- Irritation of vagina due to dryness
- Gynecological exam procedures, such as pap smears
A doctor’s exam is the best way to determine what’s going on, so don’t put off getting an exam because you’re bleeding. Even heavy breakthrough bleeding between periods shouldn’t keep you from a doctor’s visit. “Your doctor can do an exam while you’re spotting or even bleeding heavily,” says Autry. “It’s important to find out what’s going on as soon as possible because spotting can be a sign of cancer, although this is rare.”
What to Do About Spotting Between Periods
Even if your vaginal blood spotting doesn’t signal a health problem, there’s no reason to put up with the inconvenience of spotting before your period. Spotting during one cycle isn’t really a cause for alarm, but “if the spotting becomes the ‘new you,’ I recommend you see your medical provider,” says Weiss.
With your doctor’s help, you can prevent vaginal bleeding between your menstrual periods or reduce the risk of spotting by:
Taking birth control pills as prescribed. Skipping pills is a common cause of spotting. Stopping and restarting the pill may also cause bleeding between your menstrual periods. Try to take your pill at around the same time every day to reduce the chance of spotting, recommends Autry.
Limiting your intake of aspirin. Because aspirin can cause bleeding, it should be avoided if spotting is a problem. “Aspirin is a blood thinner, which could exacerbate bleeding between menstrual periods,” says Autry.
Getting regular pap smears. This screening for cervical cancer can catch the disease before you get symptomatic bleeding. Cervical cancer is 100 percent curable if caught in its early stages. An annual pap smear can detect any abnormal cells in the cancer’s earliest, treatable stage.
Maintaining a healthy weight. This will reduce your risk of uterine cancer. “Obesity is a strong risk factor for uterine cancer,” says Autry. Because uterine cancer is most common in post-menopausal women, contact your doctor immediately if you have any spotting after menopause.
Switching birth control methods. Spotting is common in women who use intrauterine devices (IUDs). Usually, the bleeding isn’t harmful, but switching to a different form of birth control can end the inconvenience of spotting.
Managing stress. Emotional stress can cause a woman’s menstrual cycle to get out of whack, which can cause spotting or irregular periods. Try to keep stress under control with relaxation techniques such as yoga, meditation, deep breathing, visualization, and aerobic exercise.
Keeping a menstrual diary. Log the dates of your menstrual periods and any spotting before your period or during your cycle. Write down how long the bleeding lasted and how heavy it was. Note whether the bleeding was mixed with vaginal discharge. This diary can help your doctor determine whether between-period bleeding is a concern.
One final point: It’s important to know your body and your unique patterns. Spotting might be your normal. However, if you notice any significant changes in your monthly routine, whatever it is, a call to your doctor is warranted.
