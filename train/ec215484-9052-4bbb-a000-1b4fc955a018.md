The theater lights dimmed as the audience settled, their faces half-lit by the glow of the stage. A single actor, arms crossed, stood rigidly before a scripted monologue, their voice a flat echo of practiced lines. The camera zoomed in on their hands—fingers flexing slightly, as if adjusting a prop they hadn’t touched in months. The other actors moved in perfect, unnatural synchrony, their gestures stiff, their smiles too wide, their laughter too loud. A child’s laughter, once spontaneous, now a broken record played in a loop.

The set design was a labyrinth of mismatched furniture, half-hidden behind crumbling drapes. A single framed photograph of a family—all smiling—dangled from a wall, its edges worn. The director’s voice, low and commanding, cut through the silence: *"Focus. This is the moment."* The actor’s breath hitched, but their eyes remained locked on the script, not the audience. The scene ended not with a sigh but a *click*, like a door slamming shut behind a ghost. The audience erupted—not in applause, but in gasps, as if they’d just witnessed something real for the first time.

The moment lingered. The camera pulled back, revealing the stage empty except for a single, discarded glove. The audience stood, their faces blank, as if they’d just stepped into a dream they couldn’t wake from. The lights flickered. The script waited.
