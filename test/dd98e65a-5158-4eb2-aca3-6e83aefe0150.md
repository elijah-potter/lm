The first time Mia saw the floating lanterns, she thought she was dreaming. They drifted just above the school’s old oak tree, their soft glow painting the leaves in shades of gold and crimson. The others had called it a prank, but tonight, the lanterns pulsed with an eerie rhythm—like the hum of a distant heartbeat.

Her math teacher, Mr. Delgado, was already late to class. He always said his lectures were "too fast for most of you," but today, his voice cut through the wind like a whip. "We’re starting with quadratic equations. But first—*why* do we learn this?" he asked, his hands gesturing wildly as if he could explain the sky itself.

Mia’s stomach twisted. She’d spent last week researching for a science project on "the science of dreams," but now, with the lanterns watching from above, she wondered if she’d misplaced her focus.

At recess, she found Elias laughing beside the empty playground, his backpack slung over one shoulder. "You’re staring at the tree like it’s got eyes," he said, grinning.

"You think?" She pointed. "They’re moving."

Elias frowned. "Or they’re just the wind."

The wind didn’t move lanterns.

---

That night, Mia dreamed of equations. Not the boring kind—the kind with answers that shifted when she tried to solve them. The numbers rearranged themselves, turning into faces: her best friend’s smirk, her little brother’s wide eyes, even Mr. Delgado’s stern mouth twisting into something sad.

When she woke, her bed sheets were damp with condensation. A single lantern had landed on her pillow, its string frayed, the paper inside crumbling into dust. The words on the paper read:

**"The equations are real. The lanterns are listening."**

She gasped, pressing her fingers to her temples. Her head pounded—not from a hangover, but something deeper. Something *alive*.

---

The next morning, the school’s clock tower stopped ticking. Not all at once, but one second at a time, like a clock winding down before stopping. The principal’s office door was slightly ajar. Inside, the desks were arranged in a perfect circle, the blackboard covered in equations that changed shape every time someone looked at them.

Mr. Delgado was there, his tie undone, his hands shaking. "They’ve been waiting for someone like you," he said, voice raw. "Someone who can solve the equations right."

Mia’s hands trembled. "What do you mean?"

He pointed to the blackboard. A single equation stood out: *x² + y² = z²*. But it wasn’t just math. It was a *person*. A girl in a tattered dress, her arms outstretched, her face frozen in a scream.

The lanterns above the tree pulsed again. This time, they weren’t just glowing—they were *singing*, a sound like a thousand voices whispering at once.

Elias found her in the library, staring at a book titled *The Algebra of Ghosts*. "They’re not ghosts," he said, holding up a folded note. "They’re *solutions*."

The note read:

**"The lanterns are asking. Solve for them."**

---

The equations became harder. Faster. Mia’s mind raced, but the answers kept slipping away like sand through her fingers. Then came the nightmares: her teacher’s face, his voice, the weight of something pressing down on her chest.

One evening, she found Elias in the school’s empty gym, tracing equations on the floor with his fingers. "We have to find the *root*," he whispered. "The one that’s been missing."

The root wasn’t a number. It was a name.

---

The final equation appeared on the blackboard that afternoon: *∞ = x + y + z + w*. Mia’s breath hitched. She knew the symbols. She just didn’t know what they meant.

The lanterns above the tree formed a circle around her. Their light turned the air inside her skull warm, like a fever. She could feel it—they were waiting.

She closed her eyes.

---

The moment she opened them again, the equations were gone. The blackboard was blank. The lanterns were gone.

But when she looked down, her hands were covered in ink. A single, perfect equation had written itself across her palm:

**√(Love) + √(Hope) + √(Truth) = ∞**

She gasped. She’d never written that before.

Elias was standing beside her, his expression unreadable. "You did it," he said quietly.

Mr. Delgado appeared behind them, his eyes wet. "She’s the key," he said. "The equations were waiting for someone who could *feel* them."

Mia’s chest ached. She wanted to run. She wanted to scream. Instead, she smiled.

The lanterns were coming back.

And this time, she was ready.
