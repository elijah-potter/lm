## TIL The “Smell of Rain” is Actually Several Distinct Scents

Today I learned that the familiar, comforting aroma most people associate with rain – often called "petrichor" – isn't a single scent at all. It's a complex combination of several distinct smells, each originating from different processes.

The term "petrichor" itself was coined in 1964 by two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and R.G. Thomas, who were researching the phenomenon. They observed that a distinctive odor arose from the ground after rainfall, particularly after a dry period.

The primary component of this smell comes from plant oils. During dry spells, plants release oils that accumulate on rocks and soil. When rain falls, these oils are released into the air, creating a fragrant, almost citrusy scent. A second, more earthy odor originates from a bacterium called *Geosmin*. This microorganism lives in the soil and produces Geosmin, which humans are incredibly sensitive to – we can detect it in concentrations as low as 5 parts per trillion. Finally, a third contributor is ozone, which is often present in the air after thunderstorms and creates a sharp, clean smell.

The combination of these three elements – plant oils, Geosmin, and ozone – creates what we collectively perceive as the “smell of rain.” It’s fascinating how a seemingly simple sensory experience is actually a complex interplay of biological and atmospheric processes.
