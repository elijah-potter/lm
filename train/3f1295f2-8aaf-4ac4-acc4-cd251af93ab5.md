The first time I noticed the weight in my pocket was an accident—my thumb brushing against it in the dim glow of a subway car. It was cold, hard as marble, and when I opened my palm, the shape of a coin was unmistakable, though the edges were worn smooth by years of handling. I’d never thought of myself as a collector, but then again, I hadn’t given much thought to anything until that moment.

The coin was English, stamped with a crown and the year 1662. The date was impossible—I was born two years later, but my father had told stories of how he’d found it beneath a floorboard in their old house, how it had been passed down through generations before being hidden again. That night, I pressed it to my chest and wondered if it was cursed. Maybe it was just a reminder that some things weren’t meant to be understood, only carried.

The next morning, I woke to the sound of knocking at my door. It was an old woman, her hands trembling with age, her breath shallow. She held out a small, worn box. “Your father left this for you,” she whispered. Inside were more coins, some familiar, some not, all of them older than I’d ever imagined. She said nothing more, only that I should take them. I did, though I didn’t know why.

The years passed, and I learned to recognize the patterns—the way the weight shifted when I held them, the way the air seemed to thicken around them. I kept them in my desk drawer, where they sat untouched until one evening, I reached for one of the newer ones, just to see what it felt like. The metal was cold, but not in a way that was unnatural. It was like the coin itself was trying to pull me under, to remind me that I was not the first to hold it, and that I would not be the last.

Now, as I write this, I hold the box in my lap, the coins shifting slightly beneath my fingers. I should have stopped long ago. I should have returned them, buried them, forgotten them. But I can’t. Not yet. The past is not mine to own, but it is mine to keep, and I do not know how to let go.

The admission is this: I have spent my life searching for answers in things I should have left behind. And now, I am learning that some truths are not meant to be understood—they are meant to be carried.
