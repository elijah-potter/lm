The crowd moved in a blur of whispers and shadows, but I watched the way their eyes lingered on the cracked pavement—how they missed the faint blue of the streetlamp’s reflection in the old tin can, or the way the wind caught the scent of damp earth before anyone else realized it was autumn. They hurried past the half-eaten sandwiches abandoned at the park bench, their stomachs rumbling unnoticed, while the bread crusts curled in the morning air, crisp and forgotten.

I stood at the edge of the harbor, where the tide lapped at the docks, and saw them for the first time—the fishermen’s worn hands gripping the ropes, the way their fingers traced the rhythm of the waves like a silent prayer. They were not watching the sky, nor the boats, but the way the water parted at the shore, revealing something deeper beneath. No one else noticed the way their breath fogged in the cold, or how the salt clung to their lips.

I learned that beauty is not always in what you see, but in what you *miss*—the quiet hum of a forgotten song, the weight of a half-remembered promise, the way the light bends around a child’s laughter, unguarded and wild. They missed it because it was not meant to be found, only felt.
