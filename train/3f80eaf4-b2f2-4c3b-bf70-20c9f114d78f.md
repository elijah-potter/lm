## TIL the "Smell of Rain" is Actually Multiple Chemical Compounds

Today I learned that what many people describe as the pleasant, earthy aroma associated with rain – often referred to as “petrichor” – isn't a single scent, but a complex combination of several distinct chemical compounds.

The term "petrichor" itself was coined in 1964 by Australian scientists Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas Guthrie. They were researching the unique scent following rainfall in Australia and sought a word to describe it. The name derives from the Greek words "petra" (stone) and "ichor" (the fluid that flows in the veins of the gods in Greek mythology).

The primary contributors to this aroma are geosmin and plant oils. Geosmin is a metabolic byproduct produced by certain bacteria, particularly *Streptomyces*, which thrive in soil. When rain falls, it splashes onto the ground, releasing these molecules into the air.  Furthermore, plants release volatile oils that accumulate on dry surfaces. The impact of raindrops dislodges these oils, creating a second layer of scent that combines with the geosmin. The specific composition of the scent varies depending on the type of vegetation and soil present, making each rainfall experience subtly unique.



It’s fascinating to realize that what we perceive as a singular, evocative smell is actually a delicate blend of microbial activity and plant chemistry, all triggered by the simple act of rain.
