## TIL That the "Smell of Rain" is Actually Multiple Chemical Compounds

Today I learned that what many people describe as the pleasant, earthy scent associated with rain – often called "petrichor" – isn’t a single smell, but a complex blend of several distinct chemical compounds. 

The term "petrichor" itself was coined in 1964 by Australian scientists Isabel Joy Bear and R.G. Thomas, who were studying the phenomenon. It’s derived from the Greek words "petra" (stone) and "ichor" (the fluid that flows in the veins of the gods in Greek mythology).

The primary contributor to this scent is a compound called geosmin, produced by bacteria, particularly *Streptomyces*, which live in the soil. These bacteria release geosmin when the ground is dry. When rain falls, the impact dislodges these compounds, which are then carried into the air. Furthermore, a second compound, produced by the crushing of plant oils on rocks and other surfaces, contributes a distinct, slightly different aroma. This second component is often described as a more citrusy or green scent.

What makes this particularly interesting is that humans are remarkably sensitive to geosmin, able to detect it at concentrations as low as 5 parts per trillion – far lower than most other scents. This explains why the "smell of rain" is so evocative and universally recognized, even if the underlying chemistry is quite complex.
