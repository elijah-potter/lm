The Bergeron process, named after Swedish meteorologist Tor Bergeron, is a key method of rain formation in colder climates. This process primarily involves the growth and eventual fall of precipitation due to differences in saturation vapor pressure over supercooled water and ice. In a cloud where the temperature is below freezing, both supercooled water droplets and ice crystals can exist together. However, the saturation vapor pressure over ice is lower than that over supercooled water, meaning that water molecules in the air more readily condense or deposit onto the ice crystals. This results in the growth of ice crystals at the expense of supercooled water droplets, a process known as the Wegener–Bergeron–Findeisen process. As ice crystals grow larger, they become heavy enough to fall through the cloud. As these ice crystals descend, they may pass through warmer air and melt into raindrops. If the air below the cloud is not warm enough to melt the ice crystals, they may reach the ground as snow, sleet, or freezing rain.
