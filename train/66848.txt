Edible Forest Gardens
Guest Article for the Tallahassee Democrat
August 18, 2017, Release for the Tallahassee Democrat
Imagine your backyard as a lush habitat of flowering and fruiting trees, shrubs, groundcovers, and vines made vibrant with the sights and sounds of buzzing insects, butterflies, hummingbirds, and songbirds. During a springtime stroll through the garden, you look up to spot luscious mulberries starting to ripen. A wild grape vine twines through the mulberry branches sporting clusters of small green grapes that will turn purple and juicy as they ripen in the summer. Clusters of edible white blooms adorn the nearby elderberry shrubs – the small, dark berries soon to follow. Patches of wildflowers including ironweed, frostweed, goldenrod, and bear’s foot attract pollinating insects. Legumes such as wild white indigo, coral bean, and clover provide a natural fertilizer by converting nitrogen from the air into a soil form that can be used by nearby plants. Seminole pumpkin, butternut squash, and sweet potato vines clamber over the ground shading out weeds with the promise of an abundant fall bounty.
This is an edible forest garden, otherwise known as a food forest. The forest garden provides food and shelter for people and wildlife. It is a group of mutually beneficial plants that are grown together in a layered pattern like a natural forest. Useful plants are found in each layer, including large trees, small trees, shrubs, herbaceous plants, groundcovers, root crops, and vines. By growing in vertical layers an abundance of food and useful plants can be grown in a relatively small area. The soil in a forest garden is never tilled. It becomes enriched and alive as fallen leaves and stems are converted into rich humus by fungus, bacteria, and millions of other little organisms, invisible to the naked eye. The water and air holding capacity of the soil is also increased as these tiny creatures break down and incorporate all the organic matter.
Forest gardening is not a new idea. This method has been practiced for thousands of years – mostly in the tropical regions of the world. What looked like wild jungle or rain forest to early explorers was, in many cases, a well-planned garden where plants that provided food, medicine, and building materials, were selected and encouraged by the native people. While this type of gardening is relatively new to temperate climates, it holds great promise as a way to sustainably produce food for people, while providing important habitat for wildlife.
The key thing to keep in mind is that this is a tree-based gardening system. Fruit or nut trees are spaced widely enough to allow some sunlight to reach the lower layers. Consideration must be given to sun and shade tolerances for the best placement of the food plants in each of the lower layers. Also, plants in the forest garden are selected to provide as many functions as possible. Garden functions include food producers, nitrogen fixers, nutrient accumulators, mulch producers, beneficial insect attractors, and wildlife habitat enhancers. For example, the elderberry shrubs in my yard produce edible flowers and fruits, they can be chopped down in the late summer and used to mulch and feed other garden trees and shrubs, they attract a host of beneficial insects, and they’re a favorite nesting shrub for the neighborhood cardinals.
Listed here are a few examples of plants for each of the forest garden layers.
- Large Trees: oak, hickory, pecan, persimmon, mulberry, pear.
- Small Trees: satsuma, kumquat, redbud, plum, rusty blackhaw.
- Shrubs: blueberry, elderberry, sumac, beautyberry.
- Herbaceous Layer: wildflowers, native grasses, herbs.
- Groundcovers: violets, partridgeberry, hog peanut, butternut squash.
- Vines: Passionflower, muscadine grapes, chayote squash, runner beans.
- Root Zone: sweet potato, canna lily, groundnut, gingers.
Once the forest garden is established, maintenance is minimal, mostly involving mulching and cutting back the more vigorous plants. Very little insect damage occurs because the rich diversity of plantings creates healthy habitats alive with insect predators that voraciously feed on the pests.
Give edible forest gardening a try if you’d like to produce more of your own food while creating a healthy habitat for wildlife. Among the many sources of information on this topic, I recommend starting with “Gaia’s Garden” by Toby Hemenway and “Edible Forest Gardens” (two volume set) by Dave Jacke.
Good luck with your new gardening adventure!
