In a dimly lit attic, beneath layers of dust-coated shelves, **Elias Voss** meticulously organized a collection of ephemeral fragments—an empty spool of thread, a half-smoked cigarette butt, a faded ticket stub from a 1978 concert, a child’s lost toy with a broken plastic leg, a receipt for a book sold at a garage sale in 1995, a single silver locket with a cracked clasp, and the last page of a love letter to someone who never returned. Each item bore a story, though Elias preferred the quiet satisfaction of the *what might have been*.

His hands, calloused from years of handling fragile things, traced the edges of a small, gold-trimmed book with a torn spine. Inside, a poem by a poet Elias had never heard of was written in ink that had faded to a ghostly gray. On the back of a napkin from a diner in Portland, Oregon, in 1989, a handwritten note read: *“They say the future is written in the past, but the past is just a draft.”*

Elias adjusted his spectacles, which had been passed down from his great-uncle, a man who had once worked in a typewriter factory. The light from a single bulb cast long shadows across the cluttered space. He picked up a pocket watch with a broken chain, its face etched with the words *“Time is the only currency that can’t be spent on what you don’t have.”*

His fingers brushed the sleeve of a moth-eaten jacket, the one he’d worn to his first job at the local newspaper in ’87. Beneath the collar, a small, silver locket glinted—a gift from his mother, who had died when he was twelve. He opened it slowly, revealing a photograph of them both, arms wrapped around each other, laughing at something she’d said. The photo was faded, the edges curled, but the warmth of the moment lingered.

Elias closed the locket gently, then reached for another item—a receipt from a 1979 sale at a flea market in Detroit. It listed a hand-carved wooden whistle for $0.98. He flipped it over to find a note: *“To a boy who believed in magic.”*

Outside, the wind rattled the attic window. Inside, Elias sorted through the detritus of forgotten lives, each piece a thread in the tapestry of time. Some days, he wondered if he should toss them all. But then he’d remember the way the light caught the gold of the locket, or the way the old book had turned its pages with a sound like whispering.

He placed the whistle on the shelf beside the locket, where it would stay until the next time he needed to remember what it felt like to believe in the world.
