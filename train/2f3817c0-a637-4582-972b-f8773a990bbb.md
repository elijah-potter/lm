The first mistake happened while the old clockmaker’s apprentice was polishing brass tools in the dim workshop light. He dropped a drop of oil on the floor, mistaking it for a spark from the forge’s embers. When he stepped forward to clean it, the floor beneath him gave way—just a crack, barely visible at first. But it widened under his weight, and before he could react, he was falling.

He landed in a heap on the workshop’s dusty floorboards, the oil slick beneath him now a river of golden liquid. The clockmaker’s wife stood frozen in the doorway, her face pale with fear. The apprentice scrambled to his knees, clutching his broken ankle as the clock’s great pendulum—still ticking, though the mechanism groaned in protest—slowly began to slow.

The mistake wasn’t the drop of oil. It was the way the floor had betrayed him, the way the clock’s hands now crept backward like fingers tracing the edges of his failure. The apprenticeship was lost, the family’s savings tied up in the broken clock, and the wife’s silence spoke louder than any apology. But the worst part? The clockmaker had already begun to mend the mistake, stitching together the broken gears with silver thread, his hands steady despite the tremors in his voice.

The apprentice stared at the oil on his hands, now stained with something darker—regret. The clock’s chime echoed: *Five strikes. Five more to go.*
