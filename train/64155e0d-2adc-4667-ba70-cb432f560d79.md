I remember the way the rain pattered against the window in our old apartment, the sound so soft it felt like a secret shared only between the city and the street below. The way my mother’s voice would rise when she told stories about the sea, her hands gripping the salt-licked air like it was something precious. How we’d sit on the balcony at dusk, the air thick with the scent of jasmine and the distant hum of the harbor. I remember the first time I held her hand in mine, her fingers warm and sure, like the tide pulling us closer to shore.

She taught me to fish in the harbor, how the fish would dart between the rocks before disappearing into the dark. We’d catch them with our bare hands, pressing them against the cool surface of the water, watching them fight before surrendering. She’d say, *"Some things are meant to be caught, not kept."* I remember the way the water dripped from her fingers, how her laughter sounded like the splash of waves against the dock.

Years later, I’d sit by the same harbor, watching the same boats pass by, but my hands were different now. I’d trace the lines of the fish we’d caught, studying the way their scales caught the light. I remember the first time I realized the ocean didn’t just give us food—it gave us stories. How the same currents that carried my mother’s voice across the water also carried the whispers of those who came before us.

One evening, I stood at the edge of the dock, the same dock she’d stood at as a child, her eyes wide with wonder. The wind carried the scent of fish and salt, and for a moment, I thought I heard her laugh again. I remember feeling something pull me forward, like the tide itself was urging me to remember. I think of how much the sea has changed since then—the boats are different, the fishermen older, the docks weathered by decades of storms. But the way the light still catches the water, the way the fish still dart between the rocks—some things never change.

I think of the stories we tell ourselves to make sense of the world, how we stitch together fragments of memory like broken glass, trying to find the edges that hold. I think of how the ocean doesn’t care about the past or the future, only the present, the endless present of waves and salt and time. I think of the way I still remember the way the rain pattered against the window, the way my mother’s voice rose, and the way the sea never lets go. I think it all comes down to this: the way we hold on, and the way we let go.
