The last day of summer was supposed to be a celebration, but for twelve-year-old Mateo, it felt more like a countdown. His parents had packed his suitcase, his favorite sneakers tucked into the bottom, and his little brother, Leo, had already started crying in the car. "We're moving to Oregon!" his mom had said, and Mateo had just stared at her like she’d lost her mind.

Oregon. It wasn’t a place Mateo knew. Not really. The last time he’d been to a new town was when his dad had transferred his job to Colorado three years ago. He’d adapted—learned to like the snow, the long winters, the way the sun seemed to hang lower in the sky. But Oregon? That was different. It was big. Too big. And he wasn’t sure he could handle it.

Their apartment was already packed when they arrived, a small, boxy house on the edge of town with a yard that looked like it had never been mowed. The neighbors were strange—someone with a dog that barked at the mailman, another who always wore flannel shirts even in August. Mateo spent his first night staring at the ceiling, wondering if he’d made a mistake.

Leo was already inside, coloring on the kitchen table, his little hands shaking. "Why did we have to leave?" he asked, not looking up.

Mateo sat beside him, rubbing his thumb along the edge of his brother’s crayon. "I don’t know," he said. "Maybe it’s better here."

But it wasn’t. The first day of school was worse. His new classmates didn’t know him. They didn’t know his name. They didn’t know that he’d spent the summer practicing his guitar, or that he could solve a math problem in his head faster than anyone else in his old school. They just stared at him like he was a ghost.

Then there was the girl.

Her name was Ava. She was sitting by herself at lunch, her backpack slung over one shoulder, her dark hair pulled into a messy bun. She had a sketchbook in her lap, and every time Mateo looked at it, he saw lines and shapes that felt like they could come to life. He waited until the teacher cleared her throat, then slid into the empty seat next to her.

"You can draw too," he said, gesturing to her book.

Ava blinked. "I—I don’t know if I can."

Mateo grinned. "You can. Just try."

She hesitated, then opened her book. Inside was a messy, jagged drawing of a tree. "I don’t know how to make it look real," she muttered.

Mateo didn’t know how to make trees look real either, but he remembered something his art teacher had said: "Art isn’t about perfection. It’s about feeling." He turned to her. "What’s in your book?"

Ava hesitated. "Just stuff. Stuff I don’t know what to do with."

Mateo’s stomach twisted. He’d felt that way too—like he wasn’t good enough, like he didn’t fit in. But then he remembered the way his dad had looked at him after he’d played guitar in the living room one night, how his eyes had lit up like he’d seen something magical. Maybe he could help Ava see that too.

They spent the rest of the week in each other’s pockets. Mateo showed her his favorite spots around town—where the old oak tree grew near the river, where the cliffs overlooked the ocean. Ava showed him how to mix colors, how to turn a simple line into something that felt alive. She even let him help her sketch her new friend.

One afternoon, as they sat on the steps of the library, Ava pulled out a blank sheet of paper. "What if we do something together?" she asked.

Mateo’s heart raced. "Like what?"

She smiled. "Like a story."

He laughed. "A story?"

She nodded. "About us. About what we both feel."

Mateo had never done anything like that before. But then again, he’d never been in a town where he didn’t know anyone either. Maybe this was his chance to find something new. To find his place.

They started with a simple idea: a boy and a girl who didn’t know what to do with themselves, but together, they learned to draw it out. The story was messy at first—full of half-formed ideas, of words that didn’t quite fit. But as the days passed, it began to take shape. It was about two kids who didn’t know how to fit in, but who discovered that sometimes, the best stories come from the places you least expect them to.

And maybe, just maybe, that was the lesson Mateo had been looking for all along.

The summer was over. The school year was just beginning. But for the first time, Mateo didn’t feel like he was moving somewhere new. He felt like he was starting something. Something that felt like home.
