The library was silent except for the hum of fluorescent lights and the occasional whisper of a forgotten novel. Across the aisle, an old man sat with a half-eaten sandwich, his fingers tracing the spine of a worn paperback. The title, *The Weight of Invisible Things*, was faded but legible—his favorite.

He had read it years ago, but now, the words felt different. Not just the weight of the stories within, but the weight of what he hadn’t said. The girl in the story, Liora, had been forced to choose between two men, both of them fathers of her own age. The one who loved her still. The one who had taken her voice.

Outside, the rain pattered against the window. He had spent thirty years trying to forget that day—when his wife had left, when his daughter had been taken from him, when the judge had signed the papers sealing the courtroom. He had built walls, but they were hollow. The walls were just paper, the letters just ink on parchment.

He exhaled, the breath curling like smoke in the draft. The admission came unbidden, a ghost of his own confession: *I never told her I loved her. Not when she was young. Not when she was old. And now, I am too old to say it again.* The paper cracked between his fingers, the ink bleeding into the margins. The story was over. The truth was written in the margins.
