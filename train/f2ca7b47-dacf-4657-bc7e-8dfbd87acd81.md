The wind howled through the hollows like a voice I couldn’t quite hear, and the old oak’s roots twisted in the earth, questioning too, as if they, too, had forgotten what to believe. I stood beneath it, my fingers brushing the rough bark where the light bent and fractured, and for a moment, I thought I saw the tree’s leaves—shaken, trembling—wanting something I couldn’t name.

We were here for the harvest, but the sheaves were thin. My father had told me the years before were different, when the earth gave more than it took, when the harvest baskets filled like the rivers in spring. Now, the fields were as sparse as the answers I found when I pressed my forehead to the window.

*Should we go back?* The question lingered in the air, sticky and unspoken. The village elders had whispered of droughts, of men who had gone mad with thirst, their skin splitting like cracked earth. But the priest said it was God’s will. I wanted to believe, but the way my hands trembled when I held a knife—when the blood on my palms felt less like offering and more like regret—proved I was still me.

The child’s laughter drifted from the cottage, light and careless, and for a second, I envied them their certainty. Then the wind sighed through the trees again, and I remembered the last time I’d been this unsure: when I’d told the elders I wouldn’t go to the war. They’d called me weak. I’d been weak, but I’d been afraid, too. And now, here I was, choosing between the harvest’s promise and the harvest’s cost.

The knife in my hand was warm. Maybe that was the truth: fear wasn’t weakness. It was the first step toward choosing something, anything, that wasn’t the quiet, aching doubt I carried like a shadow.
