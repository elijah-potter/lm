The first snowfall of winter crested over the valley like a veil, dusting the quiet town with silence. A child’s laughter, sharp and bright, echoed off the old oak tree where I sat, clutching my grandmother’s weathered hand. She had told me stories of her youth—how the river used to run faster, how the scent of wild roses filled the air in summer, how her father’s voice sounded like thunder. Now, as the snowflakes melted into her skin, I realized she was slipping away, just like the way time had erased the last time we were here together. The lantern flickered, casting long shadows across the floor, while the fire in the hearth—its embers still warm—burned low, as if it too knew the moment was fading. Outside, the wind howled, and the snow began to fall in patterns no one could predict. I reached for my phone, but hesitated—what was there to remember in pixels? The taste of her tea, the warmth of her breath on my neck, the way she’d hum a tune from her youth just to make me smile. The moment shattered like glass when she whispered, *"You’re too young to lose people like this."* I closed my eyes, and the memory stayed—fragile, perfect, and forever beyond my grasp. The snow kept falling, but it wasn’t the cold that made me shiver—it was the weight of what was lost.
