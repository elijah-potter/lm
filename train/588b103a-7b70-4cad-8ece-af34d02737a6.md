The storm raged outside, thick black clouds swallowing the sun in jagged, violent bursts. Rain lashed against the window in relentless, thunderous sheets, each drop a hammer strike. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of damp earth and damp fear. The wind howled through the broken branches of the overgrown garden, their leaves curling and snapping like bones. A single, defiant red rose pushed through the mud, its petals drenched but stubbornly holding on.

Within the cramped, cluttered study, the walls were papered with faded posters of stormy skies and stormy faces. The only light came from the flickering bulb above the desk, casting long, skeletal shadows that stretched like limbs across the floor. The old man at the desk—tall, gaunt, with hands that trembled as they gripped the edges of the desk—poured whiskey into a glass, the liquid catching the light like embers in a dying fire. His reflection in the glass was a distorted, storm-wracked version of himself: eyes hollow, lips pressed into a thin, unnatural line.

"Another storm," he muttered, voice raw. The whiskey burned, but it was the taste of the past, of what he couldn’t change. The radio crackled to life, static hissing, then a distorted voice: *"Attention, residents... warnin’ of possible flooding..."* The old man didn’t look up. His fingers traced the rim of the glass, then the scar running down his cheek, a jagged line like the edge of a storm front.

Outside, the wind screamed. The rose on the windowsill wilted, its red petals turning to blackened stubs. The storm was coming in waves—first the rain, then the wind, then the howling silence that followed. The old man took another sip, the whiskey warm and bitter. The glass was empty now, but the storm wasn’t over yet.
