The rain fell in sheets, turning the cobblestones into rivers of glass that caught the neon reflections of the city’s endless night. I sat in the back of the taxi, staring at the flickering dashboard lights, my fingers tracing the creases in the worn leather seat. Outside, the hum of traffic drowned out the distant wail of sirens. I had planned to meet my friend at that bar, to drink until the world blurred into something forgettable, but something had changed. The weight of my decision settled like a stone in my chest before I even thought to question it.

The first time I considered turning back, it was just a flicker—a doubt that whispered, *Maybe this isn’t what you want.* But I laughed it off, shaking my head like a fool. I had spent years chasing this moment, this recklessness, this *living*. What was one more drink? One more mistake? The bar was just a few blocks away, and the taxi was moving, and I had already made the decision to walk inside, to order a whiskey neat, to let the fire of the night consume me. But then, as the driver pulled into the parking lot, I hesitated. The door opened. I stood up.

I turned back.

The moment my foot hit the pavement, the world shifted. The taxi vanished in a blur of motion. The bar was still there, the neon sign glowing like a dying star, but I wasn’t going in. Instead, I walked toward the alley where the old man sat on a crate, his breath steady, his hands wrapped around a half-empty flask. He didn’t look up as I approached, but I knew him. Knew the way his fingers trembled when he drank. Knew the stories he’d never tell.

I sat beside him.

The flask was empty. I pulled out my phone, my thumb hovering over the screen. I should have called my friend, should have gotten back in the taxi, should have—

But I didn’t.

I lit a cigarette from the pack in my pocket, the first one I’d smoked in months. The smoke curled between us, thick and slow. The old man exhaled, his breath a ghost in the cold air. I took a drag, the nicotine burning my throat. Outside, the city pulsed with life, but here, in this dim corner of the alley, it felt like time had stopped.

“You look like you’re thinking,” he said.

I exhaled. “I was.”

He tilted his head. “About what?”

I didn’t answer. Instead, I watched the flicker of the neon sign, the way the rain pattered against the pavement. The world had changed. Not in a grand gesture, but in the quiet, unspoken shifts that happen when you decide to stay.

Outside, the sirens grew louder. The taxi’s headlights cut through the darkness, searching for an empty space. I stood, the cigarette still burning in my fingers. The old man didn’t move.

“You’re still here,” I said.

He didn’t answer. But I knew he was.

I walked away. The city swallowed me whole, but I carried the weight of my choice with me, like a second skin. Somewhere, my friend was still waiting for me at that bar, but I didn’t go. The night was mine now. And for the first time in a long time, I was glad to be wrong.
