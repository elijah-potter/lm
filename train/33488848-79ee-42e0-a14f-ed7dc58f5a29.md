The first time I experienced routine was as a 12-year-old in my bedroom, sitting with a half-eaten bowl of cereal at 7:30 AM. The alarm had gone off five minutes earlier, but sleep had been shallow, my mind racing through the night’s half-finished dreams. The radio played a static-filled news broadcast, and the clock on the wall ticked relentlessly.

I pulled on my socks, matching the color of my shoes, and walked downstairs. The kitchen smelled of coffee, the smell of routine I’d grown accustomed to. The door opened, and the morning sunlight spilled in, painting the hallway in gold. Outside, the city slept—no sounds, no movement, just the faint hum of a distant train.

By noon, I was back in my room, staring at a blank page on my laptop. The same book I’d started yesterday was open on my desk. The same playlist was playing, the same songs looping endlessly. The routine had settled into my bones, a rhythm that felt both familiar and foreign.

I’d forgotten what it was like to feel truly awake. The world outside my window was a blur of commuters and skyscrapers, but inside, I existed in a loop of repetition—wake, eat, work, repeat. The first time I realized how much time had passed, I was already halfway through another day. That was the start.
