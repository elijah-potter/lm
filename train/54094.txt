Plants that self-pollinate don't need animals or insects to produce seeds. Fertilization takes place within one flower. This is advantageous in areas where there is a shortage of pollinating creatures or when the climate dictates only a limited amount of plants can grow there. However, if these plants become susceptible to a disease, the whole population may suffer. With the genetic diversity created by cross-pollination, if a disease strikes one plant, some plants of that species are likely to survive because they are not genetically identical. Many plants have developed ways to promote cross-pollination over self-pollination.
Dioecious plants are those in which male and female flowers are present on separate plants. A male and female plant must be present for pollination to occur. For these plants, it is physically impossible to self-pollinate, so genetic diversity is guaranteed. Some edible fruits, such as mulberries (Morus spp.), hardy in U.S. Department of Agriculture hardiness zones 4 through 9); kiwi (Actinidia spp.), USDA zones 4 through 8; and persimmons (Diospyros spp.), USDA zones 6 through 10), are dioecious. Evergreens can be dioecious, like hollies (Ilex spp.), USDA zones 4 through 11; and junipers (Juniperus spp.), USDA zones 2 through 9. Several shade trees are dioecious, such as the maples (Acer spp.), USDA zones 2 through 9; and honeylocusts (Gleditsia spp.), USDA zones 3 through 8. USDA growing zones are approximate due to wide variability within each genus.
Monoecious plants bear separate female and male flowers on the same plant. Often, these flowers will mature at different times to prevent self-pollination. Garden vegetables such as corn (Zea spp.), USDA zones 7 through 11; and squash (Cucurbita spp.), USDA zones 9 through 10, are monoecious, as well as some evergreens, such as pines (Pinus spp.), USDA zones 3 through 8; and cedars (Cedrus spp.), USDA zones 6 through 10. Several nut-bearing trees, such as chestnuts (Castanea spp.), USDA zones 5 through 7; and hazelnuts (Corylus spp.), USDA zones 5 through 10, are monoecious as well.
With dichogamous plants, the pistils and stamens (reproductive parts) are in the same flower but mature at different times. Almost all plants in the daisy family (Compositae) and the carrot family (Umbelliferae) are dichogamous, and include plants such as sunflowers (Helianthis spp.), USDA zones 3 through 9; asters (Aster spp.), USDA zones 4 through 8; parsley (Petroselinum spp.), USDA zones 4 through 9; and celery (Apium spp.), USDA zones 10 through 11. Pecan trees (Carya spp.), USDA zones 5 through 9, are also dichogamous.
Even when a flower carries the parts necessary for reproduction within the same flower, and both mature at the same time, the pollen and stigma (receptive tip of the pollen tube) may recognize each other as being genetically related and fertilization is blocked. A number of fruit trees are self-incompatible, although there are isolated varieties that will successfully self-pollinate. Apples (Malus spp.), USDA zones 4 through 9; pears (Pyrus spp.), USDA zones 4 through 9; sweet cherries and plums (both Prunus spp.), USDA zones 6 through 10, are generally self-incompatible.
