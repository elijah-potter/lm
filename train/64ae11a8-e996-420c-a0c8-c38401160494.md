The server’s lights flickered as the old data center door creaked open, revealing a lone technician hunched over a console. A flick of a toggle switch near the firewall’s edge—no alarms blared, no red lights flashed—just a quiet hum of the system’s heartbeat adjusting. The firewall, usually rigidly enforcing the DMZ’s isolation, now pulsed faintly with a single, unencrypted port open for a debug session. The firewall’s logs showed a single, authorized entry timestamp, but the data flow was encrypted in transit, just as it should be.

Across the room, a security badge’s RFID tag glitched—no alert triggered, but the access control system’s internal logs revealed a near-instantaneous override by a known staff member. The door’s lock had cycled, but the timestamp on the access log matched the technician’s scheduled maintenance window. The logs didn’t show the override; they just showed the technician’s badge scanning.

In the break room, a coffee machine’s display showed a single, unauthorized "coffee" order—printed in black ink on the receipt, despite the machine’s locked status. The order was for a "staff only" item, but the timestamp matched the technician’s lunch break. The machine’s logs showed the order was placed by a device with the technician’s badge’s MAC address, though no physical intervention was logged.

The technician’s phone buzzed with a message from their manager: *"No changes to the network. All good."* The message’s timestamp matched the exact moment the technician had left the server room. The logs showed no unauthorized access, no changes, just a routine update logged under their name. The network’s intrusion detection system (IDS) had flagged nothing suspicious—just a single, "expected" anomaly.
