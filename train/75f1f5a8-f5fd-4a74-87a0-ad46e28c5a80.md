The contract was signed in blood-red ink on a night the moon hung low and thin, its light barely piercing the thick canopy of the old oak grove. His name was Elias Veyne, a man who had spent his life chasing dreams so vast they made his breath come in ragged gasps. The promise was one of those—*I’ll find you a place where no one else dares to tread, where the weight of the world feels lighter than air, and the stars above don’t mock you with their endless, indifferent glow.*

He’d told her in a voice that trembled like a leaf in a storm, the words slipping through his teeth as if they were sandpaper. *"I’ll make you a king. Not of this earth, but of something better. Something that won’t break when the ground does."* She had believed him. She had believed *anything* that night, because she had lost everything else to the hollow ache in her chest.

Now, the grove stood silent, the blood dried to rust in the hollow of his palm. The moon was gone, swallowed by the clouds, but the promise still hung there, heavy as a lead weight tied to his ankle. The path he’d carved through the forest—through the ruins of his old life, through the whispers of the dead—had been easy at first. Every step felt like a victory, every scar a badge of honor. But now, the forest was too tall, too dense, and the air smelled of damp earth and something older, something that coiled around his bones like a living thing.

He’d found the ruins of the old library, where the books were still bound in leather, their pages turning as if by their own will. The texts spoke of places where the veil between worlds was thin, where the dead did not linger but *transferred*—but the cost was always the same: a life for a life, a moment of joy for a lifetime of sorrow. He’d read them all. He’d tried to bargain with them. He’d almost given himself over to the darkness just to feel the weight of his own hands on hers one last time.

And then there was the weight of the promise. It was not just the grand gesture of a life’s work, but the quiet, suffocating burden of *keeping it*. Because promises, once given, do not stay promises. They become chains. They become the things that pull you under when the tide is low.

He stood at the edge of a cliff where the land dropped away into darkness, and for the first time in years, he let himself wonder if he’d ever been the one who was supposed to keep the promise. Or if it had been the other way around.

The stars above were silent. The wind carried no answer. And the promise? It was still there, hanging in the air like a ghost, just out of reach.
