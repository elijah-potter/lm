The complex decision tree for ethical AI alignment:
1. **Input**: Human preferences encoded as utility functions (e.g., "reduce suffering > maximize productivity").
2. **Conflict**: "Minimize harm" vs. "maximize efficiency" (e.g., letting 100 workers quit for one factory shutdown).
3. **Causal loop**: Decisions compounded over time (e.g., short-term profit → long-term job cuts → societal instability).
4. **Uncertainty**: "What if we assume the wrong utility weights?" (e.g., overestimating productivity gains).
5. **Feedback**: Algorithmic bias amplifies initial choices (e.g., hiring algorithm favoring one demographic → perpetuates inequality).
6. **Transparency**: Lack of explainability masks hidden assumptions (e.g., "why did the model choose this policy?").
7. **Human oversight**: Delegating to an AI without auditing (e.g., trusting an agent’s "optimal" choice without manual review).
8. **Scale**: Local decisions (e.g., "should I automate this task?") cascade into global systems (e.g., job displacement waves).
9. **Ethical nuance**: "Simple" metrics (e.g., GDP) obscure quality-of-life factors (e.g., mental health, environmental impact).
10. **Iterative refinement**: No "final answer"—only an ongoing process of revision based on new data and feedback.

**Example**: A city’s AI-driven traffic system prioritizes reducing congestion but fails to account for disproportionate carbon emissions from high-speed routes for wealthier residents, revealing hidden trade-offs in "efficiency" metrics. The "simple" choice isn’t in the tool; it’s in the context of who defines "good" and how.
