A quiet train ride from Chicago to Denver, where the windows were lined with golden light. The girl—call her Mara—hadn’t believed in these things before: that a change could be as small as a crack in the seat, as sudden as a gust of wind through the open window. The train had been late, the luggage compartment a mess of forgotten items, but Mara had packed only what she could carry: a worn journal, a half-empty coffee cup, and a ticket stub with a name she’d never write.

They arrived in Denver at dawn. The city was alive in a way she hadn’t imagined—streets slick with rain, neon signs blinking like fireflies. She took a cab to a motel with a view of the mountains, where the air tasted like pine and something wild. The next morning, she sat at a diner table, staring at the map of the Rockies on the wall. The trip wasn’t about the destination; it was about the turning. The first time she saw a hiker’s trail winding up a slope, she followed it. The first time she laughed at the absurdity of a squirrel playing tag with a picnic basket, she let herself stay. By the time she left, she’d learned that change wasn’t about leaving; it was about finding the door she’d been looking for all along.
