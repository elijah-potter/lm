Foot-tall spikes of delicately colored bluebells add a bright spot to the spring garden. Two of the most common bluebell species are the Spanish bluebells (Hyacinthoides hispanica) and English bluebells (H. non-scripta). Although both require moist soil and will not fare well in desert climates, Spanish bluebells perform better in warmer regions than English bluebells which prefer areas with cooler summers. Like other spring-blooming bulbs, bluebells should be planted in the fall.
Ideal Planting Time
Bluebells are among the many bulbs that bloom in the spring. As a rule, spring-flowering bulbs are planted in early to mid-fall. This allows them time to set down roots and exposes them to the three to four months of cold weather needed for the bulbs to flower. Planting in early to mid-fall also lets you get your bulbs in the ground before heavy late fall rains or chilly winter temperatures make gardening unpleasant.
Sometimes you may have bluebells that you were unable to get in the ground at the right time of year. Luckily, bluebells are remarkably tolerant of bad timing. If it is fall or winter, store the bulbs in a cold place, such as an unheated garage or the refrigerator, for three to four months. As soon as you can work the soil in late winter or early spring, plant your bluebells. The bluebells should still bloom, but not as profusely as fall-planted bulbs. If you received bluebell bulbs from a neighbor or plant sale in late winter or spring, you can either plant them then or wait until fall to plant. If you choose to wait, the bulbs will produce foliage, but may not bloom. If you plan to wait, store the bulbs in clean sawdust in a dry, cool and dark location.
Bluebells aren't tricky plants to grow and will tolerate a wide range of conditions. The best soil is loam that is rich in organic matter, but the plants will also grow well in clay soil that has been lightly amended with compost. Bluebells can grow in full sun in coastal gardens, but prefer partially shady conditions elsewhere. Scattering the bulbs across a garden bed creates a more natural look than lining them up in rows. Plant the bulbs to a depth that is four times the size of the bulb. For example, if the bulb is 1 inch in diameter, plant it 4 inches deep. After planting, cover the area with a layer of mulch and keep it moist but not wet.
Bluebells will spread over a garden bed and can become overcrowded after a few years. Divide bluebells during the fall when they are dormant to place the least amount of stress on the bulbs. Once you've dug out the bulbs, remove any that are mushy or shriveled and keep the plump, solid ones. You can then re-plant the bulbs into the same bed, transplant some bluebells to other areas of the garden or give away some to friends and neighbors.
